When to Go

The Northern Irish birding year has three peaks rather than one. Seabirds fill the cliffs from March to July, migration runs along the coasts through August to October, and the estuaries hold their biggest flocks from November to February.

Spring

March to June

Seabirds come back to the ledges in March, summer migrants arrive through April, and by May every woodland is singing from before five in the morning.

Go for Breeding seabirds, full song, and the longest days of the year in the north.

Trade off Rooms cost the most from Easter onward, and island boats need booking weeks ahead in June.

Typical daily average
  • March: 3 to 9 °C / 37 to 48 °F
  • April: 4 to 12 °C / 39 to 54 °F
  • May: 7 to 15 °C / 45 to 59 °F
  • June: 10 to 18 °C / 50 to 64 °F

Autumn

July to October

Wader passage builds from July, the coasts fill with migrants through September, and October is when a rarity can turn up almost anywhere.

Go for Big wader counts, seabirds moving past in an onshore wind, and the best chance of a bird from a long way off course.

Trade off Song has stopped, so birds are identified on plumage and call, and the seabird colonies are empty by August.

Typical daily average
  • July: 12 to 19 °C / 54 to 66 °F
  • August: 12 to 19 °C / 54 to 66 °F
  • September: 10 to 17 °C / 50 to 63 °F
  • October: 8 to 13 °C / 46 to 55 °F

Winter

November to February

Geese, swans and waders pack the estuaries, starlings gather over the reedbeds at dusk, and raptors are easy over open ground.

Go for Six figure goose and wader flocks, murmurations, and reserves you have largely to yourself.

Trade off Daylight is short, and the Rathlin ferry drops to a thin winter timetable.

Typical daily average
  • November: 5 to 10 °C / 41 to 50 °F
  • December: 3 to 8 °C / 37 to 46 °F
  • January: 2 to 7 °C / 36 to 45 °F
  • February: 2 to 7 °C / 36 to 45 °F

The north coast, Rathlin and Lough Foyle

Seabird cliffs, an island crossing and a vast lough shared by farmland and reedbed

Rathlin holds the largest seabird colony in Northern Ireland and the RSPB viewpoint looks straight into it. West along the same coast, the Lough Foyle mudflats take Brent Geese and waders in numbers that matter internationally.

  • Habitat: Basalt sea cliff and stack on Rathlin, with the sand and mudflat of Lough Foyle and its wet grassland behind.
  • When: May to July for the Rathlin seabird colonies, which are the largest in Northern Ireland. October to March for the Foyle whooper swans and light-bellied brent.
  • Getting there: The Rathlin ferry sails from Ballycastle on the A2 coast road and takes about half an hour. Lough Foyle is watched from the lanes off the A2 between Limavady and Derry.
  • Look for: Atlantic Puffin, Black-legged Kittiwake, Razorbill, Common Murre, Red-billed Chough, Whooper Swan
  • Parks and reserves: RSPB Rathlin Island (West Light) (RSPB reserve), Oxford Island National Nature Reserve, Larne Lough (Swan Island and Isle of Muck) (island reserve), RSPB Rathlin Island (RSPB reserve)
  • Stops:

Strangford Lough and Lough Neagh

Sea lough, drumlin island and the largest lake in these islands, within an hour of Belfast

Strangford is a drowned drumlin field and a marine nature reserve, and the light-bellied Brent Geese that arrive from Arctic Canada each autumn are most of that population. Lough Neagh, the largest lake in these islands, sits an hour west.

  • Habitat: A drowned drumlin field of small islands and mud in a sea lough, with the largest freshwater lake in these islands to the west.
  • When: October to March. Most of the Canadian light-bellied brent population arrives on Strangford each autumn, and Lough Neagh holds diving duck in tens of thousands.
  • Getting there: The A20 and A22 run either shore of Strangford from Belfast, under an hour. Copeland is a boat from Donaghadee arranged with the observatory, not a scheduled service.
  • Look for: Brant Goose, Common Eider, Atlantic Puffin, Black-legged Kittiwake, Razorbill, Sandwich Tern
  • Parks and reserves:
    • WWT Castle Espie (WWT reserve), A WWT reserve with a visitor centre and an entry fee, on the shore of Strangford Lough near Comber. Most of the world's Light-bellied Brent Geese winter here.
    • Ballyquintin Point National Nature Reserve, A DAERA nature reserve at the southern tip of the Ards Peninsula, on a farm track with free access. Migrant waders and passing seabirds cross the point.
    • RSPB Portmore Lough (RSPB reserve), An RSPB reserve with hides and a car park you can drive to. Its wet grassland holds lapwing and Whooper Swan through the winter.
  • Stops:
    • : A harbour town on the Narrows, where a ferry crosses to Strangford. The quay is free to walk and the tide race runs below it, with common seals and eiders fishing against the current.
    • : A small harbour village opposite Portaferry across the Narrows, on the same ferry and free to explore on foot. Eiders and terns fish the tide race below the castle.
    • Oxford Island on Lough Neagh : A Lough Neagh Discovery Centre and nature reserve with trails and hides. Turn up and scan the open water for diving ducks and Whooper Swan.
    • Copeland Bird Observatory: The only accredited bird observatory in Northern Ireland, on Lighthouse Island off Donaghadee. A boat takes you out, and there is bunk accommodation for ringing visitors.
  • Visitor centres: WWT Castle Espie Visitor Centre, RSPB Rathlin West Light Seabird Centre
  • Also covers: Ballyquintin Point National Nature Reserve, Comber, Copeland Bird Observatory, Downpatrick, Lurgan, Oxford Island on Lough Neagh, Portaferry, RSPB Portmore Lough, RSPB Rathlin West Light Seabird Centre, Strangford village, WWT Castle Espie Visitor Centre
  • Also watch for: Common seals and grey seals fish the tide race off Portaferry and Strangford through the summer, with pups born on the rocks in June and July. Harbour porpoises and occasional basking sharks pass close to Rathlin's cliffs between May and August.

Fermanagh and the Erne lakelands

Wooded lough shore, island monastic sites and limestone upland, about two hours west of Belfast

A landscape of interconnected lakes and wet woodland along the Erne, quiet, under-watched and holding breeding waders and wildfowl that have thinned elsewhere.

  • Habitat: Limestone lake and island country with wet grassland, callows and mixed woodland along the Erne system.
  • When: April to July for breeding waders and the woodland species. Winter holds whooper swan and wildfowl across the lough system.
  • Getting there: The A4 and A32 from Enniskillen reach most of the system, and Lough Erne is best seen from the water.
  • Look for: Whooper Swan, Sandwich Tern, Eurasian Curlew, Hen Harrier, Red Grouse, Peregrine Falcon
  • Parks and reserves:
    • Castle Caldwell Forest (reserve), A Forest Service nature reserve on a peninsula at the west end of Lower Lough Erne, free to walk in. Whooper Swan and White-fronted Goose sit offshore in winter.
    • Crom Estate (estate open to the public), A National Trust estate on Upper Lough Erne, with a lakeshore visitor centre, a tea room and an entry fee. Its wooded shore holds Whooper Swan and curlew in winter.
    • Killykeeghan National Nature Reserve, A National Nature Reserve on limestone grassland, part of Marlbank and owned by NIEA. A free car park serves the Cuilcagh uplands, and Hen Harrier and peregrine hunt overhead.
    • Marble Arch Caves Global Geopark (reserve)
    • Lough Melvin (reserve)
  • Stops:
    • Castle Archdale Country Park : A country park and Forest Service nature reserve on Lower Lough Erne, free to walk in. It has a car park, a jetty and the seasonal ferry to White Island.
    • White Island : An island in Lower Lough Erne, on a seasonal ferry from Castle Archdale Country Park. Its shore is good for curlew, redshank and breeding Common Tern.
    • Cuilcagh Boardwalk trailhead : The paid car park and trailhead for the Cuilcagh boardwalk. Book ahead for the walk onto the blanket bog summit, where Red Grouse and peregrine cross the open moor.
    • Garrison, Lough Melvin : The village shore of Lough Melvin on the Fermanagh and Leitrim border. Watch free from the pier and the lakeside road, where Whooper Swan and curlew are among the wintering wildfowl.
  • Visitor centres: Crom Estate Visitor Centre, Marble Arch Caves Visitor Centre
  • Also covers: Belleek, Castle Archdale Country Park, Castle Caldwell Forest, Crom Estate Visitor Centre, Cuilcagh Boardwalk trailhead, Enniskillen, Garrison, Lough Melvin, Killykeeghan National Nature Reserve, Lisnaskea, Marble Arch Caves Global Geopark, Marble Arch Caves Visitor Centre, White Island
  • Also watch for: Pine martens and red squirrels share the oak woods of the Crom Estate with an outstanding run of butterflies, among them the purple hairstreak and the silver washed fritillary on the wing from June to August. Otters work the channels between the islands of Upper Lough Erne all year, and the lough itself holds pollan, a rare glacial relict whitefish found at only a handful of Irish sites.

The Mournes and Carlingford Lough

Dune coast, sea lough and granite mountain, about an hour south of Belfast

Granite mountains running straight down to the sea, with upland breeding birds on the summit ridges and a sheltered sea lough at their foot.

  • Habitat: Granite mountain rises over heath and blanket bog. It drops to a sea lough with mudflat and shingle at its head.
  • When: April to July for upland breeders and the peregrines on the Mourne crags. Winter brings waders and wildfowl to Carlingford.
  • Getting there: The A2 coast road from Newcastle runs under the mountains to Carlingford. Silent Valley and the high Mournes are entered from Head Road.
  • Look for: Brant Goose, Bar-tailed Godwit, Common Redshank, Common Shelduck, Northern Wheatear, Whooper Swan
  • Parks and reserves:
    • Murlough National Nature Reserve, A National Trust nature reserve on an ancient dune system between Dundrum Bay and the Mourne Mountains, with a parking fee. Brent Goose and oystercatchers feed on the inner bay.
    • Tollymore Forest Park (reserve), A Forest Service park at the foot of the Mourne Mountains, with a parking fee. Peregrine cross overhead and dipper work the Shimna river.
    • Slieve Gullion Forest Park (reserve), A forest park on an extinct volcano south of Newry, free to enter, with a drive to the summit cairn. Peregrine and raven cross the Ring of Gullion.
    • Silent Valley Mountain Park (reserve), An NI Water reservoir park in the Mourne Mountains, with a parking fee. Peregrine and raven cross the granite tors, and wheatear hold the open slopes in summer.
  • Stops:
    • : The inner arm of Dundrum Bay. Watch from the coastal path and the roadside embankment north of the village, where redshank, godwit and Brent Goose feed on the mud at low tide.
    • Carlingford Lough shore at Greencastle : The shore of Carlingford Lough. Watch from the roadside and the pier at Greencastle for wintering Brent Goose, shelduck and Great Crested Grebe.
  • Visitor centres: Murlough National Nature Reserve information point, The Cottage, Silent Valley Visitor Centre
  • Also covers: Carlingford Lough shore at Greencastle, Dundrum Inner Bay, Kilkeel, Murlough National Nature Reserve information point, The Cottage, Newcastle, Newry, Silent Valley Mountain Park, Silent Valley Visitor Centre, Slieve Gullion Forest Park, Tollymore Forest Park, Warrenpoint
  • Also watch for: Grey and common seals haul out on the sandbanks of Dundrum Bay through the autumn, and marsh fritillary butterflies fly low over Murlough's dune heath from late May into June. Irish hares and a small band of feral goats keep to the open ground of the Mourne Mountains above Silent Valley all year round.

Frequently Asked Questions

When is the best time to go birding in Northern Ireland?

April to July for the Rathlin seabird colony, which is the province's headline. September and October for the light-bellied Brent arrival on Strangford. October to March for swans, geese and waders on Lough Foyle, Lough Neagh and the Erne.

Where should I go first?

Strangford Lough. It is within an hour of Belfast, it holds most of a world population of Brent Geese every autumn, and WWT Castle Espie gives you hides, a cafe and the lough in one stop.

Do I need a car?

Yes for three of the four regions. Castle Espie and the Strangford shore are reachable by bus from Belfast; Rathlin needs a car to Ballycastle and then the ferry and the island bus; Fermanagh and the Mournes need one throughout.

How do I get to Rathlin?

Ferry from Ballycastle, roughly six sailings a day in summer and fewer in winter. Book the car ferry well ahead or, better, leave the car and use the island minibus, which meets the boat and runs to the West Light.

Why is Northern Ireland a separate page from Ireland?

Because the agencies, the certification scheme and the recording committee are all different. Sites here sit under the Northern Ireland Environment Agency and the RSPB, and places to stay are certified by Green Tourism, which does not apply in the Republic in the same way. Two pages let each describe its own ground accurately.

Which birds are easier here than in Great Britain?

Light-bellied Brent Goose above all, because Strangford takes almost the whole Canadian population. Chough is present but scarce. Ireland as a whole lacks Tawny Owl, Willow Tit and Marsh Tit entirely, so the woodland list is shorter than a British visitor expects.

Is Lough Neagh worth a day?

In winter, yes. It is the largest lake in these islands and holds diving duck in numbers, with Oxford Island and Portmore Lough giving hides and made paths on the southern shore. In summer it is quiet.

How much does a trip here cost?

Less than most of this site. Distances are short, almost every reserve is free or a small entry, and the only real expense is the Rathlin ferry and a car.

Can I bird here with limited mobility?

Sites on this page differ from each other more than any rating could carry, so this is the framework the standard British guides use, and the reserve's own page and warden hold the answers.

  • Permission · some reserves open ground to disabled visitors that is closed to everyone else, and it is granted by asking.
  • Priority · parking bays, hides and the nearest paths are often held for disabled visitors. Ask what is reserved and how to claim it.
  • Parking · how close a car reaches the birds shapes the whole day. Ring ahead for the nearest hard standing to the water or the hide.
  • Paths · surfaces, gradients and gate widths vary inside a single reserve. Ask which trail is boarded, surfaced or level.
  • Perches · a hide with a low window, or a bench where the view opens, turns a viewpoint into a morning.

Every reserve above with its own link carries its access detail there, and a warden answers what a page cannot.
