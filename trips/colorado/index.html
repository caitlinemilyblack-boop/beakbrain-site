Birding in Colorado

Colorado puts tundra, sagebrush, shortgrass prairie and canyon country within one day's drive, and holds grouse leks that people plan a whole spring around.

Birds that decide the trip

The Gunnison Grouse at the Waunita lek

The Gunnison Grouse was separated from the Sage Grouse in 2000 on size, plumage and display, and the Gunnison Basin holds most of the world population. Waunita is the lek set aside for watching, which keeps pressure off the rest of the basin.

When April, from the road before first light.

Where Gunnison, Curecanti National Recreation Area

White-tailed Ptarmigan on Guanella Pass

Guanella Pass reaches 3,570 metres on a paved road ninety minutes from Denver, and the willow above the car park holds ptarmigan all year. They are white in winter and mottled grey in summer, and both plumages sit still until you are very close.

When July and August for the easiest walking, though the birds are resident.

Where Guanella Pass, Rocky Mountain National Park

Three rosy finches at one feeder

Brown-capped Rosy Finch breeds almost entirely in Colorado, and in winter it joins Black and Grey-crowned Rosy Finch at feeders in the valley towns below the passes. Very few places in the world offer all three together.

When November to March, at the feeders once snow closes the tops.

Where Georgetown, Arapaho National Wildlife Refuge

Sandhill Cranes in the San Luis Valley

The Rocky Mountain population of Sandhill Crane stops in the San Luis Valley twice a year on the way between its breeding grounds and Bosque del Apache. The birds feed in barley stubble with the Sangre de Cristo range behind them.

When March for the spring peak, and again in October.

Where Monte Vista National Wildlife Refuge, Alamosa National Wildlife Refuge

Mountain Plover on the Pawnee

Mountain Plover wants bare dirt rather than grass, which is why it is missed by people looking in the wrong habitat. Prairie dog towns and heavily grazed patches on the Pawnee are where it nests, and the birds are watched from the county roads.

When April through June, while the birds are nesting.

Where Pawnee National Grassland, Crow Valley

Lesser Prairie Chickens at Campo

The species survives in Colorado only in the southeastern corner, in Baca and Prowers Counties, after a century of decline. The leks near Campo are watched from a blind booked through the grassland office at Springfield.

When Late March and April, from the blind before dawn.

Where Comanche National Grassland, Campo

American Black Swift behind a Colorado waterfall

Six breeding colonies sit inside Rocky Mountain National Park, on wet cliffs behind falling water. The birds leave at first light and return at dusk, so the watching is done at the ends of the day.

When June to August, at dawn and dusk at the falls.

Where Rocky Mountain National Park, Estes Park

Sage Grouse at Walden

Around two hundred Sage Grouse breed on the Arapaho refuge in North Park. The lek tours run for two weekends in spring, led by the North Park Chamber of Commerce with the state wildlife department, and they fill quickly.

When Late April and early May, at weekends.

Where Arapaho National Wildlife Refuge, Walden

When to Go

Colorado runs from 1,000 metres on the eastern plains to over 4,300 in the mountains, and the birding year runs at two speeds because of it. The plains and the San Luis Valley work from autumn through spring, while the tundra is under snow until June and the passes stay open only into October.

Trade off The tundra is still under snow and the high roads are shut, so the mountain birds wait.

Rocky Mountain National Park and the northern Front Range

White-tailed Ptarmigan on the tundra an hour from Denver, and swifts nesting behind waterfalls

Trail Ridge Road climbs from montane forest to tundra in a single morning, and the Front Range holds the whole Colorado elevation series along it. Six breeding colonies of American Black Swift nest behind waterfalls in the park, and White-tailed Ptarmigan hold the alpine grassland at the top of the road.

  • Habitat: Alpine grassland, coniferous forest and open meadows, with montane willow along the streams.
  • When: June to August, once Trail Ridge Road is open and the alpine birds are accessible. The owls call in April and May, and the rosy finches drop to the valleys in winter.
  • Getting there: Denver is the airport. From I-25 take Highway 34 or Highway 36 west to Estes Park, then Highway 34 to the north entrance or Highway 36 to the south entrance where the park headquarters sits. From I-70 take Highway 40 north to Granby, then Highway 34 north to Grand Lake on the western side.
  • Base yourself in: Estes Park
  • Look for: White-tailed Ptarmigan, American Black Swift, Dusky Grouse, Band-tailed Pigeon, Boreal Owl, Flammulated Owl, Calliope Hummingbird, Lewis's Woodpecker, Virginia's Warbler, Brown-capped Rosy Finch, Broad-tailed Hummingbird, American Three-toed Woodpecker, Clark's Nutcracker, Mountain Chickadee, Pine Grosbeak, Cassin's Finch, American Dipper
  • Parks and reserves: Rocky Mountain National Park, Arapaho National Forest
  • Stops:
    • Medicine Bow Curve : A pull-out on Trail Ridge Road at 3,600 metres, well above treeline on the highest continuous paved road in the United States. White-tailed Ptarmigan and American Pipit hold the Colorado tundra around it, and the road closes with the first heavy snow.
    • Endovalley : A picnic area and beaver ponds below Old Fall River Road, with willow carr along the water. Boreal and Flammulated Owl are both heard from the road above it in spring.
  • Visitor centres: Rocky Mountain National Park, Beaver Meadows Visitor Center, Alpine Visitor Center, Trail Ridge Road
  • Also covers: Alpine Visitor Center, Trail Ridge Road, Arapaho National Forest, Boulder, Endovalley, Estes Park, Grand Lake, Medicine Bow Curve, Rocky Mountain National Park, Beaver Meadows Visitor Center
  • Also watch for: Elk bugle through the park in September and October, and bighorn sheep come down to Sheep Lakes in early summer.

The alpine passes above treeline

Three rosy finches at one feeder, and ptarmigan on the ridge above the car park

A short chain of paved passes west of Denver reaches alpine tundra above treeline without a park entrance or a long walk. Guanella Pass is the ptarmigan site Colorado is known for, and in winter the feeders at the foot of the passes draw all three rosy finches together, which is a combination very few places offer.

  • Habitat: Alpine tundra, willow carr at treeline, spruce and fir below it, and talus on the high slopes.
  • When: July and August for the breeding tundra birds, and November to March for the rosy finches at the lower feeders. The passes close with snow, so the winter birding works from the valley towns.
  • Getting there: Denver is the airport and everything here is within ninety minutes of it. Guanella Pass runs south from Georgetown on I-70, Loveland Pass carries US 6 over the divide above the Eisenhower Tunnel, and the Mount Blue Sky road climbs from Idaho Springs.
  • Base yourself in: Georgetown
  • Look for: White-tailed Ptarmigan, Brown-capped Rosy Finch, Black Rosy Finch, Grey-crowned Rosy Finch, American Pipit, Horned Lark, Dusky Grouse, Clark's Nutcracker, Pine Grosbeak, Golden Eagle
  • Stops:
    • : A parking area at 3,570 metres with a boardwalk onto the tundra. White-tailed Ptarmigan hold the willow above it and are watched here more reliably than anywhere in the state.
    • : The valley town at the foot of Guanella Pass, and the base for the winter rosy finch feeders when the pass itself is under snow.
  • Also covers: Georgetown, Guanella Pass summit, Idaho Springs, Loveland Pass, Mount Blue Sky
  • Also watch for: Mountain goat and bighorn sheep are both regular on the Mount Blue Sky road, and marmot and pika hold the talus.

North Park and the Arapaho refuge

Two hundred sage grouse on a lek, watched from a chamber of commerce minibus

North Park is a high sagebrush basin ringed by mountains, and the Arapaho refuge in the middle of it holds a breeding population of around two hundred Sage Grouse. The lek tours run for two weekends in spring and are the reason most birders come.

  • Habitat: Wet meadows, streams and sagebrush flats, with willow along the Illinois River.
  • When: Late April and early May for the lek tours, which run at weekends. Breeding birds hold the sage through June, and all three rosy finches are common in winter and spring.
  • Getting there: Denver is the airport, about three hours away. The refuge sits in Jackson County south of Walden on Highway 125. The lek tours are led by the North Park Chamber of Commerce with the state wildlife department, and they book up.
  • Base yourself in: Walden
  • Look for: Sage Grouse, Rufous Hummingbird, Brewer's Sparrow, Lark Bunting, Brown-capped Rosy Finch, Black Rosy Finch, Grey-crowned Rosy Finch, Sage Thrasher, Sagebrush Sparrow, Wilson's Phalarope, American Avocet, Long-billed Curlew
  • Parks and reserves: Arapaho National Wildlife Refuge, State Forest State Park
  • Stops:
    • Arapaho refuge auto tour : A six mile gravel loop through wet meadow and sage, driveable all year. Wilson's Phalarope spin on the ponds and Sage Thrasher sings from the tallest bushes.
  • Also covers: Arapaho National Wildlife Refuge, Arapaho refuge auto tour, State Forest State Park, Walden
  • Also watch for: Moose were introduced to North Park in the 1970s and are now readily seen in the willow along the Illinois River.

The Pawnee grassland and the northeast plains

Mountain Plover on bare dirt, and Thick-billed Longspur singing over the shortgrass

The Pawnee National Grassland is the shortgrass prairie Colorado is best known for among birders, and two of its breeding birds are hard to find anywhere else in the country. Mountain Plover wants bare ground and prairie dog towns rather than grass, which is why it is missed by people looking in the wrong place.

  • Habitat: Shortgrass prairie surrounded by croplands and grazing land, with prairie dog towns and scattered windbreaks.
  • When: April through June, which covers the longspur song flight and the plovers on territory. The Wray prairie chicken leks are watched in late March and April.
  • Getting there: Denver and Fort Collins are the airports. The grassland lies in Weld County northeast of Greeley, and the birding is done from the county roads on the Crow Valley side. Wray is a further two hours east towards the Nebraska line.
  • Base yourself in: Fort Collins
  • Look for: Mountain Plover, Thick-billed Longspur, Chestnut-collared Longspur, Lark Bunting, Cassin's Sparrow, Burrowing Owl, Ferruginous Hawk, Long-billed Curlew, Greater Prairie Chicken, Swainson's Hawk, Horned Lark, Prairie Falcon
  • Parks and reserves: Pawnee National Grassland
  • Stops:
    • : A cottonwood grove and campground on the edge of the grassland, and the migrant trap the whole plain funnels into. It is also the place to leave a car before driving the section roads.
    • : A town on the Nebraska line whose sandhills hold Greater Prairie Chicken leks. The viewing is run as a booked tour each spring.
  • Also covers: Crow Valley Recreation Area, Fort Collins, Greeley, Pawnee National Grassland, Wray
  • Also watch for: Black-tailed prairie dog towns are the structure the plovers and the Burrowing Owls both depend on, and pronghorn range the same short grass.

Comanche National Grassland and the southeast

Lesser Prairie Chickens from a booked blind, in the only corner of the state that still has them

The southeast corner is the driest and least visited part of Colorado, and it holds the state's last Lesser Prairie Chickens in Baca and Prowers Counties. The canyons cut into the grassland add a southwestern list that the rest of the plains does not carry.

  • Habitat: Mixed-grass and shortgrass prairie with canyonlands, cottonwood draws and juniper on the canyon rims.
  • When: Late March and April for the prairie chicken leks, and April to June for the breeding canyon birds. Summer on the open plain is hot.
  • Getting there: Denver is four hours away and Lamar is the practical base. The leks sit in mixed-grass prairie near Campo, and the blind is booked through the Comanche National Grassland office one mile south of Springfield.
  • Base yourself in: Lamar
  • Look for: Lesser Prairie Chicken, Scaled Quail, Cassin's Sparrow, Curve-billed Thrasher, Greater Roadrunner, Canyon Wren, Rock Wren, Golden Eagle, Ferruginous Hawk, Burrowing Owl, Chihuahuan Raven, Common Poorwill
  • Parks and reserves: Comanche National Grassland, John Martin Reservoir State Park
  • Stops:
    • : A box canyon in the far southeast corner with cottonwoods, a spring and rock art on the walls. Canyon Wren and Rock Wren hold the cliffs and the draw pulls migrants out of the plain.
  • Also covers: Campo, Comanche National Grassland, John Martin Reservoir State Park, Lamar, Picture Canyon, Springfield
  • Also watch for: The canyon walls carry dinosaur trackways at Picketwire, and swift fox hold the open grassland.

The San Luis Valley

Twenty thousand Sandhill Cranes on the way to Bosque del Apache

The San Luis Valley is a high desert basin at 2,300 metres with the Rio Grande running through it, and the wetlands on its floor are the major stopover for the Rocky Mountain population of Sandhill Crane. The refuges hold the birds in March and again in October, with the Sangre de Cristo range behind them.

  • Habitat: Semi-permanent wetlands, wet meadows, river oxbows, short grass and a riparian corridor in the Rio Grande floodplain, with upland shrubland of greasewood, rabbitbrush and four-wing saltbush.
  • When: Spring and autumn passage are the best times, with the crane peak in March and again in October. The Monte Vista Crane Festival falls in March.
  • Getting there: Alamosa has a small airport and Denver is four hours north. Both refuges lie between the towns of Alamosa and Monte Vista in south-central Colorado, with auto tour routes on each, and Great Sand Dunes is half an hour northeast of Alamosa.
  • Base yourself in: Alamosa
  • Look for: Sandhill Crane, White-faced Ibis, American Avocet, Snowy Plover, Wilson's Phalarope, Cinnamon Teal, Northern Harrier, Ferruginous Hawk, Sage Thrasher, Mountain Bluebird, Yellow-headed Blackbird, Short-eared Owl
  • Parks and reserves: Alamosa National Wildlife Refuge, Monte Vista National Wildlife Refuge, Great Sand Dunes National Park
  • Stops:
    • Monte Vista auto tour : A gravel loop through flooded meadow and barley stubble, which is where the cranes feed. It is driveable in any car and the birds ignore a stopped vehicle.
  • Also covers: Alamosa National Wildlife Refuge, Great Sand Dunes National Park, Monte Vista auto tour, Monte Vista National Wildlife Refuge
  • Also watch for: The dunes are the tallest in North America and hold an endemic tiger beetle, and elk winter along the valley edge.

The Gunnison Basin and the Black Canyon

The Gunnison Grouse, described as a species in 2000 and found almost nowhere else

The Gunnison Grouse was separated from the Sage Grouse on body size, plumage and display, and the Gunnison Basin holds the large majority of the world population. The Waunita lek is the site set aside for watching it, which keeps pressure off the birds elsewhere in the basin.

  • Habitat: Shrub steppe below 2,800 metres including sagebrush, riparian areas and meadows, with pinyon and juniper on the mesas.
  • When: April for the lek, which is watched at first light from the road. The canyon rim birds are on territory from May to July.
  • Getting there: Gunnison has a small airport and Denver is four hours away over the passes. The Waunita lek is a designated Colorado Watchable Wildlife site east of Gunnison, on land the Bureau of Land Management manages, and it is watched from the road rather than approached.
  • Base yourself in: Gunnison
  • Look for: Gunnison Grouse, Sage Thrasher, Brewer's Sparrow, Sagebrush Sparrow, Pinyon Jay, Juniper Titmouse, Canyon Wren, White-throated Swift, Dusky Grouse, American Dipper, Golden Eagle, Peregrine Falcon
  • Parks and reserves: Black Canyon of the Gunnison National Park, Curecanti National Recreation Area, Gunnison National Forest
  • Stops:
    • : The viewing site for the Gunnison Grouse. A car parked on the road before dawn in April is the way it is watched, and arriving late puts the birds off the lek for the morning.
  • Also covers: Black Canyon of the Gunnison National Park, Crested Butte, Curecanti National Recreation Area, Gunnison National Forest, Waunita lek
  • Also watch for: The Black Canyon drops six hundred metres to the river and holds nesting Peregrine Falcon on walls that are closed to climbers while the birds are on eggs.

The Western Slope and the Grand Valley

Pinyon Jay and Juniper Titmouse on red rock, an hour from the Utah line

The Western Slope turns into canyon country west of the divide, and the pinyon and juniper on the mesas above Grand Junction carry a bird list that belongs to the Colorado Plateau rather than the Rockies. Grand Mesa rises out of the same valley to spruce and lakes, so two very different sets sit half an hour apart.

  • Habitat: Pinyon and juniper woodland on mesas and canyon rims, sagebrush and greasewood on the valley floor, and spruce, fir and aspen on Grand Mesa above 3,000 metres.
  • When: April to June for the breeding birds on the mesas, with Grand Mesa best from June once the snow is off the top. Winter brings rosy finches down to the valley.
  • Getting there: Grand Junction has an airport and sits on I-70. Colorado National Monument rises directly above the town on Rim Rock Drive, and Highway 65 from Mesa reaches Grand Mesa, which climbs to over 3,000 metres in half an hour.
  • Base yourself in: Grand Junction
  • Look for: Pinyon Jay, Juniper Titmouse, Grey Vireo, Black-throated Grey Warbler, Chukar Partridge, Gambel's Quail, Canyon Wren, White-throated Swift, Rock Wren, Golden Eagle, Williamson's Sapsucker, Green-tailed Towhee
  • Parks and reserves: Colorado National Monument, Grand Mesa National Forest
  • Stops:
    • Rim Rock Drive : A paved road along the canyon rims with pull-outs over the walls. White-throated Swift and Canyon Wren work the cliffs below the parapets and Pinyon Jay crosses the pinyon flats behind them.
  • Visitor centres: Colorado National Monument visitor center
  • Also covers: Colorado National Monument visitor center, Fruita, Grand Junction, Grand Mesa National Forest, Rim Rock Drive
  • Also watch for: Desert bighorn sheep were reintroduced to the monument canyons, and collared lizard is common on the warm rock.

Frequently Asked Questions

When is the best time to go birding in Colorado?

April, if the grouse are why you are coming. Six species display in that month and Colorado is the only state where all of them are reachable on one trip. June to September is the answer for the mountains, because Trail Ridge Road and the passes are open and the tundra birds become easy. March and October are the crane months in the San Luis Valley.

Can I really see six grouse species on one trip?

Yes, in the second half of April, with planning. Sage Grouse at Walden, Gunnison Grouse at Waunita, Greater Prairie Chicken at Wray, Lesser Prairie Chicken at Campo, Sharp-tailed Grouse on the northern plains and Dusky Grouse in the mountains. White-tailed Ptarmigan makes a seventh if you add a pass. Several of these need booking months ahead.

Where do I go for White-tailed Ptarmigan?

Guanella Pass, an hour and a half from Denver on a paved road. The willow above the car park holds them all year, white in winter and mottled grey in summer. Rocky Mountain National Park has them too, higher up Trail Ridge Road.

Which sites need booking?

The Walden sage grouse tours run for two weekends and fill early. The Campo prairie chicken blind is reserved through the Comanche National Grassland office at Springfield. The Wray prairie chicken viewing is a booked tour each spring.

When does Trail Ridge Road open?

Usually late May or early June, and it closes with the first heavy snow, often in the second half of October. The alpine birding depends entirely on it, so a trip planned for the tundra before June will be looking at snow.

Do I need a four-wheel drive?

An ordinary car covers almost everything. The Pawnee section roads, the Comanche canyons and some of the lek approaches are gravel and become difficult after rain, so clearance helps more than drive does.

Is the eastern half of the state worth the drive?

Yes, and it is the half most visitors skip. Mountain Plover and Thick-billed Longspur breed on the Pawnee and are hard to find elsewhere, and the southeastern canyons carry a southwestern list that the mountains never repeat.

Where can I see all three rosy finches?

At feeders in the valley towns below the passes from November to March, with Georgetown the usual base. Brown-capped Rosy Finch breeds almost entirely in Colorado, and it joins the Black and Grey-crowned birds once the high ground is under snow.

Can I bird here with limited mobility?

Sites on this page differ from each other more than any rating could carry, so this is the framework the standard British guides use, and the reserve's own page and warden hold the answers.

  • Permission · some reserves open ground to disabled visitors that is closed to everyone else, and it is granted by asking.
  • Priority · parking bays, hides and the nearest paths are often held for disabled visitors. Ask what is reserved and how to claim it.
  • Parking · how close a car reaches the birds shapes the whole day. Ring ahead for the nearest hard standing to the water or the hide.
  • Paths · surfaces, gradients and gate widths vary inside a single reserve. Ask which trail is boarded, surfaced or level.
  • Perches · a hide with a low window, or a bench where the view opens, turns a viewpoint into a morning.

Every reserve above with its own link carries its access detail there, and a warden answers what a page cannot.
