Birding in Newfoundland and Labrador

Burrows measures the seabird cities of this coast in millions rather than thousands, and two of them, Witless Bay and Cape St Mary's, can be walked to from a car park.

Birds that decide the trip

Atlantic Puffin at Witless Bay

Hundreds of thousands nest on four islands off one village, and the boats sit under the burrow slopes with the birds passing overhead in a constant stream.

When June and July, from the Bay Bulls boats.

Where Witless Bay and the Avalon

Northern Gannet on Bird Rock

A flat-topped stack crowded with gannets sits a few metres off the cliff path, and the walk to it from the car park takes twenty minutes.

When June and July, from the headland path.

Where Cape St Mary's and Placentia Bay

Leach's Storm Petrel off the Avalon

The Witless Bay islands hold one of the largest colonies in the world, and the birds only come ashore after dark, so a boat is the way to meet them.

When June to August, offshore.

Where Witless Bay and the Avalon

Harlequin Duck on the winter coast

Burrows records a Christmas count of 143 at Cape St Mary's, on rock washed by heavy surf, which is where this duck spends the winter.

When December to March, on surf-washed rock.

Where Cape St Mary's and Placentia Bay, Labrador

Willow Ptarmigan on the barrens

It holds the open barrens on both the island and the mainland, white in winter and brown in summer, and walks rather than flies until it has to.

When All year, easiest in June on the plateau.

Where Gros Morne and the west coast, Labrador

Gyrfalcon in Labrador

It nests on cliffs through the north of the mainland and hunts ptarmigan across the tundra, which is the largest falcon anywhere doing the plainest thing.

When June and July, on the northern cliffs.

Where Labrador

Thick-billed Murre on the colony ledges

It shares the ledges with the Common Murre in colonies Burrows counts in the millions, and telling the two apart at speed is the whole exercise.

When June and July, on the cliffs.

Where Witless Bay and the Avalon, Cape St Mary's and Placentia Bay

Two-barred Crossbill in the island spruce

The island's conifer forest holds crossbills all year, and their numbers swing with the cone crop rather than the season.

When All year, in spruce and fir.

Where Gros Morne and the west coast, Terra Nova and the north-east coast

When to Go

Burrows notes that the auks are back off their breeding cliffs in Newfoundland and Labrador in early March and take about a month to settle onto the ledges, which sets the shape of the year here. The colonies are full through June and July and empty again by early September.

The arrival

March and April

Auks appear off the cliffs and take a month to come ashore.

Go for Cape St Mary's and the Avalon headlands.

Trade off Cold, often foul, and the boats are not yet running.

Typical daily average

The seabird season

June and July

Puffins, murres, kittiwakes and gannets fill the colonies.

Go for Witless Bay by boat, and the Cape St Mary's headland on foot.

Trade off Fog can close a colony for days at a time.

Typical daily average

The iceberg season

Late May and June

Bergs drift down the north-east coast past the seabird headlands.

Go for Twillingate, Cape Bonavista and the Strait of Belle Isle.

Trade off A cold spring keeps the pack ice in and closes the coastal boats.

Typical daily average

The winter coast

December to February

Burrows records 7,389 Common Eiders and 143 Harlequins on Cape St Mary's Christmas counts.

Go for Cape St Mary's, Placentia Bay and the St John's harbours.

Trade off Short days, storms and roads that close without much notice.

The Avalon seabird cities

St John's (YYT) 2 nights, Bay Bulls 3 nights, St Brides 3 nights, back to St John's

Eight days in late June on the two colonies Burrows rates highest for variety. Boats out of Bay Bulls to sit under the puffin cliffs, then the walk across the barrens to the gannet stack at Cape St Mary's. About 400 km, and fog is the one thing that can spoil it.

Icebergs and the north-east coast

Gander (YQX), Terra Nova 2 nights, Twillingate 3 nights, back to Gander

Six days in the first half of June, on the coast the Labrador Current brings the ice down. Bergs ground on the shoals off the headlands, the sheltered inlets behind hold loons and sea ducks, and the humpbacks arrive at the end of the month with the capelin.

9 days

Labrador and the Torngats

Goose Bay (YYR) 2 nights, Torngat Mountains base camp 5 nights, back to Goose Bay

Nine days in the second half of July, most of them at the base camp inside the park. Arctic mountain birds at the southern edge of their range, polar bears on the shore, and a licensed bear guide with every party. The season is about six weeks long.

Witless Bay and the Avalon

Four islands off a village, holding one of the largest puffin colonies on the continent

The islands off Witless Bay hold Atlantic Puffins in hundreds of thousands and Leach's Storm Petrels in greater numbers still, and boats run out from Bay Bulls all summer to sit under the cliffs. Burrows puts this colony and Cape St Mary's among the few with the greatest variety of seabirds in North America.

  • Habitat: Sea-stack island and cliff, turf and burrow slope, spruce and fir on the mainland, and cold inshore water.
  • When: June and July, when the colonies are full and the boats run daily. The birds are gone by early September.
  • Getting there: St John's is the airport, forty minutes north. Tour boats leave Bay Bulls through the summer, landing on the islands is not allowed, and fog cancels sailings often enough to leave more than one day for it.
  • Base yourself in: Bay Bulls
  • Look for: Atlantic Puffin, Common Murre, Thick-billed Murre, Razorbill, Black-legged Kittiwake, Leach's Storm Petrel, Black Guillemot, Northern Fulmar, Great Shearwater, Sooty Shearwater, Common Eider, Iceland Gull
  • Parks and reserves: La Manche Provincial Park (State or provincial park)
  • Also covers: Bay Bulls, Cape Spear, La Manche Provincial Park, St John's, Witless Bay Ecological Reserve
  • Also watch for: Humpback whales feed on the capelin that draw the seabirds, in the same water and in the same weeks, and minke whales and icebergs come with them.

Cape St Mary's and Placentia Bay

A gannet stack a stone's throw from the cliff path, at the end of a 15 km road across the barrens

Bird Rock at Cape St Mary's is a flat-topped sea stack packed with Northern Gannets, separated from the mainland cliff by a narrow gap, so the birds pass at eye level a few metres away. The walk from the interpretation centre takes about twenty minutes across open barrens.

  • Habitat: Sea stack and vertical cliff, coastal barrens and bog on the headland, sheep pasture inland, and cold open bay.
  • When: June and July for the colony. December and January for the eiders and Harlequins on the same coast.
  • Getting there: St John's is the airport, two hours east. The reserve has an interpretation centre with an entry fee, the path to the stack is a twenty-minute walk over open ground, and the last 15 km of road crosses bare barrens.
  • Base yourself in: St Brides
  • Look for: Northern Gannet, Black-legged Kittiwake, Common Murre, Razorbill, Thick-billed Murre, Black Guillemot, Great Cormorant, Common Eider, Harlequin Duck, Purple Sandpiper, Iceland Gull, Glaucous Gull
  • Parks and reserves: Cape St. Mary's Ecological Reserve
  • Also covers: Argentia, Cape St Mary's Ecological Reserve, Cape St. Mary's Ecological Reserve, Placentia, St Brides
  • Also watch for: The barrens behind the cape carry a caribou herd and an arctic plant community that grows here at sea level because the fog keeps the summers cold.

Gros Morne and the west coast

Fjords cut into a plateau, with the province's only real farmland at the southern end

Gros Morne is a plateau split by freshwater fjords, with alpine barrens on top and boreal forest below. The Codroy Valley in the far south-west is the exception on this coast: Burrows notes its richer soils have given open-country birds a footing in Newfoundland from which many are extending north.

  • Habitat: Freshwater fjord and cliff, alpine plateau barrens, balsam fir and spruce forest, bare peridotite tableland, and coastal sand and marsh.
  • When: June and July for the breeding birds, and August for the shorebirds on the coastal plain beaches.
  • Getting there: Deer Lake is the airport, half an hour from the park boundary. Gros Morne charges a day fee, the Western Brook Pond boat needs booking, and the Codroy Valley is three hours south.
  • Base yourself in: Rocky Harbour
  • Look for: Willow Ptarmigan, Rock Ptarmigan, Spruce Grouse, American Three-toed Woodpecker, Boreal Chickadee, Canada Jay, Pine Grosbeak, Two-barred Crossbill, Blackpoll Warbler, Fox Sparrow, Piping Plover, Northern Harrier
  • National park: Gros Morne National Park
  • Also covers: Bonne Bay, Codroy Valley, Gros Morne National Park, Stephenville
  • Also watch for: The Tablelands in the park are exposed mantle rock from beneath the ocean floor, orange and almost bare, and the reason the park is a World Heritage Site.

Terra Nova and the north-east coast

Sheltered inlets and headlands on the coast the icebergs come down

The north-east coast is where the Labrador Current brings icebergs and pack ice past the headlands each spring, and Terra Nova protects a stretch of sheltered inlet and boreal forest behind them. Cape Freels and Cape Bonavista are the two headlands Burrows names as the prime locations on this shore.

  • Habitat: Rock headland and cove, sheltered inlet and estuary, balsam fir and black spruce forest, bog and pond.
  • When: Late May and June for the icebergs and the arriving birds, and July for the whales and the capelin roll.
  • Getting there: Gander is the nearest airport, an hour from the park. Terra Nova charges a day fee and has serviced campgrounds, and Twillingate is two hours north on a paved road.
  • Base yourself in: Twillingate
  • Look for: Common Loon, Red-throated Loon, Black Guillemot, Common Eider, Long-tailed Duck, Surf Scoter, Black Scoter, Harlequin Duck, Bald Eagle, Osprey, Boreal Chickadee, Fox Sparrow
  • Parks and reserves: Terra Nova National Park, Terra Nova Migratory Bird Sanctuary (Reserve), Eastport – Round Island Marine Protected Area
  • Also covers: Cape Bonavista, Cape Freels, Eastport – Round Island Marine Protected Area, Terra Nova Migratory Bird Sanctuary, Terra Nova National Park, Twillingate
  • Also watch for: Icebergs calved from Greenland ground on the shoals off these headlands every spring, and the humpbacks arrive behind them with the capelin.

Labrador

The mainland, running from spruce forest to arctic mountain over a thousand kilometres

Labrador is a different country from the island: boreal forest and bog in the south, tundra and bare mountain in the north, and almost no roads. The Torngats reach 1,600 m straight out of the sea and hold arctic breeding birds at the southern end of their range.

  • Habitat: Arctic mountain and glacier-cut fjord, tundra and rock barren, black spruce and lichen woodland in the south, and coastal pack ice.
  • When: Late June to early August, which is the whole season this far north.
  • Getting there: Goose Bay has flights and a road south to the Strait of Belle Isle ferry. The Torngats are reached only by charter boat or air from Goose Bay or Kuujjuaq, in a season that runs about six weeks, and a licensed bear guide is required.
  • Base yourself in: Happy Valley-Goose Bay
  • Look for: Willow Ptarmigan, Rock Ptarmigan, Gyrfalcon, Golden Eagle, Harlequin Duck, Long-tailed Jaeger, American Golden Plover, Snow Bunting, Northern Wheatear, Redpoll, Grey-cheeked Thrush, Little Auk
  • Parks and reserves: Torngat Mountains National Park, Red Bay Basque Whaling Station (Protected area)
  • Also covers: Battle Harbour, Happy Valley-Goose Bay, Red Bay Basque Whaling Station, Torngat Mountains National Park
  • Also watch for: The Torngats hold the one wild polar bear population in eastern Canada that people regularly meet on land, and it is the reason Newfoundland and Labrador requires a licensed bear guide with every party in the park.

Frequently Asked Questions

When is the best time to go birding in Newfoundland and Labrador?

June and July. The seabird colonies are full, the boats run daily, the icebergs are still coming down the north-east coast in early June, and the whales arrive with the capelin in July. The colonies empty by early September.

Which colony should I choose?

Both, if there is time. Witless Bay is a boat trip to islands packed with puffins and storm petrels, and Cape St Mary's is a twenty-minute walk to a gannet stack a few metres off the cliff. Burrows rates the two among the few North American colonies with the greatest variety of seabirds.

How do I get to Labrador?

Fly to Goose Bay, or take the ferry across the Strait of Belle Isle to the southern coast and drive north. The Torngats in the far north are reached only by charter boat or air, in a season of about six weeks, and every party needs a licensed bear guide.

Is there anything in winter?

Sea ducks in numbers. Burrows records 7,389 Common Eiders and, in another year, 143 Harlequin Ducks on Cape St Mary's Christmas counts, along with the white-winged gulls that winter in the harbours.

What are the odds of fog?

High, on the Avalon in summer. It closes the colony boats for days at a time, and Cape St Mary's can be white from the car park to the cliff. Leave more days than the birding needs.

Can I bird here with limited mobility?

Sites on this page differ from each other more than any rating could carry, so this is the framework the standard British guides use, and the reserve's own page and warden hold the answers.

  • Permission · some reserves open ground to disabled visitors that is closed to everyone else, and it is granted by asking.
  • Priority · parking bays, hides and the nearest paths are often held for disabled visitors. Ask what is reserved and how to claim it.
  • Parking · how close a car reaches the birds shapes the whole day. Ring ahead for the nearest hard standing to the water or the hide.
  • Paths · surfaces, gradients and gate widths vary inside a single reserve. Ask which trail is boarded, surfaced or level.
  • Perches · a hide with a low window, or a bench where the view opens, turns a viewpoint into a morning.
