At a glance

Best months
  • September to November for the swamp and river birds
  • December to March for the migrant raptors and the Kalahari after rain
Regions

Routes
The delta, Savuti and the Chobe10 daysThe panhandle and the pans9 daysThe Kalahari from the south8 daysThe eastern hardveld and the Tuli Block6 days
Getting in
Gaborone has the main international airport, and Maun and Kasane take the flights that most birding trips arrive on.
Getting around
Tarred roads link the main towns and a 4WD is needed inside every major park. Distances are long, fuel is only in the larger towns, and driving after dark is unsafe because of game and livestock.

When to Go

Botswana runs one rainy season, from about November to April, and one long dry season after it. The wet months bring the migrant raptors, fill the pans and green the Kalahari. The dry months firm the roads and concentrate game on the water that is left, and the Okavango flood reaches Maun in the middle of them.

Hot and wet

December to March

The rains fill the pans, green the Kalahari and bring the migrants, and the bird list is at its longest.

Go for Migrant raptors over the Central Kalahari and Savuti, Caspian Plover on the pans, and Olive-tree Warbler in the thornveld.

Trade off Sand roads turn to mud, black cotton soil becomes treacherous, and several parks are hard to move around in.

Cool and dry

May to August

The roads firm up, the days are pleasant, and game concentrates on what water is left.

Go for Moremi and the delta at their easiest, and the Okavango flood arriving at Maun from about mid July.

Trade off The Palearctic migrants have gone, and mornings in the Kalahari are cold.

Hot and dry

The first migrants arrive, the bee-eater colonies open, and sandbars are still above water.

Go for Southern Carmine Bee-eater colonies in the panhandle, African Skimmer on the sandbars, and Rock Pratincole on the Chobe.

Trade off It is the hottest part of the year and water is scarce away from the rivers.

The Okavango Delta and Moremi

Permanent swamp and flood plain in the middle of dry sand country

A series of faults turned the Okavango River inland about two million years ago, and it now spreads into a green fan buried in Kalahari sand. Moremi Game Reserve covers the eastern side and is the part an overland traveller can drive. A poled canoe brings the swamp birds within a few metres of the water.

  • Habitat: Flood plain, permanent channels and papyrus swamp, with tall riparian woodland on the island fringes, open ilala palm savanna inside them and mopane woodland along the eastern edge.
  • When: May to October gives firm roads, low water and concentrated game. November to March brings the migrants and a wider list, with fewer visitors on the same ground.
  • Getting there: South Gate is about 100 km north of Maun, tarred as far as Shorobe and sand for the rest. A 4WD is needed inside the reserve all year, and the interior channels are reached by boat or by a poled canoe with a local poler.
  • Base yourself in: Maun
  • All year: Pel's Fishing Owl, Slaty Egret, Coppery-tailed Coucal, Wattled Crane, Chirping Cisticola, Hartlaub's Babbler, Long-toed Lapwing, Lesser Jacana
  • In the breeding season: Southern Carmine Bee-eater, Wattled Crane, African Skimmer
  • On passage: Steppe Eagle, Lesser Spotted Eagle, Wahlberg's Eagle
  • In winter: Pel's Fishing Owl, Slaty Egret, Coppery-tailed Coucal, Hartlaub's Babbler
  • National park:
    • Moremi Game Reserve, A reserve over the eastern side of the delta, and the one part of the swamp an overland traveller can drive into.
  • Stops:
    • Xakanaxa: A lagoon on the northern side with a boat launch. Heronries and water-bird roosts sit on the nearby lagoons.
    • : A river and flood plain on the northern boundary. The loops here give the best raptor watching in the reserve and Wattled Crane feeds on foot.
    • : The last village on the tar road north from Maun, where the gravel and then the sand begin.
  • Also covers: Khwai, Moremi Game Reserve, Shorobe, Xakanaxa
  • Also watch for: Elephant and Red Lechwe are abundant here, and Sitatunga, Hippopotamus and Crocodile hold the swamps.

The Okavango Panhandle

One deep channel between papyrus banks, on a road any car can drive

Above the swamps the Okavango runs in a single channel, and the panhandle holds the same birds without the sand roads. Southern Carmine Bee-eaters cut colonies into the banks in spring, and a boat cruise finds Pel's Fishing Owl and White-backed Night Heron on their day roosts.

  • Habitat: Well developed riparian woodland along a single deep channel, with extensive papyrus beds, quiet backwaters and flooded grassland behind them.
  • When: September to November is the strongest window, when the migrants arrive, the bee-eater colonies are active and the sandbars are still above water. Late summer adds a run of flood-plain water birds.
  • Getting there: A tarred road runs the length of the panhandle from the northern border down through Gumare and on to Maun, and the river settlements sit a short signposted distance off it. The channels themselves need a boat.
  • Base yourself in: Shakawe
  • All year: African Pygmy Goose, Slaty Egret, White-backed Night Heron, African Skimmer, Southern Carmine Bee-eater, Greater Swamp Warbler, Brown Firefinch, Long-toed Lapwing
  • In the breeding season: Southern Carmine Bee-eater, African Skimmer, Holub's Golden Weaver
  • On passage: Collared Pratincole, Blue-cheeked Bee-eater
  • In winter: Pel's Fishing Owl, Hartlaub's Babbler, Swamp Boubou
  • Stops:
    • : The northern end of the panhandle, and the base for boat cruises and for the bee-eater colonies in spring.
    • : A river settlement on the tar road, and a launching point into the channels below the panhandle.
    • : A village near the point where the single channel begins to spread, with camps on the water.
    • : The road town at the southern end of the panhandle, and the last supplies before the drive to Maun.
  • Also covers: Etsha 6, Gumare, Sepopa, Shakawe
  • Also watch for: Nile Crocodile and Hippopotamus hold the channels, and Sitatunga feed at the papyrus edge in the late afternoon.

Maun and the Thamalakane

Riparian woodland, thornveld and a river that floods in the dry season

Maun sits at the southern tip of the delta on the Thamalakane, and the flood reaches it in the middle of the dry season rather than during the rains. When the river is down, flooded grassland at its edges carries most of the swamp specials within a short drive of town.

  • Habitat: Riparian woodland along the river, dry acacia savanna behind it, and wetland with emergent vegetation wherever the flood spreads.
  • When: The Thamalakane usually carries water from about mid July to March, which is when the water birds are here. Olive-tree Warbler sings in the thornveld from December to March.
  • Getting there: Maun has the regional airport and tarred roads east to Nata and Kasane, north towards Moremi and west along the panhandle. Every site around the town is reachable in a two wheel drive vehicle.
  • Base yourself in: Maun
  • All year: Slaty Egret, Rufous-bellied Heron, African Pygmy Goose, Red-necked Falcon, Dickinson's Kestrel, Bradfield's Hornbill, Olive-tree Warbler, Long-toed Lapwing
  • In the breeding season: Southern Carmine Bee-eater, Holub's Golden Weaver
  • On passage: Olive-tree Warbler, Blue-cheeked Bee-eater
  • In winter: Red-necked Falcon, Dickinson's Kestrel, Bradfield's Hornbill
  • National park: Maun Game Reserve
  • Stops:
    • : A town of substantial size at the southern tip of the delta, and the springboard for most trips north.
    • : The river through town. Its flooded grassland edges hold crakes, jacanas and the occasional Slaty Egret.
    • : A lake bed that is usually dry and grass-covered. In the years it floods, thousands of water birds arrive to breed.
  • Also covers: Lake Ngami, Maun Game Reserve, Thamalakane River, Toteng
  • Also watch for: Red Lechwe graze the Thamalakane flood plain close to town.

Chobe and the Kasane riverfront

A broad river, teak forest behind it, and the densest game in the country

Over 450 species have been recorded around the Chobe River and the town at its eastern end, and almost 300 have been seen there in a single day. A boat cruise upstream reaches the flood-plain birds within an hour, and the teak forest inland holds a separate set the river never shows.

  • Habitat: Open flood plain with grass, reeds and papyrus, riparian thicket along the water, and teak, acacia and mopane woodland inland.
  • When: November to March brings the migrants and the summer raptors. African Skimmer and Rock Pratincole need exposed sandbars and rocks, so they are surest from about August until the river rises.
  • Getting there: Kasane sits on the main road between the northern border crossings and has the region's second airport. The riverfront is watched from the road west of town or from a boat, and the sandy tracks behind the town need a 4WD.
  • Base yourself in: Kasane
  • All year: Rock Pratincole, African Skimmer, African Finfoot, Collared Palm Thrush, Racket-tailed Roller, Slaty Egret, Western Banded Snake Eagle, Trumpeter Hornbill
  • In the breeding season: Southern Carmine Bee-eater, Rock Pratincole
  • On passage: River Warbler, Thrush Nightingale, Olive-tree Warbler
  • In winter: Racket-tailed Roller, Collared Palm Thrush, African Finfoot
  • National park: Chobe National Park
  • Stops:
    • : The riverside town at the eastern end of the park, and the base for boat cruises on the Chobe.
    • : The corner where four countries meet. The sandy road south from here runs into good acacia woodland.
    • : The western gate on the river, with excellent teak woodland along the road back towards Kasane.
    • : A sandy 10 km track through acacia woodland south of the border road, good for waxbills and migrant warblers.
  • Also covers: Chobe National Park, Kasane, Kazungula, Lesoma, Ngoma
  • Also watch for: Elephant, Buffalo, Lion, Leopard, African Wild Dog and Red Lechwe all use the riverfront.

Savuti and the Mababe Depression

A dry marsh, grassy plains and exceptional raptor watching

The southern part of Chobe National Park is a sand-filled basin inside the Mababe Depression, and the marsh at its centre is now dry grassland studded with dead trees. Kori Bustard stride the plains in numbers, often with a Southern Carmine Bee-eater riding on the back to take what the bustard flushes.

  • Habitat: Grassy plains on a dry marsh floor, pumped pans, acacia thickets around the margins and mopane woodland across the rest.
  • When: November to March for the harriers, the small falcons and the storks that follow army worm and termite emergences. The dry months from May give far easier driving.
  • Getting there: Savuti lies inside Chobe National Park about 160 km north of Maun and about 170 km south west of Kasane. A 4WD is essential, and the black cotton soil of the depression is treacherous after rain.
  • Base yourself in: Maun
  • All year: Kori Bustard, Southern Carmine Bee-eater, Temminck's Courser, Caspian Plover, Burchell's Sandgrouse, Yellow-throated Sandgrouse, Dusky Lark, Bradfield's Hornbill
  • In the breeding season: Kori Bustard, Southern Carmine Bee-eater
  • On passage: Montagu's Harrier, Pallid Harrier, Amur Falcon, Abdim's Stork
  • In winter: Bradfield's Hornbill, Burchell's Sandgrouse, Yellow-throated Sandgrouse
  • Stops:
    • : The marsh and the plains around it, with pumped pans that draw sandgrouse and pratincoles to drink.
    • : A village on the southern approach, at the edge of the black cotton soil of the depression.
  • Also covers: Mababe, Savuti
  • Also watch for: Spotted Hyaena are common and persistent around the camps, and the plains hold large predators all year.

The Makgadikgadi pans and Nata

Salt pan and grassland, with flamingos in tens of thousands when the water comes

The Makgadikgadi pans are the floor of a lake that dried twenty thousand years ago, fed now by the Nata River from the north and by the delta from the west. In a wet year the Nata delta carries water birds in their thousands and Greater and Lesser Flamingos in their tens of thousands. In a dry year the surrounding grassland still holds the Kalahari birds.

  • Habitat: Grassland and salt pan with seasonal marsh at the river mouths, mopane and mixed woodland around the edges, and short grass on the fossil lake floor.
  • When: October to February in a wet year for the flooded delta and the flamingos. The resident grassland birds hold all year and the grass is shortest at the end of the dry season.
  • Getting there: The sanctuary gate is 19 km south of Nata on the Francistown road and most of it is passable in a two wheel drive vehicle. The pan edge itself is treacherous mud, and Nxai Pan needs a 4WD.
  • Base yourself in: Nata
  • All year: Lesser Flamingo, Greater Flamingo, Great White Pelican, Yellow-throated Sandgrouse, Grey Crowned Crane, Northern Black Korhaan, Double-banded Courser, Kori Bustard
  • In the breeding season: Great White Pelican, Grey Crowned Crane
  • On passage: Little Stint, Curlew Sandpiper, Ruff, Black-winged Pratincole
  • In winter: Northern Black Korhaan, Double-banded Courser, Secretarybird
  • Also recorded in the parks here: Abdim's Stork, African Barred Owlet
  • National parks: Nxai Pan National Park, Makgadikgadi Pans National Park
  • Stops:
    • : A community reserve at the northern edge of Sowa Pan. It protects the Nata delta and gives 20 km of grassland driving from the gate.
    • : The eastern pan. When it holds water the flamingo flocks on it can run to hundreds of thousands.
    • : The road junction town where the Francistown, Kasane and Maun roads meet, and the last fuel in several directions.
    • : A village on the Maun road at the northern edge of the pans, with mopane woodland and grass plains around it.
  • Also covers: Gweta, Makgadikgadi Pans National Park, Nata Sanctuary, Nxai Pan National Park, Sowa Pan
  • Also watch for: Zebra and Wildebeest cross the Makgadikgadi grassland in the wet season, and Brown Hyaena work the pan edges.

The Central Kalahari

Fossil valleys and grassy pans in the largest unbroken sand sheet on earth

The Central Kalahari Game Reserve protects a tract of sandveld larger than Switzerland, and Deception Valley in its north east is a broad fossil valley that greens after rain and fills with game. A few days in summer can produce more than twenty raptor species, and the pan edges hold the larks and coursers the sand country is known for.

  • Habitat: Kalahari savanna and scrub on deep sand, with grassy pans, fossil valleys and stands of acacia thicket and woodland. A few artificial waterholes run dry from time to time.
  • When: December to March brings the migrant raptors, the plovers and the greenest pans. May to October is drier and much easier to drive.
  • Getting there: The turn-off is about 3 km north of Rakops and the park gate is a further 44 km across a sand ridge. A 4WD is strongly advised, visitors need to be self-sufficient, and the nearest fuel, water and supplies are in Rakops.
  • Base yourself in: Rakops
  • All year: Pallid Harrier, Caspian Plover, Pink-billed Lark, Rufous-eared Warbler, Kori Bustard, Double-banded Courser, Crimson-breasted Shrike, Burchell's Sandgrouse
  • In the breeding season: Kori Bustard, Crimson-breasted Shrike, Southern Pied Babbler
  • On passage: Montagu's Harrier, Pallid Harrier, Red-footed Falcon, Lesser Kestrel
  • In winter: Pale Chanting Goshawk, Greater Kestrel, Ashy Tit, Marsh Owl
  • National park: Central Kalahari Game Reserve
  • Stops:
    • : The village on the eastern approach, and the closest fuel, water and supplies to the reserve.
    • Deception Valley: A broad fossil valley 40 km inside the gate, grassy after rain and usually full of game.
  • Also covers: Central Kalahari Game Reserve, Deception Valley, Rakops
  • Also watch for: After rain Gemsbok, Springbok and Blue Wildebeest gather in numbers, with Lion, Cheetah and Brown Hyaena behind them.

Gaborone and the south east

Rocky hills, artificial wetlands and grazed grassland at the edge of a capital

The eastern strip of the country has harder soils than the sand and both cities stand on it. Wetlands, thornveld and hills within 25 km of Gaborone regularly produce more than 150 species in a day, and the grazed grassland towards the southern border holds Short-clawed Lark, which lives almost nowhere else.

  • Habitat: Dry Kalahari grassland and grazed thornveld in the south, acacia thicket and artificial wetland near the city, and moist woodland on sandstone cliffs and granite hills.
  • When: November to March for the larks, the harriers and the migrant warblers, and after good rain the grassland can carry eight lark species in a day. Short-clawed Lark calls all year.
  • Getting there: Everything here is on public tarred roads within about 80 km of the capital, which has the main international airport. The vulture cliff at Otse is signposted from the road towards Lobatse and the last stretch is walked.
  • Base yourself in: Gaborone
  • All year: Short-clawed Lark, Cape Vulture, Maccoa Duck, Olive-tree Warbler, Sociable Weaver, South African Cliff Swallow, Verreaux's Eagle, Natal Spurfowl
  • In the breeding season: Cape Vulture, South African Cliff Swallow, Verreaux's Eagle, Black Stork
  • On passage: Montagu's Harrier, Pallid Harrier, Olive-tree Warbler, Common Reed Warbler
  • In winter: Short-clawed Lark, Maccoa Duck, Natal Spurfowl
  • National park: Mannyelanong Game Reserve
  • Stops:
    • : A border settlement with dry grassland north of it. Short-clawed Lark is common in the grazed acacia scrub 14 to 16 km up the road.
    • : A village below a sandstone cliff that carries one of only two Cape Vulture breeding colonies in the country.
    • : The town between the capital and the border grassland, and the nearest accommodation to it.
    • : The capital, with sewage ponds, a dam and two wooded hills inside or beside it.
    • : A town north east of the capital, near the dam and the acacia thicket that holds Olive-tree Warbler.
  • Also covers: Gaborone, Lobatse, Mannyelanong Game Reserve, Mochudi, Otse, Ramatlabama
  • Also watch for: Chacma Baboon and Klipspringer hold the rocky hills, and the reserves around the capital carry plains antelope.

Francistown, the hardveld and the Tuli Block

Granite hills, mixed woodland and a riverine strip on the eastern border

Low wooded hills line the roads out of Francistown and hold Boulder Chat, a bird of rocky slopes under broad-leaved woodland that occurs at only a scattering of sites. South and east of the city the hardveld runs down to the Tuli Block, where the border river carries riverine forest with an exceptional list.

  • Habitat: Mixed broad-leaved woodland over harder soils, granite koppies and low rocky hills, mopane woodland in the east, and riverine forest and thicket along the border river.
  • When: November to March for Thrush Nightingale and the migrant warblers in the riverine thickets. Boulder Chat calls all year and the sandgrouse come to water soon after dawn.
  • Getting there: Francistown is on the main tarred road north and has an airport. The Boulder Chat hills are watched from the public roads out of the city, and the Tuli Block reserves in the eastern corner are privately owned and arranged in advance.
  • Base yourself in: Francistown
  • All year: Boulder Chat, Mocking Cliff Chat, Meves's Starling, White-crowned Lapwing, Three-banded Courser, Pel's Fishing Owl, Burchell's Sandgrouse, Pink-backed Pelican
  • In the breeding season: White-fronted Plover, Collared Pratincole, Pink-backed Pelican
  • On passage: Thrush Nightingale, River Warbler, Garden Warbler
  • In winter: Boulder Chat, Meves's Starling, Burchell's Sandgrouse
  • National park: Khama Rhino Sanctuary
  • Stops:
    • : The second city, surrounded by mixed woodland, with Boulder Chat koppies on the roads north west and north east.
    • : A dam south west of the city. It holds a good variety of water birds and both pelicans occur on it.
    • : A large village on the road north, beside a community reserve that is the best place in the country for Burchell's Sandgrouse.
    • : The junction on the main road where the 65 km road west to the rhino sanctuary begins.
    • : The nearest town to the Tuli Block, in the eastern corner where three countries meet.
  • Also covers: Bobonong, Francistown, Khama Rhino Sanctuary, Palapye, Serowe, Shashe Dam
  • Also watch for: White Rhinoceros were reintroduced at the community reserve north of Serowe, and the eastern reserves hold Elephant and Leopard.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I bird here with limited mobility?

Often yes, and it varies by site. A good deal of the birding here happens from roadsides, hides and lodge grounds rather than on long walks, and some reserves keep flat, surfaced trails. Ask a lodge or reserve directly about step free access, surfaces and seating before you book. The Birdability Map records those details for birding sites, and their Access Considerations guide lists the questions worth asking. Coverage is thin outside the United States, so read a blank area as unrecorded rather than unsuitable.
