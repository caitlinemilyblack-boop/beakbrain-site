Getting in
Two airports take the flights, both from Quito and Guayaquil: Seymour (GPS) on Baltra for Santa Cruz and the cruise fleet, and San Cristobal (SCY) for the capital. There is no international service, so the mainland leg is compulsory.
Getting around
Boats reach almost every site and a licensed naturalist guide is required at all of them, with the islands any boat may visit fixed by the National Park itinerary its berth is licensed for; Santa Cruz, San Cristobal and Isabela have roads, hotels and public ferries between them, which is the one way to bird the archipelago without a cruise.

When to Go

The Galapagos has no off season and two very different ones. The warm months put the land birds into song and flatten the sea; the cool garua months fill the water with food and put the seabirds, penguins and cormorants to work. April and May sit between the two and carry both.

Warm

December to March

The warm season. Seas are calm, the water is at its clearest and warmest, and rain greens the arid zone so the land birds breed and sing. Waved Albatross are away at sea for all of it.

Go for Darwin's finches singing and nesting, land iguanas breeding from January, green turtles nesting, and the easiest crossings of the year for anyone who feels the swell.

Trade off Espanola loses its albatross entirely, and this is the hottest and most humid stretch on the trails.

Peak

April to June

The crossover, and the best weeks in the year for a birder. Albatross are back on Punta Suarez and courting, seas are still calm from the warm season, and the highlands have started to catch mist again.

Go for Waved Albatross courtship at Punta Suarez, Great Frigatebird males displaying on Genovesa from March, marine iguanas still breeding on Fernandina, and everything else present.

Trade off The most popular window, so berths on the smaller yachts go a year ahead.

Garua

July to November

The cool season, when the Humboldt current pushes cold water north and a low grey mist called the garua sits on the highlands. The sea is rougher and richer.

Go for Seabird activity at its highest, penguins and flightless cormorants at their most active in cold water, Blue-footed Booby nesting, sea lions pupping, and the highland finches working wet scalesia.

Trade off Crossings between islands are choppier, the water is cold enough to want a wetsuit, and the highlands can stay grey all day.

Two weeks is what it takes to reach every island that holds something found on no other. Genovesa, Espanola, Floreana, Isabela and San Cristobal each carry at least one bird the rest of the archipelago lacks, and no single loop touches more than three of them.

Santa Cruz and Puerto Ayora

The hub island, and the only ground in the archipelago where one road crosses every vegetation zone

The road up from Puerto Ayora leaves cactus and palo santo within twenty minutes, runs into the dripping scalesia forest at Los Gemelos, and comes out on the miconia and fern of Cerro Crocker. Woodpecker Finch and Vegetarian Finch hold the middle band, Galapagos Crake the top one, and the giant tortoises walk between the two.

  • Habitat: Arid palo santo and opuntia at the coast, endemic scalesia tree-daisy forest on the windward shoulder, and a miconia and fern-sedge summit at 864 m. Mangrove lagoons ring the north coast.
  • When: June to November, when the garua mist keeps the scalesia wet and the highland finches active. Giant tortoises are up in the highlands through the dry months and Galapagos Petrel return to burrows on Cerro Crocker at dusk.
  • Getting there: Seymour airport on Baltra, a bus to the Canal de Itabaca, the ferry, then 42 km of surfaced road to Puerto Ayora. Every highland site is a taxi ride from Bellavista or Santa Rosa and needs no guide.
  • Look for: Woodpecker Finch, Vegetarian Finch, Galapagos Crake, Galapagos Petrel, Vermilion Flycatcher, Paint-billed Crake, Galapagos Martin
  • Parks and reserves:
    • Reserva El Chato (protected area), The highland tortoise reserve south of Santa Rosa. Wild tortoises graze the pasture and the pools hold White-cheeked Pintail.
  • Stops:
    • : Twin collapsed magma chambers either side of the Baltra road, and the most accessible stand of endemic scalesia forest in the islands.
    • : The fern-sedge and miconia zone above Bellavista, on foot from the park boundary. It is the ground for Galapagos Crake.
    • : A network of mangrove lagoons on the north coast, entered by dinghy, with nesting herons and pelicans over the water.
    • : A white-sand beach on the north coast backed by a saltwater lagoon. Waders work the tideline and turtles nest in the dunes.
    • Cerro Dragon: The northwestern headland, with a shallow lagoon good for flamingos and a low hill holding reintroduced land iguanas.
    • : A paved 2.5 km path from the edge of Puerto Ayora through arid scrub to a beach and a mangrove lagoon. It is walkable without a guide.
    • : The channel crossing between Baltra and Santa Cruz. The ferry queue is a seabird and finch watchpoint.
  • Visitor centres: Charles Darwin Research Station
  • Also covers: Bellavista, Caleta Tortuga Negra, Canal de Itabaca, Cerro Dragon, Charles Darwin Research Station, Isla Baltra, Isla Santa Cruz, Las Bachas, Los Gemelos, Media Luna and Cerro Crocker, Puerto Ayora, Reserva El Chato, Santa Rosa, Tortuga Bay
  • Also watch for: Giant tortoises of the Santa Cruz population range across the farmland and the reserve above Santa Rosa, and the lava tubes near Bellavista hold roosting bats. Green turtles mate in Caleta Tortuga Negra, and golden and spotted eagle rays school through the same lagoons.

North Seymour and Daphne Major

Two uplifted slabs of sea floor north of Baltra, flat, low and thick with nesting seabirds

North Seymour is the first landing on most cruises and the easiest colony in the archipelago to walk. A level loop runs through saltbush, with Blue-footed Booby nesting on open ground and both frigatebirds in the bushes on either side. Daphne Major is the tuff cone on the horizon. It is closed to visitors, and it is the island where four species of Darwin's finch were measured bird by bird for thirty years.

  • Habitat: Low uplifted lava plateau under 30 m, carpeted in saltbush and muyuyo, with dwarf palo santo and a prostrate endemic opuntia that grows low because no tortoise or land iguana evolved here to browse it.
  • When: Frigatebirds and Swallow-tailed Gull breed year round. Blue-footed Booby nest mainly in the garua season, June to November.
  • Getting there: Dry landing 40 km north of Puerto Ayora, on almost every cruise's first or last day. Daphne Major has a dangerous landing and access is restricted.
  • Look for: Blue-footed Booby, Magnificent Frigatebird, Great Frigatebird, Swallow-tailed Gull, Brown Noddy, Medium Ground Finch, Galapagos Shearwater
  • Parks and reserves: Parque Nacional Galápagos (national park)
  • Stops:
    • : A flat uplifted plateau with a 2 km loop trail through the frigatebird and booby colonies, and the standard first landing of a cruise.
    • : A 120 m tuff cone with a crater floor full of nesting Blue-footed Booby. Landings are restricted, so it is viewed from the water.
    • : A sand spit between Baltra and North Seymour, a sea lion haul-out and a roost for waders and gulls.
  • Also covers: Isla Daphne Mayor, Isla Mosquera, Isla Seymour Norte, Parque Nacional Galápagos
  • Also watch for: Galapagos sea lions lie across the landing dock, and the land iguanas here descend from animals carried over from Baltra in the 1930s, which is the reason the species survived when Baltra's own population was wiped out.

South Plaza and Santa Fe

Two small eastern islands where land iguanas hold territories under giant opuntias

South Plaza is a kilometre long and a hundred metres wide, tilted so that one short loop carries you from a sea lion channel up to a clifftop where Red-billed Tropicbird display at eye level. Santa Fe, an hour south, grows the tallest opuntias in the archipelago and has its own land iguana holding territory under them.

  • Habitat: Uplifted sea floor rather than volcanic cone, with sea purslane matting the flats, giant opuntia above, and 25 m cliffs on the windward southern side.
  • When: Year round. Swallow-tailed Gull breed on a nine-month cycle rather than an annual one, so there are eggs or young in every month.
  • Getting there: Both are dinghy landings on cruise itineraries out of Puerto Ayora. South Plaza is a dry landing at the channel; Santa Fe is a wet landing onto the beach at Bahia Barrington.
  • Look for: Common Cactus Finch, Swallow-tailed Gull, Red-billed Tropicbird, Galapagos Hawk, Galapagos Shearwater, Elliot's Storm Petrel, Galapagos Dove
  • Parks and reserves: Parque Nacional Galápagos (national park)
  • Stops:
    • : A tilted islet with a 1 km clifftop loop. Land iguanas are dense under the opuntias, and a bachelor sea lion colony holds the far end.
    • : The sheltered anchorage on Santa Fe's northeast coast, with two sea lion beaches and a breakwater of islets.
    • : An 800 m track climbing from the beach into giant prickly pear with trunks 2.5 m round. It is the habitat of the Santa Fe land iguana.
  • Also covers: Bahia Barrington, Isla Plaza Sur, Isla Santa Fe opuntia forest, Parque Nacional Galápagos
  • Also watch for: South Plaza carries around a thousand Galapagos sea lions and the territorial bulls make swimming there a poor idea. Santa Fe has both an endemic land iguana and an endemic rice rat, which survived because the black rat never landed.

Santiago, Rabida and Bartolome

The central lava islands, where fur seals lie in collapsed grottos and penguins fish under Pinnacle Rock

Puerto Egas on Santiago is the dependable place to see Galapagos fur seal, which rest under shaded overhangs above rock pools that Lava Heron and Yellow-crowned Night Heron work through the day. Rabida's red beach backs onto a brackish lagoon holding flamingos, and the penguins at Bartolome swim at the foot of the rock.

  • Habitat: Bare pahoehoe and aa lava on the youngest ground, arid palo santo and croton scrub on the older, and small brackish lagoons behind the beaches.
  • When: Waders are best at Playa Espumilla November to March, and green turtles nest through the wet months. Brown Pelican nest at Rabida mainly in the garua season.
  • Getting there: Wet landings at every site, 60 to 85 km northwest of Puerto Ayora, on the standard northern and western cruise loops.
  • Look for: Galapagos Penguin, Lava Heron, Yellow-crowned Night Heron, American Oystercatcher, American Flamingo, Galapagos Dove, Galapagos Hawk
  • Parks and reserves: Parque Nacional Galápagos (national park)
  • Stops:
    • : A black-sand bay on Santiago's west coast with tidal rock platforms and lava grottos. It is the archipelago's most reliable fur seal site.
    • : A golden beach on James Bay backed by brackish mangrove lagoons, and the best wader ground on the island.
    • : The edge of a lava flow that erupted in 1897. Walk it for its pahoehoe rather than its birds, with penguins at the landing beach.
    • : Rabida's red cinder beach, backed by a mangrove-fringed lagoon that holds a small flock of American Flamingo.
    • : An eroded tuff cone leaning into Bartolome's northern bay, with penguins on the rocks at its foot.
    • : A boardwalk climbing 114 m through spatter cones and lava tubes to the archipelago's most photographed viewpoint.
  • Also covers: Bahia Sullivan, Bartolome summit trail, Isla Bartolome, Isla Rabida, Isla Santiago, Parque Nacional Galápagos, Pinnacle Rock, Playa Espumilla, Playa Rojo, Puerto Egas
  • Also watch for: Galapagos fur seal lie up in the collapsed lava tubes at Puerto Egas through the daylight hours, which is the one place in the archipelago they are straightforward. Bottlenose dolphin are regular off Rabida and short-finned pilot whale pass through the same water.

Isabela

The largest island in the archipelago, six volcanoes joined into one, and the only ground holding Mangrove Finch

Isabela runs 130 km north to south and each volcano carries its own giant tortoise. The west coast is the productive side: flightless cormorants nest at Punta Moreno and Tagus Cove, tortoise females walk down to Bahia Urvina to lay in the wet season, and the closed mangrove stands north of Tagus Cove hold the whole world population of Mangrove Finch, which numbers around a hundred birds.

  • Habitat: Six shield volcanoes rising to 1,689 m and joined by their own lava, with mangrove-fringed lagoons along the west coast and brackish pools set in raw lava fields at Punta Moreno.
  • When: Female giant tortoises descend to Bahia Urvina December through March, and land iguanas begin breeding in January. Waders are best at Puerto Villamil November through March.
  • Getting there: Puerto Villamil has an airstrip and hotels and sits 90 km southwest of Puerto Ayora. West coast sites are reached only by boat, with dry landings at Tagus Cove and Punta Moreno and wet landings at Bahia Urvina.
  • Look for: Mangrove Finch, American Flamingo, Flightless Cormorant, Galapagos Penguin, Galapagos Martin, Woodpecker Finch, White-cheeked Pintail
  • Parks and reserves: Parque Nacional Galápagos (national park)
  • Stops:
    • : A sheltered anchorage between two tuff craters. Whalers used it for three centuries, and a boardwalk runs to the rim of the brackish Lago Darwin.
    • : A strip of black sand on a coastline uplifted 5 m in 1954, which stranded coral a kilometre inland. It is the nesting ground for Alcedo's tortoises.
    • Punta Moreno: A vast lava field on the southwest coast, punctuated by water-filled depressions that draw flamingos, pintails and martins.
    • : A mangrove lagoon system with no landing. Work it by dinghy for penguins and cormorants at the narrows.
    • : The island's small southern town. Brackish lagoons west of it are among the best flamingo ground in the archipelago.
    • : The one active volcano visitors may climb, from Santo Tomas above Puerto Villamil. Its caldera is 10 km across.
    • : The eroded half-cone of Volcan Ecuador at the island's northwestern tip. Hundreds of Blue-footed Booby nest on the cliffs.
  • Also covers: Bahia Elizabeth, Bahia Urvina, Isla Isabela, Parque Nacional Galápagos, Puerto Villamil, Punta Moreno, Punta Vicente Roca, Sierra Negra, Tagus Cove
  • Also watch for: Volcan Alcedo holds the largest giant tortoise population in the archipelago. The Canal Bolivar off the west coast is the best cetacean water in the islands, with Bryde's whale the commonest large species and killer whale reasonably reliable.

Fernandina and the Canal Bolivar

The youngest and most volcanically active island, and the world's largest colony of marine iguanas

Punta Espinosa is one landing on one point and it carries flightless cormorants nesting at the lava's edge, penguins loafing on the rock platforms and hundreds of marine iguanas lined up in the morning sun. The channel you cross to reach it is the best whale water in the archipelago.

  • Habitat: Recent pahoehoe and aa lava from a shield volcano that erupts on average every five years, with mangrove stands and tidal pools at the single point where anything grows.
  • When: Marine iguanas breed January through June and flightless cormorants breed year round. Waved Albatross cross the Canal Bolivar April to December.
  • Getting there: Dry landing at Punta Espinosa, wet at low tide, 275 km northwest of Puerto Ayora. Western-loop cruises only, and this one point is the only place ashore on the island.
  • Look for: Flightless Cormorant, Galapagos Penguin, Galapagos Hawk, Wedge-rumped Storm Petrel, Elliot's Storm Petrel, Lava Heron, Galapagos Petrel
  • Parks and reserves: Parque Nacional Galápagos (national park)
  • Stops:
    • : A jagged lava promontory on the northeast coast and the island's only visitor site, with cormorant nests at the northern tip.
    • : Mangrove-fringed tidal pools south of Punta Espinosa. Work them by dinghy for turtles, rays and herons.
    • : The channel between Fernandina and Isabela. Every western loop crosses it, and it is the archipelago's most productive cetacean water.
  • Also covers: Canal Bolivar, Isla Fernandina, Parque Nacional Galápagos, Punta Espinosa, Punta Mangle
  • Also watch for: Bryde's whale and killer whale in the Canal Bolivar, with bottlenose dolphin on most crossings and whale shark at the channel's southern end. The marine iguanas here are the largest subspecies, males reaching 1.2 m.

San Cristobal

The eastern island and the seat of the provincial capital, holding the only permanent fresh water in the archipelago

El Junco is a crater lake 700 m up and the only standing fresh water in the islands, which is why White-cheeked Pintail, Common Gallinule and Pied-billed Grebe are there and scarce everywhere else. Punta Pitt at the northeastern tip is one of two sites in the archipelago where all three boobies nest side by side.

  • Habitat: An old eroded shield rises to 730 m. It carries a freshwater crater lake, a remnant of miconia where guava has not taken it, and arid palo santo and cactus down to the coast.
  • When: Red-footed Booby breed year round at Punta Pitt and sea lions pup through the garua season, June to November. The lake holds water in every month.
  • Getting there: San Cristobal airport (SCY) at Puerto Baquerizo Moreno takes direct flights from Quito and Guayaquil. El Junco is a road trip from town; Punta Pitt and Isla Lobos are boat trips.
  • Look for: San Cristobal Mockingbird, Red-footed Booby, Nazca Booby, Blue-footed Booby, White-cheeked Pintail, Pied-billed Grebe, Wedge-rumped Storm Petrel
  • Parks and reserves: Parque Nacional Galápagos (national park)
  • Stops:
    • : A crater lake at 700 m and the archipelago's only permanent fresh water. Frigatebirds come in to bathe.
    • : An eroded cone at the northeastern tip with a green olivine beach. It is the one easy site for nesting Red-footed Booby outside Genovesa.
    • : A low islet across a narrow channel from the north coast, thick with sea lions and nesting Blue-footed Booby.
    • : A 140 m eroded tuff cone standing offshore, circled by boobies, frigatebirds and tropicbirds.
    • : The tortoise ground in the island's dry southeast, on a long hot trail from a nesting beach.
    • : Frigatebird Hill, a 2 km round walk from the interpretation centre. Both frigatebirds thermal off the cliff.
  • Visitor centres: Galapagos National Park Interpretation Centre, Puerto Baquerizo Moreno
  • Also covers: Cerro de las Tijeretas, El Junco, Galapagos National Park Interpretation Centre, Puerto Baquerizo Moreno, Isla Lobos, Isla San Cristobal, La Galapaguera, Parque Nacional Galápagos, Punta Pitt, Roca Leon Dormido
  • Also watch for: About a thousand San Cristobal giant tortoises survive in the highlands and are seen most reliably around Cerro Medialuna at La Galapaguera. Isla Lobos in the channel is the best place in the islands to watch marine iguanas feeding underwater.

Espanola

The oldest and southernmost island, and the world's breeding colony of Waved Albatross

Punta Suarez is the finest seabird walk in the archipelago. The trail passes Nazca Booby nesting on the low cliffs, then a blowhole throwing spray 25 m, then the albatross colony, where birds waddle to the edge of the precipice and launch themselves off it. Bahia Gardner on the sheltered north coast is two kilometres of white coral sand and sleeping sea lions.

  • Habitat: Uplifted lava tilting from 100 m cliffs on the windward southern coast to a low sheltered north shore, with waist-high saltbush over most of the surface.
  • When: Waved Albatross are at Punta Suarez late March until late December and absent January through March. Green turtles nest at Bahia Gardner January through March.
  • Getting there: Dry landing at Punta Suarez, wet at Bahia Gardner, 90 km southeast of Puerto Ayora, and the longest single run on a southern loop.
  • Look for: Waved Albatross, Espanola Mockingbird, Espanola Cactus Finch, Nazca Booby, Red-billed Tropicbird, Galapagos Hawk, Galapagos Dove
  • Parks and reserves: Parque Nacional Galápagos (national park)
  • Stops:
    • : A cliff-edge trail past booby colonies and a blowhole to the world's Waved Albatross colony. It is the best seabird walk in the islands.
    • : A 2 km white coral beach on the north coast, one of the longest in the archipelago. The scrub behind it holds Espanola Cactus Finch.
  • Also covers: Bahia Gardner, Isla Espanola, Parque Nacional Galápagos, Punta Suarez
  • Also watch for: The Espanola marine iguanas are the archipelago's most colourful, and they turn pink and turquoise in the breeding season. The island's giant tortoise population fell to three males and twelve females and now exceeds a thousand. Every one of them was bred at the research station in Puerto Ayora and carried back here.

Floreana

The southern settled island, and the only ground on earth holding Medium Tree Finch

Medium Tree Finch lives in the scalesia remnant on Cerro Pajas and nowhere else. The lagoon behind Punta Cormorant carries up to 150 American Flamingo, which is the largest flock in the archipelago, and the two offshore islets of Champion and Gardner hold the last Floreana Mockingbird after the species was lost from the main island.

  • Habitat: An extinct volcano rising to 640 m with a small scalesia and agricultural highland on its shoulder, and a mangrove-fringed brackish lagoon behind the northern coast.
  • When: Flamingos breed July through March. Galapagos Petrel are on Cerro Pajas all year but only come to the burrows at dusk, and the tree finch may drop to the lowlands in the dry months.
  • Getting there: Wet landings at every site except Puerto Velasco Ibarra, 55 km south of Puerto Ayora. The highland finch wants a walk up from the settlement at first light, so it needs an overnight rather than a cruise stop.
  • Look for: Medium Tree Finch, Floreana Mockingbird, American Flamingo, Galapagos Petrel, Galapagos Penguin, Small Tree Finch, White-cheeked Pintail
  • Parks and reserves: Parque Nacional Galápagos (national park)
  • Stops:
    • : A green olivine landing beach with a mangrove lagoon behind it, and the archipelago's most reliable flamingo site.
    • : Devil's Crown, a drowned cinder cone whose eroded rim breaks the surface. It is snorkelled rather than landed on.
    • : The highland scalesia remnant above Puerto Velasco Ibarra. It is the world range of Medium Tree Finch and holds a Galapagos Petrel colony.
    • : The barrel left by British whalers in 1793 and used as an unstamped post box ever since. A lagoon lies east of the beach.
    • : The island's small settlement on the west coast, and the only base from which the highland finch is walkable at dawn.
  • Also covers: Cerro Pajas, Corona del Diablo, Isla Floreana, Parque Nacional Galápagos, Post Office Bay, Puerto Velasco Ibarra, Punta Cormorant
  • Also watch for: Devil's Crown, the sunken cinder cone off the northern point, holds some fifty fish species and the best snorkelling in the archipelago, with hammerheads outside the caldera wall and marbled rays inside it.

Genovesa

The only island north of the equator open to visitors, and the world's largest colony of Red-footed Booby

Genovesa is the rim of a sunken crater, and every landing is on its inner wall. Some 140,000 pairs of Red-footed Booby nest in the palo santo, 200,000 pairs of Wedge-rumped Storm Petrel nest in the broken lava above Prince Philip's Steps, and Short-eared Owl hunt them there in daylight, which they do nowhere else on earth.

  • Habitat: A low drowned caldera under 65 m, with palo santo and muyuyo scrub inside the rim and a bare field of broken lava at the top of the eastern arm.
  • When: Male Great Frigatebird display March through June. Red-footed Booby breed year round, and the wet season puts a green flush through the deciduous scrub.
  • Getting there: Wet landing at Bahia Darwin and a dry landing up Prince Philip's Steps, 125 km north of Puerto Ayora. It is an overnight crossing, so only the longer cruises reach it.
  • Look for: Red-footed Booby, Genovesa Cactus Finch, Sharp-beaked Ground Finch, Wedge-rumped Storm Petrel, Great Frigatebird, Short-eared Owl, Large Ground Finch
  • Parks and reserves: Parque Nacional Galápagos (national park)
  • Stops:
    • : The flooded caldera that forms a natural harbour 2.5 km wide, with a coral beach and mangrove lagoons at its northern edge.
    • : A natural rockfall staircase up a 25 m cliff to a palo santo forest full of Red-footed Booby, with a storm-petrel field beyond it.
  • Also covers: Bahia Darwin, Isla Genovesa, Parque Nacional Galápagos, Prince Philip's Steps
  • Also watch for: Genovesa is young enough that giant tortoises, land iguanas, snakes and lava lizards never arrived, and its marine iguanas are the smallest subspecies in the archipelago. Galapagos fur seal are straightforward on the cliffs below the steps.

Frequently Asked Questions

When is the best time to go birding in the Galapagos?

April to June. Waved Albatross are back on Espanola and courting, the seas are still calm from the warm season, and every land bird is in breeding condition. If seabirds and cold-water species are the point, come July to November instead, when the Humboldt current is running and the penguins and flightless cormorants are most active. January to March is the one window to avoid if the albatross matters: the whole colony is at sea.

How many bird species does the Galapagos have?

About 180 have been recorded, which is a short list, and that is the point. Only around 30 are resident land birds, and more than half of those live here and nowhere else. A birder finishes the archipelago's endemics in two weeks, which is impossible in any mainland country.

Can I bird the Galapagos without a cruise?

Yes, on three islands. Santa Cruz, San Cristobal and Isabela have towns, hotels and public ferries between them, and the Santa Cruz highlands are the best land-bird ground in the archipelago. That covers the tree finches, the crake, the tortoises and the freshwater lake. Everything else needs a licensed boat, Espanola's albatross and Genovesa's boobies among it.

Do I need a guide?

At every National Park visitor site, yes: a licensed naturalist guide is required and the group size is capped. The exception is the settled area, where the Santa Cruz highland sites, Tortuga Bay and the town lagoons can be walked alone. A birding guide is still worth hiring there, because the tree finches are the hard part.

Where do I see a Waved Albatross?

Punta Suarez on Espanola, which holds effectively the world's entire breeding population. Birds are ashore from late March until late December and the trail runs along the edge of the colony. They are absent January through March.

Which island has the most endemic birds?

Santa Cruz has the longest list, about 85 species, because it carries every vegetation zone from arid coast to a fern-sedge summit. For birds found on one island alone, Genovesa, Espanola, Floreana and San Cristobal each hold something the rest of the archipelago lacks: Genovesa Cactus Finch, Espanola Cactus Finch and Espanola Mockingbird, Medium Tree Finch and Floreana Mockingbird, and San Cristobal Mockingbird.

Is Darwin's finch one bird or several?

Eighteen species across the archipelago and Cocos Island, descended from one colonising ancestor. They separate on bill shape rather than plumage, which is why they are difficult in the field and why the ground finches on Daphne Major have been measured bird by bird for over thirty years.

How much does the National Park cost?

There is an entrance fee paid on arrival and a transit control card bought at the mainland airport, both set by the Ecuadorian government and both revised periodically. The Galapagos National Park Directorate publishes the current figures, and they are worth checking close to travel rather than at booking.

Can I bird here with limited mobility?

Sites on this page differ from each other more than any rating could carry, so this is the framework the standard British guides use, and the reserve's own page and warden hold the answers.

  • Permission · some reserves open ground to disabled visitors that is closed to everyone else, and it is granted by asking.
  • Priority · parking bays, hides and the nearest paths are often held for disabled visitors. Ask what is reserved and how to claim it.
  • Parking · how close a car reaches the birds shapes the whole day. Ring ahead for the nearest hard standing to the water or the hide.
  • Paths · surfaces, gradients and gate widths vary inside a single reserve. Ask which trail is boarded, surfaced or level.
  • Perches · a hide with a low window, or a bench where the view opens, turns a viewpoint into a morning.

Every reserve above with its own link carries its access detail there, and a warden answers what a page cannot.
