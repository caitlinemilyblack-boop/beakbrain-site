Birding in North Carolina

North Carolina runs from the highest ground in the eastern United States to a barrier island that sits closest to the Gulf Stream, which gives one state a spruce-fir bird list and the best pelagic trips in the country.

Getting in
Raleigh-Durham (RDU) for the coastal plain and Charlotte (CLT) or Asheville (AVL) for the mountains. Norfolk in Virginia is the closest airport to the northern Outer Banks
Getting around
A hire car throughout. Highway 12 runs the Outer Banks with free state ferries, and Cape Lookout is passenger ferry only

Birds that decide the trip

Black-capped Chickadee in the spruce-fir

The southern Appalachians hold an isolated population at the top of the mountains, with Carolina Chickadee in the valleys below. The two meet in a narrow band and the elevation decides which one is singing.

When Late May to July, above about five thousand feet.

Where The southern Appalachians

Red-cockaded Woodpecker in the longleaf

A woodpecker that excavates in living pine and needs fire to keep its stand open. The Sandhills at Fort Bragg carry one of the largest populations left, and the cavity trees are ringed with white paint.

When April to June, at first light around the cavity clusters.

Where The coastal plain forests

Tundra Swan on Lake Mattamuskeet

Most of the eastern population winters on a handful of North Carolina lakes, and Mattamuskeet is the biggest of them. The birds feed on the surrounding farmland by day and return to the shallow water in numbers that fill the horizon.

When December to February, from the causeway at either end of the day.

Where Mattamuskeet and the pocosin lakes

Black-capped Petrel off Hatteras

It breeds on a few Caribbean cliffs and feeds along the Gulf Stream edge, which runs closer to Cape Hatteras than to anywhere else on the eastern seaboard. A day boat out of the village reaches it and turns back the same afternoon.

When Late May to August, on the day trips from Hatteras village.

Where The Outer Banks

Wood Stork on the coastal plain

A stork that follows falling water, and North Carolina sits at the northern edge of its range. Late summer birds move up from the south into the impoundments and the flooded pine flats.

When July to September, on the drying impoundments.

Where The coastal plain forests

American Oystercatcher on the Cape Lookout flats

The undisturbed sand of Cape Lookout carries breeding oystercatchers, and the passenger-ferry access is exactly why they are there rather than on the busier seashore beaches.

When April to July, on the sound-side flats.

Where The Outer Banks

Swainson's Warbler in the cane

A warbler that is heard long before it is seen, in dense cane and rhododendron on wet ground. The Roanoke floodplain and the lower Smokies slopes both hold it.

When May and June, from thick cover at dawn.

Where The coastal plain forests, The southern Appalachians

Snow Goose over Pocosin Lakes

Pungo Lake takes a winter flock that lifts off the water in one sheet at first light and drops onto the surrounding farmland. It is the loudest thing in eastern North Carolina in January.

When December to February, at first light over Pungo.

Where Mattamuskeet and the pocosin lakes

When to Go

The state works in two halves that peak at opposite ends of the year. The mountains are a June proposition and the eastern refuges are a January one, and the pelagic season sits across the summer between them.

The waterfowl months

December to February

Mattamuskeet holds tens of thousands of Tundra Swans and Snow Geese, and the coast is at its emptiest.

Go for Mattamuskeet and Pocosin Lakes for the swans, then the Outer Banks for wintering seabirds and gannets offshore.

Trade off The mountains are iced and the high roads are shut.

Typical daily average

Spring passage

April and May

Migrants move up the coastal plain and the bottomland warblers arrive on the Roanoke.

Go for Croatan and the Roanoke floodplain, then the first mountain arrivals by late May.

Trade off The high spruce is not yet in full song and the pelagic season has barely opened.

Typical daily average

The mountain season

Late May to July

The spruce-fir breeders are singing above five thousand feet, which is the state at its most distinctive.

Go for The Smokies and the Blue Ridge Parkway, with Hatteras pelagics on the same trip.

Trade off It is hot and humid in the east and the refuges are quiet.

Typical daily average

Storms and seabirds

August to October

Hurricanes push seabirds inshore and the shorebird passage runs down the banks.

Go for Cape Hatteras and Pea Island, and Cape Lookout for the flats.

Trade off Storms close Highway 12 without notice and trips cancel.

The Outer Banks

A barrier island chain that reaches further into the Atlantic than anywhere else on the coast

The Outer Banks bow forty kilometres out from the North Carolina mainland, which puts the Gulf Stream closer to shore here than anywhere on the rest of the eastern seaboard. That is why the pelagic trips out of Hatteras are the best in the country, and why storms drop seabirds on the beach.

  • Habitat: Barrier island beach, dune and overwash flat, with salt marsh and sound behind, impounded freshwater ponds on Pea Island, and a maritime forest of live oak in the dune hollows at Nags Head.
  • When: November to March for the wintering waterfowl and swans, and August to October for storm-driven seabirds. The offshore trips run from late spring through autumn.
  • Getting there: Norfolk or Raleigh are the airports and Buxton is the base. Highway 12 runs the length of the banks with a free ferry to Ocracoke, and Cape Lookout is reached by passenger ferry only.
  • Base yourself in: Buxton
  • Look for: Piping Plover, Wilson's Plover, American Oystercatcher, Black Skimmer, Least Tern, Snow Goose, Tundra Swan, Northern Gannet, Red Knot, Marbled Godwit, Seaside Sparrow, Brown Pelican
  • Parks and reserves: Cape Hatteras National Seashore (National site), Cape Lookout National Seashore (National site), Pea Island National Wildlife Refuge, Nags Head Woods Preserve, Cedar Island National Wildlife Reserve
  • Also covers: Cape Hatteras National Seashore, Cape Lookout National Seashore, Cedar Island National Wildlife Reserve, Nags Head Woods Preserve, Pea Island National Wildlife Refuge
  • Also watch for: Wild horses hold the northern banks and loggerhead turtles nest along the seashore beaches.

Mattamuskeet and the pocosin lakes

Tundra Swans in tens of thousands, and red wolves in the pocosin behind them

Lake Mattamuskeet is very large and very shallow, and it holds one of the great winter waterfowl concentrations of the Atlantic coast. The pocosin peatland behind it is where the red wolf was returned to the wild.

  • Habitat: A very large shallow lake ringed with marsh and cypress, with pocosin peatland, farmed impoundments and pine flatwoods behind it.
  • When: December to February for the swan and goose concentration, which is at its peak after the first hard weather.
  • Getting there: Engelhard is the base, two hours east of Greenville. Mattamuskeet has a causeway across the lake and a refuge road, and Alligator River is entered from Highway 64 on the way to the banks.
  • Base yourself in: Engelhard
  • Look for: Tundra Swan, Snow Goose, Northern Pintail, American Wigeon, Sandhill Crane, Wood Stork, Black Rail, King Rail, Prothonotary Warbler, Swainson's Warbler, Red-cockaded Woodpecker, Bald Eagle
  • Parks and reserves: Alligator River National Wildlife Refuge, Mattamuskeet National Wildlife Refuge, Pocosin Lakes National Wildlife Refuge, Swanquarter National Wildlife Refuge, Swanquarter Wilderness (Protected area)
  • Also covers: Alligator River National Wildlife Refuge, Mattamuskeet National Wildlife Refuge, Pocosin Lakes National Wildlife Refuge, Swanquarter National Wildlife Refuge, Swanquarter Wilderness
  • Also watch for: The red wolf was reintroduced to Alligator River and the population there is the only wild one on earth.

The coastal plain forests

Longleaf pine and pocosin, with one of the largest red-cockaded populations on the east coast

The Sandhills at Fort Bragg and the longleaf on Croatan carry red-cockaded woodpeckers in numbers, and the Roanoke floodplain behind them is bottomland hardwood that floods every spring. The Great Dismal Swamp is peat under maple and gum on the Virginia line.

  • Habitat: Open longleaf pine savannah on sandhills, kept by fire, with pocosin, bottomland hardwood on the Roanoke floodplain and peat swamp of maple and gum in the Great Dismal.
  • When: April to June for the breeding warblers and the woodpeckers at dawn. The Roanoke floodplain is often under water in spring, which is the point of it.
  • Getting there: Raleigh is the airport. Fort Bragg is an active army installation and needs a recreation permit and a check at the range control office. Croatan is entered from Highway 70 near New Bern.
  • Base yourself in: New Bern
  • Look for: Red-cockaded Woodpecker, Bachman's Sparrow, Brown-headed Nuthatch, Swainson's Warbler, Prothonotary Warbler, Kentucky Warbler, Hooded Warbler, Wood Thrush, Barred Owl, Chuck-will's-widow, Anhinga, Swallow-tailed Kite
  • Parks and reserves: Croatan National Forest (Forest or grassland), Roanoke River National Wildlife Refuge, Great Dismal Swamp National Wildlife Refuge, Dismal Swamp State Park (State or provincial park), Roanoke River Region (Protected area)
  • Also covers: Croatan National Forest, Dismal Swamp State Park, Fort Bragg, Great Dismal Swamp National Wildlife Refuge, Roanoke River National Wildlife Refuge, Roanoke River Region
  • Also watch for: Black bear are at high density in the pocosin and the Great Dismal peat is deep enough to burn underground.

The southern Appalachians

Spruce and fir at six thousand feet, and a northern bird list on a North Carolina mountain

The highest ground in the eastern United States sits on the North Carolina line, and above about five thousand feet it carries red spruce and Fraser fir with the birds that go with them. Walking up a Smokies trail moves through the equivalent of a thousand miles of latitude.

  • Habitat: Red spruce and Fraser fir above about five thousand feet, with northern hardwood, cove forest of tulip poplar and hemlock below, and heath bald on the exposed ridges.
  • When: Late May to July, when the high-elevation breeders are singing. The park roads are open all year but the high gaps close in ice.
  • Getting there: Asheville is the airport and Waynesville the base. The Blue Ridge Parkway runs the ridge with numerous pull-ins, and Clingmans Dome road climbs to the spruce-fir but shuts in winter.
  • Base yourself in: Waynesville
  • Look for: Black-capped Chickadee, Red Crossbill, Golden-crowned Kinglet, Blackburnian Warbler, Canada Warbler, Chestnut-sided Warbler, Black-throated Blue Warbler, Veery, Northern Saw-whet Owl, Ruffed Grouse, Winter Wren, Northern Raven
  • Parks and reserves: Pisgah National Forest (Forest or grassland), Nantahala National Forest (Forest or grassland), Great Smoky Mountains National Park, Blue Ridge Parkway (Protected area), Needmore Gameland (Protected area)
  • Also covers: Blue Ridge Parkway, Great Smoky Mountains National Park, Nantahala National Forest, Needmore Gameland, Pisgah National Forest
  • Also watch for: Elk were returned to Cataloochee in 2001 and black bear are at one of the highest densities in the country.

Frequently Asked Questions

When is the best time to go birding in North Carolina?

It depends which half of the state. Late May to early July for the spruce-fir breeders in the Smokies and for the Hatteras pelagic season, and December to February for the Tundra Swan and Snow Goose concentration at Mattamuskeet. April and May work everywhere at once but neither peak is at its best.

Why are the Hatteras pelagic trips considered the best in the country?

The Outer Banks reach further into the Atlantic than the rest of the eastern seaboard, so the Gulf Stream edge sits within a morning of the harbour. A day boat from Hatteras village crosses it and comes back the same afternoon, which takes two days from most other ports.

Can I really see northern birds on a southern mountain?

Yes. Above about five thousand feet the southern Appalachians of North Carolina carry red spruce and Fraser fir, and with them Black-capped Chickadee, Red Crossbill, Winter Wren, Northern Saw-whet Owl and Canada Warbler. Walking up a Smokies trail passes through roughly the same change you would drive a thousand miles north to find.

Do I need a permit for Fort Bragg?

Yes. It is an active army installation, and birding the Sandhills there needs a recreation permit and a check with range control on the day, because live-fire areas close without notice.

How do I reach Cape Lookout?

By passenger ferry only, from Harkers Island or Beaufort. There is no road and no vehicle crossing for the ordinary visitor, which is why the flats there hold shorebirds that the busier seashore does not.

Can I bird here with limited mobility?

Sites on this page differ from each other more than any rating could carry, so this is the framework the standard British guides use, and the reserve's own page and warden hold the answers.

  • Permission · some reserves open ground to disabled visitors that is closed to everyone else, and it is granted by asking.
  • Priority · parking bays, hides and the nearest paths are often held for disabled visitors. Ask what is reserved and how to claim it.
  • Parking · how close a car reaches the birds shapes the whole day. Ring ahead for the nearest hard standing to the water or the hide.
  • Paths · surfaces, gradients and gate widths vary inside a single reserve. Ask which trail is boarded, surfaced or level.
  • Perches · a hide with a low window, or a bench where the view opens, turns a viewpoint into a morning.

Every reserve above with its own link carries its access detail there, and a warden answers what a page cannot.
