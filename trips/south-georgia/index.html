When to Go

Early season

October and November

Southern elephant seals fill the beaches to breed and the king chicks from last season still stand among them. Snow lies low on the tussock and the light is long.

Go for St Andrews Bay and Royal Bay for the elephant seals, and Grytviken as soon as the ice clears the bay.

Trade off Fur seals take over the beaches from late November and close several landings. Prion Island shuts at the peak of their breeding season.

Peak season

December and January

Snowy Albatrosses sit on their nests on Prion Island, Light-mantled Albatrosses display along the cliffs, and the king colonies run at full noise.

Go for Prion Island for the albatrosses, Salisbury Plain and St Andrews Bay for the kings, and Cooper Bay for the chinstraps.

Trade off Fur seals hold every beach and some landings are cancelled outright. This is also the half of the season that books first.

Late season

February and March

Fur seal pups swim in the shallows, king chicks stand brown and half grown, and adult penguins crowd ashore to moult.

Go for Gold Harbour and Salisbury Plain for the moult, and the fjords in the southeast for prions and Snow Petrels.

Trade off The weather turns earlier and the last voyages of the season lose landings to it.

The closed half

April to September

The sea ice and the winter storms end the shipping season, and the birds that stay move offshore.

Go for Nothing sails, and the island keeps a few dozen people on it at King Edward Point.

The Southern Ocean Circuit

The long route calls at the Falkland Islands on the way out and gives South Georgia eight or nine days at the far end. The extra time buys the landings that weather takes from a shorter voyage.

Grytviken and Cumberland Bay

The old whaling station, the museum and the island's tamest birds

Almost every ship calls first at the head of Cumberland East Bay, where the buildings of a whaling station still stand. Snowy Sheathbills walk between the oil tanks, Gentoo Penguins nest on the flats behind the beach, and the pools above the settlement hold the island's own pintail. Whaling ended here in 1965 and the ruins it left are the easiest place on the island to watch birds at close range.

  • Habitat: Tussock grass and glacial moraine around a deep sheltered bay, with the ruins of a shore station and freshwater pools behind the beach.
  • When: November to March, which is the only stretch of the year that ships reach the island and the low ground is clear of snow.
  • Getting there: Ships anchor in Cumberland East Bay and put passengers ashore by inflatable boat at the station and at King Edward Point. The ground behind the beach is flat and the walking is easy, and the path over the ridge to Maiviken takes about two hours each way. A landing permit is arranged through the expedition operator before the voyage sails.
  • Look for: Snowy Sheathbill, Gentoo Penguin, Yellow-billed Pintail, South Georgia Pipit, Imperial Shag, Kelp Gull, Antarctic Tern, Brown Skua
  • Stops:
    • : The whaling station, the museum and the whalers' cemetery where Ernest Shackleton is buried. Snowy Sheathbills hold the ground between the buildings and fur seals lie in the doorways.
    • : The research station on the spit across the cove. Imperial Shags and Gentoo Penguins feed in the shallow water off it.
    • : A cove on the far side of the ridge from the station. The path to it crosses the tussock and boggy pools that South Georgia Pipits and pintail use.
    • : A narrow anchorage east of Cumberland Bay. Gentoo Penguins climb the slope above the beach and old whale bones still line the shore.
  • Also covers: Cumberland Bay, Godthul, Grytviken, King Edward Point, Maiviken, Ocean Harbour
  • Also watch for: Antarctic fur seals hold the beaches through December and January, and southern elephant seals lie up on the flats to moult in February.

Salisbury Plain and the Bay of Isles

A quarter of a million king penguins on an outwash plain, and albatrosses on the islands offshore

Salisbury Plain spreads flat and green in front of the Grace Glacier, and King Penguins cover it while they moult. Prion Island lies a few kilometres out in the same bay and rats never reached it, so Snowy Albatrosses nest on its summit among burrowing petrels and South Georgia Pipits sing in the tussock below them.

  • Habitat: A wide glacial outwash plain behind a long beach, with tussock hillsides above it and rat-free islands scattered across the bay.
  • When: November to March. Prion Island closes while the fur seals are at the peak of their breeding season in late November and December.
  • Getting there: The plain is reached by inflatable boat onto an open beach and the surf decides whether the landing happens. Prion Island carries a boardwalk that visitors keep to, and only small parties go ashore at a time. A landing permit is arranged through the expedition operator before the voyage sails.
  • Look for: King Penguin, Snowy Albatross, South Georgia Pipit, Southern Giant Petrel, Northern Giant Petrel, Antarctic Prion, Brown Skua, Antarctic Tern
  • Stops:
    • : The second largest king penguin colony on the island, and as many as 250,000 birds gather on it during the moult. The tussock hillside above the beach gives the view over the whole bay.
    • : A small rat-free island where Snowy Albatrosses nest on mud pedestals among the tussock. A boardwalk runs to the summit and burrowing petrels honeycomb the ground beside it.
    • : A ruined whaling station at the mouth of Possession Bay. Sealers called the place rat harbour, because brown rats first reached South Georgia here in 1800.
  • Also covers: Bay of Isles, Possession Bay, Prince Olav Harbour, Prion Island, Salisbury Plain
  • Also watch for: Antarctic fur seals fill the beaches from late November, and southern elephant seals haul out to moult through January and February.

Fortuna Bay and Stromness

The last leg of the Shackleton crossing, between two king penguin beaches

A low saddle separates these two bays, and the walk over it follows the final stretch of Ernest Shackleton's 1916 crossing of the island. A large King Penguin colony stands at the head of Fortuna Bay and Light-mantled Albatrosses nest on the cliffs above the beach. Hercules Bay, a few kilometres east along the same coast, is where Macaroni Penguins climb the rock.

  • Habitat: Two glacial bays either side of a grassy pass, with tussock slopes, seabird cliffs and three ruined whaling stations along the shore.
  • When: November to March. The Fortuna Bay walk needs settled weather and the pass holds snow into December.
  • Getting there: Ships land at Fortuna Bay and pick the walkers up at Stromness some five hours later, and the route climbs about 300 metres over open ground. The whaling stations themselves stand closed because the buildings are unsafe, so the landings are made on the beaches beside them. A landing permit is arranged through the expedition operator before the voyage sails.
  • Look for: King Penguin, Macaroni Penguin, Light-mantled Albatross, South Georgia Pipit, Brown Skua, Snowy Sheathbill, Antarctic Tern, Kelp Gull, Sooty Shearwater
  • Stops:
    • : A large king penguin colony sits at the head of the bay among elephant and fur seals, and Light-mantled Albatrosses nest on the cliffs above the beach.
    • : The whaling station Shackleton reached at the end of his crossing. The ruins stand closed and the beach in front of them takes the landing.
    • : A steep cove named for a whaling boat that sheltered in it. Macaroni Penguins nest on the rock and the bay is usually watched from the inflatable boats.
  • Also covers: Fortuna Bay, Hercules Bay, Husvik, Stromness Bay
  • Also watch for: Southern elephant seals moult on the Fortuna Bay beach in January and February, and reindeer grazed this coast until the herds were removed.

St Andrews Bay and Royal Bay

The largest king penguin colony on earth, and a quieter bay beside it

Around 300,000 King Penguins stand on the grey beach at St Andrews Bay below three glaciers, and the sound of them carries out to the anchored ship. Royal Bay lies fifteen kilometres south and holds kings, Gentoo Penguins and Imperial Shags on a shore far fewer people reach. A scientific expedition wintered at Moltke Harbour inside it in 1882 to watch the transit of Venus, and the foundations of its huts are still visible.

  • Habitat: Long grey beaches below glacier fronts, with braided meltwater rivers crossing them and tussock slopes rising behind.
  • When: November to March. Chicks from the previous season still crowd the beach in November, and the adult moult packs it again in February.
  • Getting there: Both bays lie open to the swell and the landing is called off as often as it happens. Inflatable boats put passengers onto the beach and the walk to the viewpoint over the colony is flat and about two kilometres. A landing permit is arranged through the expedition operator before the voyage sails.
  • Look for: King Penguin, Gentoo Penguin, Imperial Shag, Brown Skua, Snowy Sheathbill, Light-mantled Albatross, Antarctic Tern, Southern Giant Petrel, Pintado Petrel, Southern Fulmar
  • Stops:
    • : The largest king penguin colony anywhere, and about 300,000 birds stand on the beach and the moraine behind it.
    • : A wide bay south of the main colony where King and Gentoo Penguins breed alongside Imperial Shags.
    • : The cove inside Royal Bay where a scientific expedition spent the winter of 1882 and observed the transit of Venus. Its hut foundations survive and penguins walk over them.
  • Also covers: Moltke Harbour, Royal Bay, St Andrews Bay
  • Also watch for: Southern elephant seals breed on both beaches in October and November, and the bulls stay on to moult.

Gold Harbour and the southeast

Kings under a hanging glacier, chinstraps round the point, and a fjord of bare rock

Gold Harbour holds some 25,000 pairs of King Penguins under a hanging glacier, with several hundred pairs of Gentoo Penguins among them and Light-mantled Albatrosses on the cliffs behind. Cooper Bay round the southeast corner carries the island's largest colony of Chinstrap Penguins. Drygalski Fjord cuts fourteen kilometres inland to the Risting Glacier, and rats never reached its shores, so South Georgia Pipits and Snow Petrels still hold them.

  • Habitat: Narrow beaches under hanging glaciers, tussock cliffs above them, and a deep fjord walled in bare rock at the southeast tip.
  • When: November to March. King chicks are large and brown by February, and Light-mantled Albatrosses display along the cliffs through December and January.
  • Getting there: Gold Harbour and Cooper Bay are landed by inflatable boat, and the beach at Gold Harbour is narrow enough that breeding fur seals often close it. Drygalski Fjord takes no landing at all and the ship cruises the length of it. A landing permit is arranged through the expedition operator before the voyage sails.
  • Look for: King Penguin, Chinstrap Penguin, Macaroni Penguin, Gentoo Penguin, Light-mantled Albatross, Snow Petrel, South Georgia Pipit, Southern Giant Petrel, Antarctic Tern, Pintado Petrel, Blue Petrel, Common Diving Petrel, South Georgia Diving Petrel
  • Stops:
    • : About 25,000 pairs of King Penguins breed under the glacier here, and Antarctic Terns and Southern Giant Petrels share the beach with them.
    • : Some 20,000 Chinstrap Penguins breed here, which is the island's largest colony of them, and Macaroni and Gentoo Penguins nest along the same shore.
    • : A rat-free fjord that runs fourteen kilometres to the Risting Glacier. South Georgia Pipits, prions and Snow Petrels hold its rock walls.
  • Also covers: Cooper Bay, Drygalski Fjord, Gold Harbour, Larsen Harbour
  • Also watch for: Weddell seals breed in Larsen Harbour off the fjord, which is the most northerly place in the world they do.

Right Whale Bay and the northwest

Fur seal beaches, a king colony behind them, and the albatross islands offshore

The northwest end of the island takes the worst of the weather and carries most of its seabirds. A large King Penguin colony sits behind the beach at the eastern end of Right Whale Bay. Elsehul, round the corner, packs its shore so tightly with fur seals that ships cruise it from the boats, and Macaroni and Gentoo Penguins nest on the slopes above. Bird Island lies off the tip and holds a research station that takes no visitors.

  • Habitat: Exposed north-facing bays with wide fur seal beaches, tussock slopes above them, and steep offshore islands that the rats never reached.
  • When: November to March. Fur seal numbers peak from late November to January and the beaches are hardest to land on then.
  • Getting there: Right Whale Bay keeps enough open ground behind the beach for a landing when the seals allow one. Elsehul is normally cruised from the inflatable boats because the shore is impassable. A landing permit is arranged through the expedition operator before the voyage sails.
  • Look for: King Penguin, Macaroni Penguin, Gentoo Penguin, Grey-headed Albatross, Black-browed Albatross, Snowy Albatross, Northern Giant Petrel, Antarctic Prion, Brown Skua
  • Stops:
    • : A long fur seal beach with a large king penguin colony behind its eastern end, and the beach is wide enough that a landing is often possible.
    • : A cove crowded with breeding fur seals. Macaroni and Gentoo Penguins nest above it and thousands of petrels and smaller albatrosses breed on the slopes.
  • Also covers: Bird Island, Elsehul, Right Whale Bay
  • Also watch for: Antarctic fur seals breed along this coast in their hundreds of thousands, and the beaches are impassable at the peak of the season.

Frequently Asked Questions

When is the best time to go birding in South Georgia?

Ships run from October to March and nothing reaches the island outside that stretch. October and November put southern elephant seals on the beaches with last season's king chicks standing among them. December and January are the months for Snowy Albatross on the nest and for the king colonies at full noise. February and March bring the penguin moult and fur seal pups in the shallows.

How do you get to South Georgia?

South Georgia has no airport and no scheduled service of any kind, so every visit arrives by ship. Voyages sail from Ushuaia at the southern tip of South America, which is four days of open ocean each way, or from Stanley in the Falkland Islands, which is two. Those sea days are part of the birding, because albatrosses, prions and petrels follow the ship the whole way across.

How long does a South Georgia voyage take?

Twelve days is the shortest shape that works. It sails from Stanley and gives the island about a week on the northeast coast. Eighteen days out of Ushuaia reaches every landing site on the island, and three weeks buys eight or nine days at South Georgia along with a call at the Falkland Islands on the way out. The extra days are what recover the landings the weather takes.

Can you visit South Georgia independently?

No. Every landing is made from a ship, and the landing permit is arranged through the expedition operator before the voyage sails. The ship is the base for the whole visit, shore parties go in by inflatable boat, and the island keeps no accommodation for visitors. A few private yachts make the crossing and they apply for the same permission.

What are the birds to plan the voyage around?

King Penguin in hundreds of thousands is what most people come for, and about 300,000 of them stand on the beach at St Andrews Bay below three glaciers. Snowy Albatross nests on Prion Island in the Bay of Isles, and Light-mantled Albatross displays along the cliffs at Fortuna Bay and Gold Harbour. Chinstrap Penguin holds Cooper Bay in the southeast. The South Georgia Pipit is the only songbird that breeds this far south, and the pools behind the beaches carry the island's own form of the Yellow-billed Pintail.

How close do you get to the birds?

Landings put you on the beach by inflatable boat, and the walk from the water into a king colony is short and flat at St Andrews Bay, Salisbury Plain and Gold Harbour. Prion Island carries a boardwalk that every visitor keeps to, and small parties go up it in turn. Fur seals hold the same beaches from late November to January, and they decide how much of a landing site is usable on the day.

What are the biosecurity checks before a landing?

Rats and mice came ashore with the sealers and the whalers and cleared the pipit and the burrowing petrels off the ground they reached, and an eradication has since removed them from the island. Every bag, pocket and Velcro strap is checked for seeds and soil before the first landing, and boots are scrubbed and disinfected between landings after that. The expedition staff run the checks on board while the ship is still at sea.

Can you visit the whaling stations?

Grytviken is the one station open to visitors, and it holds the museum, the whalers' church and the cemetery where Shackleton is buried. The others stand closed because the buildings are unsafe, so ships land on the beaches beside them. Whaling ran on this coast from 1904 until the 1960s, and the ruins are now among the easiest places on the island to watch Snowy Sheathbill and Gentoo Penguin at close range.

What does the weather do to a plan here?

It decides which landings happen. The bays on the northeast coast lie open to the swell, and St Andrews Bay and Gold Harbour are called off about as often as they run. A voyage carries more sites in its plan than it can use and works through them as the wind allows, which is why a longer route comes home with more of the island seen.

How many bird species does South Georgia have?

Thirty-one species breed on the island. Bird Island off the northwest tip holds twenty-seven of the thirty-one and it takes no visitors, so the bays of the main island are where a voyage meets most of that list. The water adds more on top: albatrosses, prions, petrels and storm petrels follow the ship across from South America and through the days offshore.

Can I bird here with limited mobility?

Often yes, and it varies by site. A good deal of the birding here happens from roadsides, hides and lodge grounds rather than on long walks, and some reserves keep flat, surfaced trails. Ask a lodge or reserve directly about step free access, surfaces and seating before you book. The Birdability Map records those details for birding sites, and their Access Considerations guide lists the questions worth asking. Coverage is thin outside the United States, so read a blank area as unrecorded rather than unsuitable.
