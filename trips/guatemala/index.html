At a glance

Best months
November to April for the highlands, the Pacific slope and the Peten, with February and March best for nesting quetzals. The Caribbean coast is wet all year.
Regions
Tikal and the Peten forestLaguna del Tigre and the western PetenThe Caribbean coast: Rio Dulce and Cerro San GilSierra de las Minas and the Biotopo del QuetzalThe Motagua Valley dry forestLake Atitlan and its volcanoesLos Tarrales and the Pacific slopeAntigua, Fuego and AcatenangoThe Cuchumatanes and Todos SantosMonterrico and the Pacific coast
Routes
Antigua, Atitlan and the Pacific slope6 daysThe classic loop: highlands and Tikal8 daysHighlands, cloud forest and the Caribbean11 daysThe endemic route, volcanoes to the Cuchumatanes14 daysThe whole country, dry valley to wet coast17 days
Getting in
Guatemala City (GUA) takes the international flights and Antigua is an hour away. Flores (FRS) serves Tikal, and the internal hop from the capital takes about an hour against a full day by road.
Getting around
A hired car covers the highlands, the Pacific slope and the Caribbean road comfortably. The western Peten needs a boat and a guide arranged ahead, and the Cuchumatanes road is paved but slow.

When to Go

The country runs two calendars. The highlands, the Pacific slope and the Peten have a real dry season from November to April, and the Caribbean coast has none: Puerto Barrios averages more rain in its driest month than Guatemala City gets in its wettest. Plan the inland regions around the dry months and the coast around the boats.

Peak

November to April

The dry season inland. Roads to the Cuchumatanes and the Peten are firm, the highland mornings are clear, and every wintering migrant from North America is in the country.

Go for Quetzals nesting in the cloud forest from February, the full Tikal list in comfortable weather, and warblers through the highland oak.

Trade off The busiest and most expensive months, and the Caribbean coast stays wet throughout.

Typical daily average
  • Tikal: 18 to 31 °C / 64 to 88 °F
  • Antigua: 12 to 26 °C / 54 to 79 °F
  • Rio Dulce: 19 to 31 °C / 66 to 88 °F

Green

May to August

The rains, which fall as clear mornings and heavy afternoons rather than all day. Resident birds are breeding and the forest is at its loudest.

Go for Displaying manakins on the Pacific slope, the greenest the country gets, and lodges at their quietest.

Trade off Afternoons are usually lost to rain, and the rough roads into the western Peten can close.

Typical daily average
  • Tikal: 22 to 33 °C / 72 to 91 °F
  • Antigua: 15 to 26 °C / 59 to 79 °F
  • Rio Dulce: 23 to 32 °C / 73 to 90 °F

Passage

September and October

The wettest weeks of the year and the strongest passage. Raptors and northern songbirds move down the Pacific plain in numbers.

Go for Migration on the coastal plain, shorebirds arriving at Monterrico, and the lowest prices of the year.

Trade off The peak of the hurricane season, and the wettest month on the Caribbean side falls just before it.

Typical daily average
  • Tikal: 22 to 31 °C / 72 to 88 °F
  • Antigua: 15 to 25 °C / 59 to 77 °F
  • Rio Dulce: 23 to 32 °C / 73 to 90 °F

Tikal and the Peten forest

Lowland rainforest around the Maya city, with the birds watched from the temples

The forest around Tikal is the most reachable lowland rainforest in the country, and the ruins are the reason it survived: the park protects a block of Peten forest that farming took almost everywhere else. Temple IV rises above the canopy, so a bird that would be a silhouette from a trail is at eye level from the top at dawn.

  • Habitat: Tikal preserves an accessible example of the forest that once covered most of northern Guatemala, and it sits inside the Maya Biosphere Reserve, which runs to almost 2 million ha before the contiguous protected areas across the borders are counted. Over 300 species have been found within 20 km of the ruins. Hunting is prohibited, so the large gamebirds show far better here than almost anywhere else, with Ocellated Turkey common, tame and wild alongside Great Curassow and Crested Guan.
  • When: Good all year. Ocellated Turkey, Great Curassow and Crested Guan are here in every month.
  • Getting there: Flores has daily flights from the capital, and the road on to the Tikal entrance takes an hour to an hour and a half. Cars are hired at Flores airport, buses leave the main hotels through the morning and return in the evening, and a taxi will do the return run for a negotiated fare. Tikal repays several days, and the first hour of light is the one that counts.
  • Look for: Ocellated Turkey, Great Curassow, Crested Guan, Keel-billed Toucan, Collared Aracari, Orange-breasted Falcon, Ornate Hawk-Eagle, Montezuma Oropendola
  • Also recorded in the parks here: American Yellow Warbler, Black-and-white Hawk-Eagle
  • National parks: Tikal National Park, Yaxha Nakum Naranjo National Park
  • Stops:
    • : The park itself. Bird the causeways between the temple groups from first light, and the top of Temple IV, where the platform rises above the canopy.
    • : A lakeside forest reserve with a hill trail. It is quieter than the ruins and good for the understorey.
    • : The lakeside village most people stay in, half an hour from the park gate.
    • : A forest village north of Tikal on a rough road. The forest around it sees few visitors.
    • : Ruins on a lake between Tikal and the Belize border, with waterbirds along the shore.

  • Also watch for: Spider and howler monkeys through the ruins, coati along the paths, and grey fox at dusk near the entrance.

Laguna del Tigre and the western Peten

The largest protected wetland in the country, and the Scarlet Macaw nesting forest

Laguna del Tigre is wetland and seasonally flooded forest on a scale nothing else in Guatemala matches, and the western Peten holds the country's nesting Scarlet Macaws. Getting in means a boat and a guide arranged in advance, which is why the birding here is quiet in every sense.

The Caribbean coast: Rio Dulce and Cerro San Gil

Rainforest running down to mangrove, on the wettest coast in the country

The Rio Dulce cuts a gorge between Lake Izabal and the sea, and the hills either side hold rainforest that catches the Caribbean weather all year. Cerro San Gil rises straight off the coastal plain, so a morning takes you from mangrove to wet forest without a long drive.

  • Habitat: Bocas del Polochic at the western end of Lago de Izabal is a 235-square-kilometre wetland. About 6,400 hectares stay permanently flooded and another 14,000 flood seasonally, and it holds several lagoons and stands of permanently flooded forest.
  • When: Good all year. Keel-billed Motmot, Rufous-tailed Jacamar and Tropical Royal Flycatcher hold the ground through the year.
  • Getting there: The refuge is reached only by boat, hired at El Estor on Highway 7E or at Mariscos on the south shore. The refuge itself has cabins with bunks and a kitchen.
  • Look for: Keel-billed Motmot, Lovely Cotinga, Rufous-tailed Jacamar, Tropical Royal Flycatcher, White-crowned Parrot, Brown-hooded Parrot, Mangrove Swallow, Northern Jacana
  • Also recorded in the parks here: Band-tailed Barbthroat, Central American Pygmy Owl

Sierra de las Minas and the Biotopo del Quetzal

Cloud forest on a limestone range, and the most reliable quetzal country in Guatemala

Sierra de las Minas is a long limestone range holding the largest cloud forest in Central America, and the reserve on its northern approach was set aside for the Resplendent Quetzal specifically. Cloud forest here means constant moisture rather than a rainy season, so the trees are loaded with bromeliads and moss at any time of year.

  • Habitat: The Mario Dary Rivera reserve is roughly 1,100 hectares of cloud forest at about 2,000 metres, with a good trail system: the Fern Trail runs 2 km and the Moss Trail 4 km, and the road itself is worth birding.
  • When: Good all year. Resplendent Quetzal, Highland Guan and Azure-hooded Jay are here in every month.
  • Getting there: Take the Atlantic highway from the capital to El Rancho Junction, then turn northwest on the road to Coban and fork for Purulha. The reserve entrance is signed on the highway, and it opens in the morning and closes in the late afternoon. Public transport on that route takes about three and a half hours, and drivers will set you down at the gate. Nights turn very cold.
  • Look for: Resplendent Quetzal, Highland Guan, Azure-hooded Jay, Northern Emerald Toucanet, Slate-colored Solitaire, Fulvous Owl, White-faced Quail-Dove, Violet Sabrewing
  • National parks: Sierra de las Minas Biosphere Reserve, Mario Dary Rivera Quetzal Biotope

    • : The village beside the reserve, where the lodges on this stretch of road are.
    • : A private reserve on the northern slope of the range, higher and wetter than the biotope.

The Motagua Valley dry forest

Thorn forest in a rain shadow, an hour from cloud forest and nothing like it

The Motagua valley sits in the rain shadow of two mountain ranges and holds the driest habitat in Guatemala, a thorn forest of cactus and acacia. The contrast is the point: the road from the Sierra de las Minas drops into it in under an hour, and the bird list changes completely.

  • Habitat: A semi-arid valley holding the dry-country species, and it sits conveniently on the road to the Biotopo del Quetzal.
  • When: Good all year. Turquoise-browed Motmot, Lesser Roadrunner and Russet-crowned Motmot are here in every month.
  • Getting there: The arid ground lies along the Motagua river between El Rancho and Purulha, and a signed track to El Jicaro off the Biotopo road reaches more of it.
  • Look for: Turquoise-browed Motmot, Lesser Roadrunner, Russet-crowned Motmot, White-bellied Chachalaca, Blue-and-white Mockingbird, White-throated Magpie-Jay, Altamira Oriole, Laughing Falcon
  • National park: Heloderma Natural Reserve
  • Stops:
    • : The valley town beside the thorn forest reserve, on the road east.
    • : The junction town where the Caribbean road leaves the valley, and a practical overnight.
    • : Dry hillsides on the south side of the valley, below the Sierra de las Minas escarpment.
  • Also covers: Cabanas, El Progreso, Heloderma Natural Reserve, Rio Hondo, San Agustin Acasaguastlan
  • Also watch for: The Guatemalan beaded lizard lives in this valley and nowhere else, which is why the reserves here exist.

Lake Atitlan and its volcanoes

Three volcanoes round a lake, and the Horned Guan on the upper slopes

Atitlan is a flooded caldera with three volcanoes on its southern rim, and the forest above about two thousand metres on them holds the Horned Guan. The climb is steep and starts before dawn, and the reward is a bird that lives only in the Guatemalan highlands and just over the border in southern Mexico.

  • Habitat: Lago de Atitlan is a large and stunningly beautiful mountain lake ringed by high volcanoes. Volcan Toliman rises to 3158 m and Volcan Atitlan to 3537 m on the south side, and both hold the rare Horned Guan. Reedbeds lie 2 km northwest of Santiago, and forest stands along the road west of the town. The lake was once the sole home of the Atitlan Grebe.
  • When: Good all year. Horned Guan, Blue-throated Motmot and Rufous-browed Wren hold the ground through the year.
  • Getting there: The Pan-American Highway skirts the northeast of the lake and passes through the resort town of Panajachel. South of San Andres Semetabaj a surfaced road, No. 11, forks right toward San Lucas Toliman, and a very poor road carries on from there to Santiago de Atitlan. The reedbeds northwest of Santiago are reached by hiring a boat in Santiago or by walking the road toward San Lucas for 30 minutes to the park entrance signed Pachaval. Boats also run between Panajachel and Santiago. Reaching the two volcanoes from Santiago is difficult, and trails run up from near San Lucas.
  • Look for: Horned Guan, Blue-throated Motmot, Rufous-browed Wren, Black-throated Jay, Wine-throated Hummingbird, Bushy-crested Jay, Rufous Sabrewing, Mottled Owl
  • National parks: Atitlan Multiple Use Reserve, Volcan San Pedro Regional Park
  • Stops:
    • : The most walked of the three. A guided trail climbs from San Pedro La Laguna to the guan forest.
    • : The highest of the three and a longer climb. Most people start from Santiago Atitlan.
    • : The lakeside town most itineraries base in, with boats to every village.
    • : A quieter village on the northern shore, with steep forest behind it.
  • Also covers: Atitlan Multiple Use Reserve, Panajachel, San Marcos La Laguna, San Pedro La Laguna, Santiago Atitlan, Solola, Volcan Atitlan, Volcan San Pedro Regional Park
  • Also watch for: Grey fox on the volcano trails, and the lake itself is the only home of a giant water bug found nowhere else.

Los Tarrales and the Pacific slope

Coffee farms running up the volcano, from lowland heat to cloud forest in one property

The southern slope of the Sierra Madre climbs from the coastal plain to the volcanic crest in a few kilometres, and the working coffee farms on it keep shade trees the birds use. Los Tarrales runs from about six hundred metres to well above two thousand, so a single track passes through several bird communities in a morning.

  • When: Good all year. Azure-rumped Tanager, Long-tailed Manakin and Highland Guan are on site in all twelve months.
  • Look for: Azure-rumped Tanager, Long-tailed Manakin, Highland Guan, Blue-tailed Hummingbird, Belted Flycatcher, Rufous Sabrewing, Rose-throated Becard, Collared Forest Falcon
  • National park: Volcan Atitlan Protection Zone
  • Stops:
    • : A coffee and fruit farm with its own reserve, and the usual base for the Azure-rumped Tanager.
    • : The town on the coastal highway that the farm road leaves from.
    • : A farm reserve further west on the same slope, lower and hotter, with feeders.
    • : The pass town between the Pacific slope and Lake Atitlan, useful for linking the two.
  • Also covers: Finca El Patrocinio, Los Tarrales, Patulul, Retalhuleu, San Lucas Toliman, Volcan Atitlan Protection Zone
  • Also watch for: Coati and agouti through the coffee, and armadillo on the tracks after dark.

Antigua, Fuego and Acatenango

Pine and oak above a colonial town, with an active volcano behind it

Antigua sits in a bowl between three volcanoes with pine and oak woodland on the slopes above it, which is the habitat the wintering warblers from North America use. Finca El Pilar on the hillside keeps hummingbird feeders, and the walk above them reaches oak forest inside an hour of the town.

  • Look for: Garnet-throated Hummingbird, Blue-throated Motmot, Golden-cheeked Warbler, Unspotted Saw-whet Owl, Black-capped Siskin, Green Jay, Rufous Sabrewing, Spectacled Owl
  • National park: Volcan de Acatenango Regional Park
  • Stops:
    • : A private reserve on the slope above town, with hummingbird feeders and oak forest trails.
    • : The colonial town almost every itinerary starts or ends in, an hour from the airport.
    • : The high volcano above Antigua, climbed overnight, with pine forest on the lower slopes.
  • Also covers: Antigua Guatemala, Ciudad Vieja, Finca El Pilar, Guatemala City, Volcan de Acatenango Regional Park
  • Also watch for: Volcan de Fuego erupts most days, and the ash plume is visible from the ridge trails in clear weather.

The Cuchumatanes and Todos Santos

The highest ground in Central America outside the volcanoes, and the Pink-headed Warbler

The Cuchumatanes are a limestone plateau rising above three thousand metres, the highest ground in Guatemala outside the volcanoes, and high enough to carry a different set of birds from the volcanic highlands to the south. The Pink-headed Warbler lives in the scrubby pine and oak up here and in the highlands just across the Mexican border, and nowhere else at all.

  • Habitat: Todos Santos Cuchumatan is a traditional Mayan village at 2450 m in the heart of the Cordillera de los Cuchumatanes, the most extensive area of high altitude habitat in Guatemala. The trail to San Juan Atitan carries Pink-headed Warbler, White-breasted Hawk, White-faced Quail-Dove, Grey Silky-Flycatcher, Black-capped Swallow, Rufous-browed Wren, Rufous-collared Robin and Spotted Nightingale-Thrush. Altiplano opens out about 20 km southeast of the village near a junction known as Tres Caminos, and it holds Black-capped Siskin and the local Goldman's Warbler.
  • When: Good all year. Pink-headed Warbler, Black-capped Siskin and Unspotted Saw-whet Owl are here in every month.
  • Getting there: Leave Huehuetenango north towards Chiantla and La Capellania, then go northeast on Highway 9N. Todos Santos lies about 40 km from Huehuetenango and four buses a day make the run. Local tracks reach the highland habitat, and directions for them are best asked for on site.
  • Look for: Pink-headed Warbler, Black-capped Siskin, Wine-throated Hummingbird, Unspotted Saw-whet Owl, Black-throated Jay, Rufous-browed Wren, Fulvous Owl, Green Jay
  • National park: Todos Santos Cuchumatan Regional Park
  • Stops:
    • : The village the high pine and oak is birded from, on the plateau road.
    • : The town at the foot of the climb, where the plateau road starts.
    • : The regional city, and the practical base for the whole range.
  • Also covers: Chiantla, Huehuetenango, Todos Santos Cuchumatan Regional Park
  • Also watch for: The plateau grazes sheep above the treeline, which is a landscape found nowhere else in the region.

Monterrico and the Pacific coast

Mangrove canals behind a black sand beach, and the shorebird coast

The Pacific shore is a straight line of black sand with a mangrove lagoon system running behind it, and the canals are birded from a boat at first light. Northern shorebirds use this coast through the winter, and the mangrove holds a set of birds that reaches nowhere else in the country.

  • Habitat: A mangrove swamp reserve on the Pacific shore, with a trail running along the edge of the swamp from the reserve headquarters east of the post office.
  • When: Good all year. Mangrove Swallow, Boat-billed Heron and Roseate Spoonbill stay through the year.
  • Getting there: Leave the Pan-American Highway on Highway 16 just east of Cuilapa, turn west on Highway 2 south of Chiquimulilla, then south at Taxisco for La Avellana. Alternatively take Highway 9 to Escuintla and Highway 2 east to Taxisco. From La Avellana a twenty-minute boat ride reaches the docks at Monterrico.
  • Look for: Mangrove Swallow, Boat-billed Heron, Roseate Spoonbill, Wood Stork, Royal Tern, Black-necked Stilt, Willet, Magnificent Frigatebird
  • National parks: Monterrico Multiple Use Reserve, Hawaii Wildlife Refuge
  • Stops:
    • : The beach village the mangrove boats leave from, two hours from Antigua.
    • : The quieter eastern end of the same lagoon system, with a turtle hatchery.
    • : Mangrove and estuary west along the coast, less visited than Monterrico.
    • : The river mouth east of Puerto San Jose, where the canal system meets the sea.
  • Visitor centres: Monterrico biotope station and turtle hatchery
  • Also covers: Hawaii Reserve, Hawaii Wildlife Refuge, Iztapa, Monterrico biotope station and turtle hatchery, Monterrico Multiple Use Reserve, Sipacate
  • Also watch for: Olive ridley turtles nest along this beach, and the hatcheries at Monterrico and Hawaii release through the autumn.

Frequently Asked Questions

When is the best time to go birding in Guatemala?

November to April for the country as a whole: the highlands and the Peten are dry, the roads are firm, and the wintering migrants from North America are all in. February and March add nesting quetzals in the cloud forest. The Caribbean coast is the exception and stays wet all year, so treat it on its own terms rather than saving it for the dry season.

Where do I go for the Resplendent Quetzal?

The cloud forest of the Sierra de las Minas and the Biotopo del Quetzal, which is where the reserve on the Coban road was set aside for the bird. February to May is nesting, when a pair holds a territory and calls, and that is much easier than looking for a quiet bird in thick forest at other times of year.

Is the Horned Guan realistic on a normal trip?

Yes, with a day set aside for it. The bird lives above roughly two thousand metres on the volcanoes around Lake Atitlan and its volcanoes, so it means a guided pre-dawn climb from San Pedro La Laguna or Santiago Atitlan and a hard walk back down. Fitness matters more than luck here.

How many days does Guatemala need?

Eight covers the classic loop of highlands and Tikal. Eleven adds the Caribbean lowlands, which is the only way to the wet forest of Cerro San Gil. Fourteen reaches the Cuchumatanes and the birds that live only in these highlands, and seventeen takes in the Motagua thorn forest and the western Peten as well.

Do I need a guide, or can I do this myself?

The lodges and reserves are straightforward to reach with a hired car and the trails are marked. A local guide earns their fee for three things: the Horned Guan climb, the boats into the western Peten, and the highland endemics around Todos Santos, where the sites are scattered and the birds are quiet.

How do I get between the highlands and Tikal?

Fly. Guatemala City to Flores takes about an hour and the same journey by road is a full day on the Peten highway. Most routes fly one way and hold the road day in reserve for the Sierra de las Minas, which sits between the two.

What is the birding like on the Caribbean side?

Wet forest running down to mangrove, with a different list from anywhere else in the country. Puerto Barrios averages more rain in its driest month than the highlands get in their wettest, so pack for rain whenever you go and plan the boats for first light.

Which birds live in Guatemala and almost nowhere else?

The Guatemalan highlands and those just over the Mexican border share a small set of birds found nowhere else on earth: the Horned Guan on the volcanoes, the Pink-headed Warbler on the Cuchumatanes, and the Azure-rumped Tanager on the Pacific slope above Los Tarrales. A route that takes in all three crosses the country.

Is the Petén worth more than a night at Tikal?

Yes if the forest interests you as much as the ruins. Cerro Cahui and Yaxha are quieter than the main park and hold the same birds. The western Peten around Laguna del Tigre is the only place in the country with nesting Scarlet Macaws, and you get there by boat with a guide arranged ahead.

What does a birding trip to Guatemala cost?

Simple rooms near the reserves run about fifty to a hundred US dollars a night and the comfortable lodges two hundred to four hundred and fifty. A hired car with insurance is around sixty a day, the Flores flight is a modest add-on, and a local guide for a day is usually between fifty and a hundred and fifty.

Can I bird here with limited mobility?

Often yes, and it varies by site. A good deal of the birding here happens from roadsides, hides and lodge grounds rather than on long walks, and some reserves keep flat, surfaced trails. Ask a lodge or reserve directly about step free access, surfaces and seating before you book. The Birdability Map records those details for birding sites, and their Access Considerations guide lists the questions worth asking. Coverage is thin outside the United States, so read a blank area as unrecorded rather than unsuitable.
