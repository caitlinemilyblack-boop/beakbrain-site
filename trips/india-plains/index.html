Getting in

Birds that decide the trip

Sarus Crane at Keoladeo

The tallest flying bird in the world at over a metre and a half, and a resident breeder on the Bharatpur marsh. Pairs stay together for life and display with a bugling duet that carries across the whole park.

When All year, and it breeds in the monsoon.

Where Bharatpur

Great Indian Bustard at Nannaj

Under two hundred birds left nationally, in a species that was a candidate for the national bird. Nannaj near Solapur is one of a handful of places it survives and the grassland it needs is disappearing under law that calls it wasteland.

When All year, and it displays in the monsoon.

Where Solapur

Grey Hypocolius in the Banni grassland

A bird in a family of one. It winters in the Kutch scrub and its nearest other population is in Iran. It comes to fruiting salvadora bushes in flocks at dawn, which puts it on almost every Gujarat itinerary.

When November to March.

Where Bhuj

Indian Skimmer on the Chambal

A tern-like bird whose lower mandible is longer than the upper. It ploughs the water surface as it flies. The Chambal sandbars are its main remaining breeding ground, and the boat trips there are timed for it.

When March to June, and it breeds on the sandbars.

Where Sawai Madhopur

Lesser Florican in the monsoon grass

A small bustard whose male leaps two metres above the grass in display, five hundred times a day, and then vanishes for the rest of the year. It displays only in the monsoon, which is why the wet season has any birding case at all.

When July to September.

Where Solapur

Indian Vulture at Ranthambhore

One of three Gyps vultures that fell by more than ninety-five per cent in the 1990s after diclofenac entered the cattle they fed on. The Ranthambhore cliffs hold a small breeding colony and the recovery is being fought for.

When All year, and it nests from December to April.

Where Sawai Madhopur

Malabar Pied Hornbill at Satpura

A large black and white hornbill of riverine forest. Watch it from the boat on the Denwa at Satpura, one of very few reserves in India where a boat is an option.

When All year.

Where Madhai

Painted Sandgrouse at Ranthambhore

A sandgrouse of dry rocky scrub rather than open desert. It comes to water at dusk in small parties, and the Ranthambhore forest roads are where it is looked for on the way back from a drive.

When All year, at water in the late afternoon.

Where Sawai Madhopur

When to Go

The plains run on the southwest monsoon, which arrives in June and is gone by September, and on the heat, which is the thing that actually decides when to come. November to March is the season: cool, dry, and when the wintering waterbirds are at Bharatpur. April and May are unbearable and the monsoon closes the tiger parks.

Peak

November to March

The cool dry season, which is the only sensible time to be here.

Go for Bharatpur's wintering waterbirds are at their peak, Kutch is dry and workable, the tiger parks are open and the mornings are cold rather than hot.

Trade off It is also when everybody else comes, and Ranthambhore's jeep permits sell out months ahead.

Typical daily average
  • Bharatpur: 7 to 24 °C / 45 to 75 °F
  • Bhuj: 11 to 28 °C / 52 to 82 °F
  • Solapur: 16 to 32 °C / 61 to 90 °F

Hot

April and May

The hot season, when the tigers are at the waterholes and everything else suffers.

Go for Tiger sightings peak as the water shrinks to a few pools, and the parks are quieter than in winter.

Trade off Bharatpur runs 26 to 42 C in May. This is genuinely dangerous heat and the birding is over by nine in the morning.

Typical daily average
  • Bharatpur: 26 to 42 °C / 79 to 108 °F
  • Satpura: 26 to 38 °C / 79 to 100 °F
  • Solapur: 25 to 39 °C / 77 to 102 °F

Monsoon

June to September

The southwest monsoon, when the parks close.

Go for The Lesser Florican displays on the Deccan grassland only now and leaps above the grass. It is the one reason to come in the wet.

Trade off Satpura takes 512 mm in July, the tiger reserves shut for the season and the roads to the grassland sites can be impassable.

Typical daily average
  • Bharatpur: 26 to 34 °C / 79 to 93 °F
  • Satpura: 23 to 29 °C / 73 to 84 °F
  • Solapur: 22 to 30 °C / 72 to 86 °F

Bharatpur and the Keoladeo wetland

A duck shoot turned national park, 29 square kilometres of managed marsh three hours from Delhi

Keoladeo was built as a hunting reserve for the Maharaja of Bharatpur and is now the most concentrated waterbird site in India. The whole reserve is birded on foot or by cycle-rickshaw along raised bunds, which makes it the easiest serious birding in the country. Sarus Crane breeds on the marsh, the heronries hold Painted Stork and Asian Openbill in thousands, and Indian Courser and Sirkeer Malkoha work the dry scrub at the back.

  • Habitat: A 29-square-kilometre oasis of shallow lakes, the jheels, ringed by woodland, savanna and scrub. It sits at the edge of the heavily cultivated Gangetic Plain, and over 400 species have been recorded.
  • When: The winter months, November to March, are when the wetlands fill. About 180 species in a day is possible then, with masses of waterbirds and over 25 raptors.
  • Getting there: The town of Bharatpur is reached by road and rail, 177 km south of Delhi and 50 km west of Agra. The reserve lies a couple of kilometres outside the town and is best explored by bicycle, which can be hired in town or at some of the lodges nearby. Guides are available and the best of them know where to find the specialities, above all the nightbirds.
  • Look for: Sarus Crane, Painted Stork, Asian Openbill, Indian Courser, Sirkeer Malkoha, Dusky Eagle-Owl, Bar-headed Goose, Indian Spotted Eagle
  • Parks and reserves: Keoladeo National Park
  • Stops:
    • : The town at the park gate, three hours from Delhi by road and forty minutes from Agra.
  • Also covers: Bharatpur, Keoladeo National Park
  • Also watch for: Golden Jackal and Nilgai are everywhere on the bunds, and Smooth-coated Otter works the deeper channels. Indian Rock Python basks on the path edges through the winter, which is a sight the cycle-rickshaw men point out.

Ranthambhore and the Rajasthan tiger forests

Dry deciduous forest and lakes around a hill fort, and the easiest wild tiger viewing in India

Ranthambhore is a tiger park with good birding attached rather than the other way round, and the game-drive format is what it is. The lakes below the fort hold Painted Stork and Indian Spot-billed Duck, the dry forest carries Painted Sandgrouse and Indian Vulture, and the Chambal river north of it is the reason to add a day: Indian Skimmer breeds on its sandbars and almost nowhere else.

  • Habitat: Dry deciduous forests and grasslands cover this large park of 400 sq km in eastern Rajasthan.
  • When: November to March is the season here, and the park is at its best for raptors then.
  • Getting there: Ranthambore is reached by road from Sawai Madhopur, which is connected to Delhi by rail. Access within the reserve is limited to expensive vehicles, so many birders stay in Sawai Madhopur, hire bicycles and work the fringes, where many of the best birds occur. The entrance road and the fort complex near the reserve headquarters are the best sites outside the park.
  • Look for: Indian Skimmer, Painted Sandgrouse, Indian Vulture, Indian Spot-billed Duck, Brown Fish Owl, Rufous Treepie, Indian Courser, Indian Stone-curlew
  • Parks and reserves: Ranthambhore National Park
  • Stops:
    • : The town at the park gate. The hotels and the drive bookings are here.
  • Also covers: Ranthambhore National Park, Sawai Madhopur
  • Also watch for: Ranthambhore's tigers are habituated to vehicles and are seen on a majority of drives, which is true almost nowhere else. Sloth Bear, Leopard and Marsh Crocodile share the park, and the Chambal holds Gharial and Ganges River Dolphin.

The Rann of Kutch and the Gujarat plains

Salt desert, grassland and creek on the Pakistan border, and the driest ground above

Kutch is India's Central Asian corner. The Banni grasslands hold Grey Hypocolius, a bird whose whole family has one member and whose nearest population is in Iran, and the Naliya plains carry Asian Houbara and White-naped Tit. The Little Rann to the east is a salt flat with wild ass on it and flamingo colonies at its edge.

  • Habitat: The Rann of Kutch is a vast, shallow inland lake. Much of it has dried out by February and March, and the shallow lagoons that remain still hold wintering wetland birds.
  • When: Best from September to April. Sykes's Nightjar, Greater Flamingo and Indian Courser hold the ground through the year.
  • Getting there: The Rann is three hours by road from Ahmadabad. The monsoon between June and September closes it off.
  • Look for: Grey Hypocolius, Asian Houbara, White-naped Tit, Sykes's Nightjar, Greater Flamingo, Marshall's Iora, Sykes's Lark, Indian Courser
  • Parks and reserves: Wild Ass Wildlife Sanctuary, Little Rann of Kutch, Kutch Desert Wildlife Sanctuary
  • Stops:
    • : The town in central Kutch, with an airport. It is the base for the Banni grassland and the Naliya plains.
    • : The village on the edge of the Little Rann. It is the way in to the wild ass sanctuary.
  • Also covers: Bhuj, Dasada, Kutch Desert Wildlife Sanctuary, Wild Ass Wildlife Sanctuary, Little Rann of Kutch
  • Also watch for: The Little Rann holds the world's only population of Indian Wild Ass, several thousand of them on the salt flat. Desert Fox and Indian Wolf both use the Banni grassland, and the Gir forest south of Bhavnagar carries the last Asiatic Lions.

The central highlands: Satpura, Kanha and Pench

Sal and teak forest across the middle of the country, the landscape the Jungle Book was written about

The central Indian forests are tiger parks that also hold the peninsular birds, and Satpura is the one where you can walk. Boat, canoe and foot safaris are allowed there and nowhere else in a major tiger reserve, which changes the birding completely. Malabar Pied Hornbill, Indian Scops Owl and White-naped Woodpecker are on the walks, and Kanha's meadows carry the open-country raptors.

  • Habitat: Sal forest and grassland cover the 940 sq km of Kanha in Madhya Pradesh, which also carries a good cross section of the birds of India.
  • When: April is the best month at Kanha, when the grass is short and the prey of the tigers gathers at the few waterholes that remain.
  • Getting there: Kanha is about six hours by road from Jabalpur and about two hours by road from Gondia. Both towns sit on the rail network.
  • Look for: Malabar Pied Hornbill, Indian Scops Owl, White-naped Woodpecker, Indian Pitta, Crested Serpent Eagle, Jungle Owlet, Indian Grey Hornbill, White-rumped Vulture
  • Parks and reserves: Satpura National Park, Kanha National Park, Pench National Park
  • Stops:
    • Madhai: The gate on the Denwa river for Satpura. A boat crosses into the reserve here.
    • : The sal forest and meadow reserve. It is the barasingha stronghold.
  • Also covers: Kanha National Park, Madhai, Pench National Park, Satpura National Park
  • Also watch for: Tiger, Leopard, Sloth Bear, Dhole and Gaur all occur across these parks, and Kanha holds the only population of Hard-ground Barasingha, brought back from sixty-odd animals in the 1970s. Satpura's walking safaris put you on foot with all of it.

The Deccan grasslands and Nannaj

What is left of the Indian grassland belt, and the last ground of the Great Indian Bustard

India's grasslands are classed as wasteland in law and have gone accordingly, and the birds that need them have gone with them. Nannaj near Solapur is one of a handful of places Great Indian Bustard survives, and the national population is now under two hundred. Lesser Florican displays on the same ground in the monsoon and leaps above the grass, and it is being watched into extinction too.

  • Habitat: Open grassland with scattered Acacia and Cassia scrub on the Deccan plateau, the last Maharashtra stronghold of the Great Indian Bustard, with the Mardi plots kept as pure grass.
  • When: August to January, when the grass is green after the monsoon and the bustards are most reliably found.
  • Getting there: Nannaj is 22 km from Solapur on the Barshi road, with state buses every half hour and a walk of about a kilometre from the stop along the Mardi road.
  • Look for: Great Indian Bustard, Lesser Florican, Indian Courser, Chestnut-bellied Sandgrouse, Montagu's Harrier, Rufous-tailed Lark, Ashy-crowned Sparrow-Lark, Painted Francolin
  • Parks and reserves: Great Indian Bustard Sanctuary, Nannaj (wildlife sanctuary)
  • Stops:
    • : The city nearest the Nannaj grassland. The sanctuary is a short drive north.
  • Also covers: Great Indian Bustard Sanctuary, Nannaj, Solapur
  • Also watch for: Indian Wolf and Blackbuck both hold the remaining Deccan grassland, and Indian Fox works the same open ground at dusk. The grassland itself is the rarest thing here.

Frequently Asked Questions

When is the best time to bird the northern plains?

November to March. Keoladeo's wintering waterbirds are at their peak, Kutch records almost no rain, the tiger reserves are open and the mornings are cold rather than hot. May runs to 42 C at Bharatpur and the monsoon closes the parks from June. The three seasons.

Is Bharatpur still worth it?

Yes, and it remains the most concentrated waterbird site in India. The Siberian Cranes that made it famous stopped coming in 2002 and the marsh depends on water allocations that have been fought over for years, but the heronries, the Sarus Cranes and the winter assembly are all still there and it is all done on foot. Bharatpur and the Keoladeo wetland.

Why is Kutch on a birding page?

Because it is Central Asia inside India. Grey Hypocolius winters in the Banni grassland and its nearest other population is in Iran; Macqueen's Bustard and White-naped Tit are on the Naliya plains; and the Little Rann holds the world's only Indian Wild Ass. Nothing else above shares that list. The Rann of Kutch.

Can I combine birds and tigers?

Yes, and most itineraries do. The tiger reserves hold the peninsular forest birds and the drive format works for both. Satpura is the one where you can walk, canoe and take a boat, which no other major reserve allows, and that changes the birding completely. The central highlands.

How bad is the grassland situation?

Bad. India's grasslands are classed as wasteland in law and have been converted accordingly. Great Indian Bustard is under two hundred birds nationally, Lesser Florican is going the same way, and Nannaj is one of a handful of places either survives. It is named here because the scale of the loss is part of what you are looking at. The Deccan grasslands.

Do I need permits?

Not for the birding, but every tiger reserve requires a jeep and a licensed guide booked in advance, and Ranthambhore's permits in particular sell out months ahead in the peak season. That is arranged by the operator or the lodge. The itineraries.

Do the places to stay in the northern plains and the west hold sustainability certification?

Almost none. India has no national scheme and the international ones reach the city hotel chains rather than the forest lodges. Certification.

How many bird species does India have?

Around 1,350 recorded, which is more than any country outside the tropics except China, and about eighty live nowhere else. India is four guides here because the Himalaya, the northeast, the plains and the Western Ghats share a country rather than an avifauna. The birds.

Can I bird here with limited mobility?

Often yes, and it varies by site. A good deal of the birding here happens from roadsides, hides and lodge grounds rather than on long walks, and some reserves keep flat, surfaced trails. Ask a lodge or reserve directly about step free access, surfaces and seating before you book. The Birdability Map records those details for birding sites, and their Access Considerations guide lists the questions worth asking. Coverage is thin outside the United States, so read a blank area as unrecorded rather than unsuitable.
