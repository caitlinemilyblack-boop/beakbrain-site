Birding in Alaska

Alaska is closer to Russia than to Seattle at its western end, and takes Asian birds, Arctic breeders and five million shorebirds on one delta that reach nowhere else in the country.

Birds that decide the trip

Bristle-thighed Curlew above Nome

It winters on Pacific islands and breeds on a handful of Alaskan hills, and the Kougarok road out of Nome is the only place on earth where a visitor can reasonably walk up to the breeding ground.

When Early to mid-June, on the tundra hills at the road end.

Where Seward Peninsula, Nome

Red-legged Kittiwake on the Pribilofs

A kittiwake that breeds almost entirely on four Bering Sea islands, and St Paul holds the largest colony. The cliffs there carry auklets, murres and puffins on the same ledges.

When June to August, on the island cliffs.

Where Pribilof Islands

Spectacled Eider past the ice edge

A sea duck that winters in openings in the pack ice and breeds on wet coastal tundra. Utqiagvik in June is where most people see one, moving past the point with the other eiders.

When June, on the coastal tundra and past the point.

Where Barrow, Yukon Delta National Wildlife Refuge

Every Pacific Brant on one lagoon

Izembek Lagoon holds one of the largest eelgrass beds on earth, and essentially the entire Pacific Brant population stages on it before crossing the ocean in a single flight.

When Late September and October, before they leave.

Where Izembek National Wildlife Refuge

Bluethroat on the Seward Peninsula

A Eurasian chat that crosses the Bering Strait to breed in willow scrub in western Alaska. The males song-flight over the willows in the first weeks of June and are unmistakable.

When June, in willow scrub along the Nome roads.

Where Seward Peninsula, Nome

Five million shorebirds on the Copper River

The delta is the largest contiguous wetland on the Pacific coast of North America and the western flyway funnels across it in about a fortnight. Cordova holds a festival for the week it happens.

When The first two weeks of May.

Where Copper River Delta

Kittlitz's Murrelet at the glacier face

A murrelet that feeds in the silty water in front of tidewater glaciers and nests on bare scree above them. It is scarce, poorly known, and tied to ice that is retreating.

When May to August, in glacial meltwater.

Where Glacier Bay National Park, Copper River Delta

McKay's Bunting, which breeds on two islands

The whole world population breeds on St Matthew and Hall Islands in the Bering Sea and winters on the western Alaskan coast. It is one of the most range-restricted birds in North America.

When Winter on the western coast, and summer only on islands almost nobody reaches.

Where Pribilof Islands, Nome

When to Go

Alaska has a very short season. Almost everything here works from late May to early August, and outside it the tundra is frozen, the villages are hard to reach and most of the birds are somewhere else entirely.

Go for Cordova for the delta, and the southeast panhandle as the rainforest birds arrive.

Trade off The tundra is still frozen and Nome and Utqiagvik are not yet worth the flight.

Nome and the Seward Peninsula

Bristle-thighed Curlew, and three roads into open tundra from one small town

Nome has three gravel roads running out of it into tundra, and between them they reach almost everything the Seward Peninsula holds. The Bristle-thighed Curlew breeds nowhere accessible on earth except the hills at the end of one of them.

  • Habitat: Open tundra of dwarf willow and sedge, with rocky hills, tundra ponds, coastal lagoons and a gravel beach at the town.
  • When: Early June to mid-July. The curlews are on the breeding hills from the first week of June and the roads are passable once the snow goes.
  • Getting there: Nome is reached only by air from Anchorage. Hire cars are available in town and the three gravel roads, to Teller, Council and Kougarok, run about seventy miles each into the tundra.
  • Base yourself in: Nome
  • Look for: Bristle-thighed Curlew, Bluethroat, Arctic Warbler, Northern Wheatear, Gyrfalcon, Willow Ptarmigan, Rock Ptarmigan, Long-tailed Jaeger, Bar-tailed Godwit, Red-necked Stint, Aleutian Tern, Eastern Yellow Wagtail
  • Also covers: Nome, Seward Peninsula
  • Also watch for: Muskox range the tundra near the roads and grizzly are regular along the rivers.

Gambell and the Bering Sea islands

Asian strays on a gravel spit, and a million auklets on a cliff

Gambell sits closer to Siberia than to mainland Alaska and takes Asian birds that reach North America nowhere else. The Pribilofs, further south, hold seabird cliffs with the only Red-legged Kittiwakes in the world worth counting.

  • Habitat: A gravel spit and boneyard beside a seabird mountain on St Lawrence, and basalt seabird cliffs above tundra and lupine on the Pribilofs.
  • When: Late May to mid-June for Asian strays at Gambell, and June to August for the Pribilof seabird cliffs.
  • Getting there: Both are flights from Anchorage. Gambell is a village stay with organised birding groups and no hotel in the usual sense; St Paul is reached on a scheduled flight and the island is toured with a guide.
  • Base yourself in: Gambell
  • Look for: Red-legged Kittiwake, Least Auklet, Crested Auklet, Parakeet Auklet, Thick-billed Murre, Common Murre, Horned Puffin, Tufted Puffin, McKay's Bunting, Emperor Goose, King Eider, Sharp-tailed Sandpiper
  • Parks and reserves: Alaska Maritime National Wildlife Refuge
  • Also covers: Alaska Maritime National Wildlife Refuge, Pribilof Islands, St. Lawrence Island
  • Also watch for: Northern fur seals breed on the Pribilof beaches in hundreds of thousands and Arctic fox work the cliffs.

Denali and the interior

Boreal forest and alpine tundra on one park road, with the continent’s highest mountain behind it

The Denali road climbs from spruce taiga through willow to open tundra in ninety miles, and the bus that runs it is the way most people see the park. The Yukon Flats north of it is one of the densest waterfowl breeding areas in North America.

  • Habitat: Spruce taiga on the lower ground rising through willow and dwarf birch to open alpine tundra and scree, with braided glacial rivers between.
  • When: June to early August. The park road opens progressively as the snow clears and the tundra birds are on territory through June.
  • Getting there: Anchorage or Fairbanks are the airports. Private vehicles are restricted beyond mile fifteen of the Denali road, so the park is seen from the shuttle bus, which stops for wildlife.
  • Base yourself in: Denali Park
  • Look for: Willow Ptarmigan, Rock Ptarmigan, White-tailed Ptarmigan, Northern Hawk-Owl, Great Grey Owl, Boreal Chickadee, Bohemian Waxwing, Rusty Blackbird, Sandhill Crane, Golden Eagle, Surfbird, American Three-toed Woodpecker
  • Parks and reserves: Yukon Flats National Wildlife Refuge, Denali National Park, Anchorage Coastal Wildlife Refuge (National wildlife refuge), Denali Wilderness (Protected area), Susitna Flats State Game Refuge (Protected area)
  • Also covers: Anchorage Coastal Wildlife Refuge, Denali National Park, Denali Wilderness, Susitna Flats State Game Refuge, Yukon Flats National Wildlife Refuge
  • Also watch for: Grizzly, wolf, caribou, moose and Dall sheep are all seen from the park road, which is why the bus exists.

The Arctic coast and the refuge

Eiders past a gravel point at the top of the continent

Utqiagvik is the northernmost town in the United States and the sea ice moves past it with hundreds of thousands of eiders in spring. The Arctic refuge behind it is coastal plain rising to the Brooks Range, and it is a flight and a charter to reach.

  • Habitat: Wet coastal tundra of polygon ponds and sedge, with gravel beaches, shifting sea ice offshore and the Brooks Range rising inland.
  • When: June and early July. The eider migration past the point runs in late May and June, and the tundra breeders are on territory through June.
  • Getting there: Utqiagvik is a flight from Anchorage or Fairbanks and has hotels and hire vehicles. The Arctic refuge has no roads at all and needs a chartered bush flight from Fairbanks.
  • Base yourself in: Utqiagvik
  • Look for: Spectacled Eider, Steller's Eider, King Eider, Common Eider, Yellow-billed Loon, Snowy Owl, Buff-breasted Sandpiper, Pectoral Sandpiper, Red Phalarope, Snow Bunting, Lapland Longspur, Pomarine Jaeger
  • Parks and reserves: Arctic National Wildlife Refuge, National Petroleum Reserve–Alaska, Mollie Beattie Wilderness (Protected area)
  • Also covers: Arctic National Wildlife Refuge, Barrow, Mollie Beattie Wilderness, National Petroleum Reserve–Alaska
  • Also watch for: Polar bear come ashore at Utqiagvik and the Porcupine caribou herd calves on the refuge coastal plain.

The southeast panhandle

Temperate rainforest and tidewater glaciers, reached by boat

The panhandle is a drowned mountain range of fjords and islands under Sitka spruce and hemlock, with almost no roads. Glacier Bay is a day boat from Bartlett Cove and Misty Fiords is floatplane or ferry country.

  • Habitat: Sitka spruce and western hemlock rainforest on drowned mountains, with tidewater glaciers, fjords, muskeg bog and rocky islets.
  • When: May to September. The murrelets are on the water all summer and the rainforest is wet in every month.
  • Getting there: Juneau is reached by air or by the state ferry and has no road to the rest of the continent. Glacier Bay is a day boat from Bartlett Cove, and Misty Fiords is floatplane or boat from Ketchikan.
  • Base yourself in: Juneau
  • Look for: Kittlitz's Murrelet, Marbled Murrelet, Black Oystercatcher, Harlequin Duck, Barrow's Goldeneye, Rufous Hummingbird, Varied Thrush, Chestnut-backed Chickadee, Steller's Jay, American Crow, Bald Eagle, Sooty Grouse
  • Parks and reserves: Glacier Bay National Park, Tongass National Forest (Forest or grassland), Kluane / Wrangell-St. Elias / Glacier Bay / Tatshenshini-Alsek (Protected area), Glacier Bay Wilderness (Protected area), Misty Fjords National Monument Wilderness
  • Also covers: Glacier Bay National Park, Glacier Bay Wilderness, Juneau, Kluane / Wrangell-St. Elias / Glacier Bay / Tatshenshini-Alsek, Misty Fiords National Monument, Misty Fjords National Monument Wilderness, Tongass National Forest
  • Also watch for: Humpback whales feed in Glacier Bay through the summer and brown bear work the salmon streams.

Prince William Sound and the Copper River

Five million shorebirds on one delta in a fortnight of May

The Copper River Delta is the largest contiguous wetland on the Pacific coast of North America and effectively the whole western flyway funnels across it. Cordova holds a festival for the week it happens.

  • Habitat: A vast silt delta of tidal flats, sloughs and sedge marsh, with spruce and hemlock on the uplands and glaciers reaching the sea in the sound.
  • When: The first two weeks of May for the shorebird passage, which is compressed and enormous. The sound holds its seabirds all summer.
  • Getting there: Cordova is a flight or a ferry from Anchorage with no road in. The Copper River Highway runs out across the delta to the Million Dollar Bridge, and washouts sometimes shorten it.
  • Base yourself in: Cordova
  • Look for: Western Sandpiper, Dunlin, Short-billed Dowitcher, Aleutian Tern, Trumpeter Swan, Black Oystercatcher, Kittlitz's Murrelet, Marbled Murrelet, Harlequin Duck, Rufous Hummingbird, Bald Eagle, Sooty Grouse
  • Parks and reserves: Homer Airport Critical Habitat Area (Protected area), Chugach National Forest (Forest or grassland), Kenai Fjords National Park, Kachemak Bay State Park (State or provincial park), Caines Head State Recreation Area (Protected area)
  • Also covers: Caines Head State Recreation Area, Chugach National Forest, Copper River Delta, Homer Airport Critical Habitat Area, Kachemak Bay State Park, Kenai Fjords National Park, Seward
  • Also watch for: Sea otters raft in the sound and the delta was lifted several metres by the 1964 earthquake.

The Alaska Peninsula and the western deltas

The entire world population of Brant on one eelgrass lagoon

Izembek Lagoon on the Alaska Peninsula holds one of the largest eelgrass beds on earth, and essentially every Pacific Brant stops on it in autumn. The Yukon Delta to the north, on the western coast, is a vast wet tundra plain and the most important waterbird breeding ground in the state.

  • Habitat: An eelgrass lagoon behind a barrier spit, with tundra, volcanic uplands and, on the Yukon Delta, a flat wet tundra plain of ponds and sedge running to the Bering Sea.
  • When: Late September and October for the Brant staging at Izembek. June and July for the delta breeders.
  • Getting there: Cold Bay is a scheduled flight from Anchorage, and the refuge is reached on gravel roads from the village. The Yukon Delta is charter flying and village stays from Bethel.
  • Base yourself in: Cold Bay
  • Look for: Brant Goose, Emperor Goose, Steller's Eider, Spectacled Eider, Tundra Swan, Bar-tailed Godwit, Bristle-thighed Curlew, Sandhill Crane, Aleutian Tern, Red-throated Loon, Willow Ptarmigan, Rock Ptarmigan
  • Parks and reserves: Yukon Delta National Wildlife Refuge, Izembek National Wildlife Refuge, Izembek Wilderness (Protected area), Tongass National Forest (Forest or grassland), Alaska Peninsula National Wildlife Refuge
  • Also covers: Alaska Peninsula National Wildlife Refuge, Izembek National Wildlife Refuge, Izembek Wilderness, Redoubt Bay, Tongass National Forest, Yukon Delta National Wildlife Refuge
  • Also watch for: Brown bear are at very high density on the peninsula and sea otters use the lagoon.

Frequently Asked Questions

When is the best time to go birding in Alaska?

June and the first days of July for almost everything: the tundra breeders are nesting, the seabird cliffs are full, and the daylight barely ends. The first fortnight of May is the Copper River shorebird passage, and late September into October is the Brant staging at Izembek. Outside those windows most of the state is frozen or empty.

Which birds can I see in Alaska and nowhere else in the country?

Bristle-thighed Curlew breeds on a few Alaskan hills and winters on Pacific islands. Red-legged Kittiwake, McKay’s Bunting, Spectacled Eider, Steller’s Eider and Emperor Goose are all effectively Alaskan, and Gambell takes Asian birds that reach North America nowhere else at all.

How much of this needs flying?

Most of it. Nome, Gambell, St Paul, Utqiagvik, Cold Bay and Cordova have no road connection to anywhere, so each is a scheduled flight from Anchorage. Only Denali, Anchorage and the road system around them can be driven.

How far ahead do I need to book?

A year for Gambell and St Paul. Both are small villages with limited beds and organised birding groups take most of what there is in the June window.

Is Denali worth it for birds alone?

It is worth it for the elevation series and for seeing tundra without a charter flight, and the mammals are extraordinary. But the specialities that bring people to Alaska are mostly west and north of it.

Can I bird here with limited mobility?

Sites on this page differ from each other more than any rating could carry, so this is the framework the standard British guides use, and the reserve's own page and warden hold the answers.

  • Permission · some reserves open ground to disabled visitors that is closed to everyone else, and it is granted by asking.
  • Priority · parking bays, hides and the nearest paths are often held for disabled visitors. Ask what is reserved and how to claim it.
  • Parking · how close a car reaches the birds shapes the whole day. Ring ahead for the nearest hard standing to the water or the hide.
  • Paths · surfaces, gradients and gate widths vary inside a single reserve. Ask which trail is boarded, surfaced or level.
  • Perches · a hide with a low window, or a bench where the view opens, turns a viewpoint into a morning.

Every reserve above with its own link carries its access detail there, and a warden answers what a page cannot.
