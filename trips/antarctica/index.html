Getting in
No scheduled flight lands in Antarctica. Ships sail from Ushuaia (USH) at the southern tip of South America, and a few fly-cruises leave from Punta Arenas (PUQ) for the airstrip on King George Island.
Getting around
A visit runs entirely from the ship. Landings are made by inflatable boat and depend on the swell and the ice, and the International Association of Antarctica Tour Operators sets how many people may be ashore at a site and how close anyone may come to the wildlife.

Birds that decide the trip

Emperor Penguin at Snow Hill Island

The only bird that breeds through the Antarctic winter, and the colony off Snow Hill Island is the one a tourist voyage can reach. Getting there needs an icebreaker with helicopters aboard, and the sea ice cancels the attempt in many seasons.

When October and November

Where Weddell

Adelie Penguin at Paulet Island

This is the true Antarctic penguin and it nests further south than any other bird. Paulet Island and Hope Bay each hold more than 100,000 pairs, and Cape Adare in the Ross Sea holds the largest rookery on earth.

When November to February

Where Weddell, Ross Sea

Chinstrap Penguin at Baily Head

Tens of thousands of pairs nest in a natural amphitheatre behind the beach on the outer side of Deception Island, and the birds file through one narrow valley to reach the sea. The surf allows a landing only in calm weather.

When November to February

Where South Shetland

Gentoo Penguin in the Gerlache Strait

This is the commonest penguin of the landings and it nests on almost every ice-free point between the South Shetland Islands and Petermann Island. The colony at Port Lockroy lives among the buildings of a restored British base.

When November to March

Where Gerlache

Snow Petrel over the pack ice

An entirely white petrel that feeds along the edge of the pack ice and nests on cliff ledges inland. The birds at Brown Bluff nest low enough on the slope to be seen on the nest, which is rare anywhere.

When November to February

Where Weddell, Antarctic Circle

Antarctic Petrel on the Weddell ice

A brown and white fulmarine petrel that breeds on inland cliffs hundreds of kilometres from the sea and feeds at the pack ice edge. Ships working the Weddell Sea and the Ross Sea meet it in flocks over the floes.

When November to February

Where Weddell, Ross Sea

Snowy Albatross across the Drake

The largest wingspan of any living bird, and the crossing is where a visitor meets it. The birds hang off the stern for hours at a time and cover the passage without a wingbeat.

When November to March

Where Drake

Wilson's Storm Petrel at the colonies

This is one of the most numerous birds on earth and it nests in the scree of the same islands the penguins use. The birds flutter over the water with their feet down and return to the talus slopes at all hours.

When November to March

Where South Shetland, Gerlache

Snowy Sheathbill at the penguin colonies

The only Antarctic bird with no webbed feet, and it walks between the nests taking eggs, spilled food and scraps it can reach. Every large colony on the peninsula has a few.

When November to March

Where South Shetland, Gerlache

South Polar Skua at the rookeries

A migrant that breeds beside the penguin colonies and winters as far north as Greenland and Japan. It nests on the high ground above the rookeries and takes eggs and chicks from them.

When November to February

Where Gerlache, Ross Sea

When to Go

Early season

Late October and November

The sea ice is still breaking up and every landing is white. Penguins are courting and laying, and the colonies are at their cleanest and their loudest.

Go for Snow Hill Island for the emperor colony, and the chinstrap and Adelie colonies as the birds come ashore.

Trade off Ice closes the southern channels, so the Lemaire Channel and the water beyond the Antarctic Circle stay out of reach.

Typical daily average
  • Hope Bay: -8 to 1 °C / 18 to 34 °F
  • Anvers Island: -7 to 1 °C / 19 to 34 °F

High summer

December and January

The warmest weeks of the year and the longest daylight. Penguin chicks hatch through late December and are large and demanding by the end of January.

Go for The gentoo colonies of the Gerlache Strait, and Petermann Island at the southern end of the Lemaire Channel.

Trade off This is the busiest part of the season, and the colonies turn muddy with meltwater and guano.

Typical daily average
  • Hope Bay: -3 to 3 °C / 27 to 37 °F
  • Anvers Island: -2 to 4 °C / 28 to 39 °F

Late season

February and March

The ice has gone from the channels and ships reach their furthest south. Penguin chicks fledge and the beaches empty as the birds go to sea.

Go for Crystal Sound and the Antarctic Circle, and the humpback whales that gather to feed in Wilhelmina Bay.

Trade off The colonies are past their peak and the daylight is shortening. Antarctic fur seals are ashore in numbers and defend their ground.

Typical daily average
  • Hope Bay: -6 to 2 °C / 21 to 36 °F
  • Anvers Island: -2 to 3 °C / 28 to 37 °F

A month at sea to the far side of the continent, and the only way to reach the largest Adelie rookeries and the huts Scott and Shackleton left behind. Pack ice governs the route and several of these landings fail in most seasons.

The Drake Passage

Two days of open ocean each way, and the best seabird watching of the trip

Every ship sailing south from Ushuaia crosses about 800 kilometres of the Southern Ocean, and the crossing takes roughly two days in each direction. Snowy Albatross and Black-browed Albatross follow the ship from the first morning, and the water changes at the Antarctic Convergence, where cold southern surface water sinks beneath warmer water from the north. Prions and storm petrels come up as the ship nears the ice.

  • Habitat: Deep open ocean over the Antarctic Circumpolar Current, with a front near the middle of the crossing where cold Antarctic surface water meets warmer subantarctic water.
  • When: November to March, which is the only part of the year ships sail.
  • Getting there: The passage is crossed by expedition ship from Ushuaia and the sailing takes about two days each way. Sea conditions govern the whole crossing and swells of several metres are ordinary. Birders watch from the open stern deck, which stays usable in most weather.
  • Base yourself in: On board the ship
  • Look for: Snowy Albatross, Black-browed Albatross, Grey-headed Albatross, Light-mantled Albatross, Southern Giant Petrel, Pintado Petrel, Antarctic Prion, Blue Petrel, Wilson's Storm Petrel, Sooty Shearwater
  • Also covers: Drake Passage
  • Also watch for: Fin whales and hourglass dolphins cross the ship's track in the middle of the passage, and humpback whales appear as the water shallows towards the South Shetland Islands.

The South Shetland Islands

First land after the crossing, a flooded volcano and the biggest chinstrap colonies on the route

The South Shetland Islands lie about 120 kilometres off the tip of the Antarctic Peninsula and take the opening landings of almost every voyage. Deception Island is a drowned volcanic caldera that a ship sails into through a gap in its own wall, and Chinstrap Penguin nests on the outer slopes at Baily Head in tens of thousands of pairs. Hannah Point on Livingston Island holds gentoo and chinstrap colonies side by side, with a few Macaroni Penguin tucked in among them.

  • Habitat: Black volcanic beaches, scoria slopes and low ice-free headlands, with moss and lichen beds on the damper ground and glaciers standing behind almost every landing.
  • When: November to March, with the chinstrap chicks on the beaches through January and February.
  • Getting there: Landings are made from an expedition ship by inflatable boat and every one of them depends on the swell. Baily Head faces the open sea and is turned back often, and Whalers Bay inside the caldera is the sheltered alternative.
  • Base yourself in: On board the ship
  • Look for: Chinstrap Penguin, Gentoo Penguin, Macaroni Penguin, Southern Giant Petrel, Kelp Gull, Antarctic Tern, Snowy Sheathbill, Wilson's Storm Petrel, Black-bellied Storm Petrel, Brown Skua, White-chinned Petrel
  • Stops:
    • : Deception Island is a volcano whose caldera lies open to the sea, and a ship enters it through a gap in the crater wall called Neptunes Bellows. Whaling tanks and an abandoned British base stand on the black sand of Whalers Bay inside.
    • : Chinstrap Penguin nests in tens of thousands of pairs on the scoria slopes behind the beach on the outer side of Deception Island. The surf breaks straight onto the landing, so a ship reaches it only in calm weather.
    • : A crescent of volcanic rock off Livingston Island where Chinstrap Penguin nests on the southern prong. Wilson's Storm Petrel breeds in the scree of the higher ground and flies around it at all hours.
    • : A Gentoo Penguin colony sits on the rising ground behind a long gravel spit on Greenwich Island. Seals rest along the spit and a sealer's iron try-pot still lies at its base.
  • Also covers: Aitcho Islands, Baily Head, Deception Island, Greenwich Island, Half Moon Island, King George Island, Livingston Island, Whalers Bay, Yankee Harbour
  • Also watch for: Southern elephant seals moult in muddy wallows at Hannah Point through January, and Antarctic fur seals haul out on most of the beaches late in the season.

The Gerlache Strait and Paradise Harbour

Sheltered water inside the peninsula, with the gentoo colonies and the two mainland landings

The Gerlache Strait separates Anvers and Brabant Islands from the mainland, and almost every ship works up and down it between landings. Paradise Harbour and Neko Harbour give the two landings that put a visitor on the continent itself, and Gentoo Penguin nests on the bare rock above both beaches. Imperial Shag and Kelp Gull breed along the eastern shore of Cuverville Island, and skuas hold the high ground above the colonies.

  • Habitat: A deep channel between glaciated islands and the mainland, with small ice-free points and moss-covered domes carrying the colonies.
  • When: November to March. The gentoo chicks hatch through late December and are on the beaches in January.
  • Getting there: Landings and inflatable boat cruises are run from an expedition ship, and brash ice closes some of the bays late in the season. Port Lockroy sits in the Neumayer Channel on the western side and is reached on the same circuit.
  • Base yourself in: On board the ship
  • Look for: Gentoo Penguin, Imperial Shag, Kelp Gull, Brown Skua, South Polar Skua, Snowy Sheathbill, Southern Giant Petrel, Wilson's Storm Petrel, Antarctic Tern, Pintado Petrel
  • Stops:
    • : A rocky point on the eastern side of Andvord Bay, and one of two places a visitor stands on the Antarctic mainland. Gentoo Penguin nests up the slope from the beach and skuas nest above them.
    • : A dome-shaped island in the Errera Channel carrying large gentoo colonies on its northern shore. Kelp Gull and Imperial Shag breed along the eastern coast, which is watched from an inflatable boat.
    • : A small island in the Errera Channel with a gentoo colony spread up its summit ridge. The climb up the snow slope is the usual reason ships stop here.
    • : A restored British base on Goudier Island in the Neumayer Channel, with a gentoo colony living among the buildings. The colony here is one of the longest studied on the peninsula.
  • Also covers: Andvord Bay, Anvers Island, Cuverville Island, Danco Island, Errera Channel, Gerlache Strait, Neko Harbour, Neumayer Channel, Paradise Harbour, Port Lockroy, Wiencke Island, Wilhelmina Bay
  • Also watch for: Humpback and minke whales feed in Wilhelmina Bay through February and March, and leopard seals lie on the floes at the edge of the gentoo colonies.

The Lemaire Channel and Petermann Island

A narrow passage under vertical cliffs, and the southernmost landing most voyages reach

The Lemaire Channel runs about 11 kilometres between the mainland and Booth Island, and the rock stands almost vertically on both sides of it. Petermann Island at the southern end carries the southernmost Gentoo Penguin colony most voyages reach, and Adelie Penguin nests beside them on the higher rock. Imperial Shag breeds on the Yalour Islands a few kilometres further south.

  • Habitat: A steep-sided channel choked with icebergs, opening south into an archipelago of low rocky islands that carry gentoo and Adelie colonies.
  • When: December to February. Ice blocks the channel early in the season and ships take the outside route around Booth Island instead.
  • Getting there: The channel is transited by expedition ship when the ice allows, and landings on Petermann Island are made by inflatable boat. Deep snow lies on the island into January and movement ashore is slow.
  • Base yourself in: On board the ship
  • Look for: Gentoo Penguin, Adelie Penguin, Imperial Shag, Kelp Gull, Snowy Sheathbill, South Polar Skua, Brown Skua, Antarctic Tern, Wilson's Storm Petrel, Southern Giant Petrel
  • Stops:
    • : The southernmost landing on most peninsula voyages, at 65 degrees 10 minutes south. Gentoo Penguin nests near the landing place and a small Adelie Penguin colony sits on the higher rock behind it.
    • : A low island at the southern end of the Lemaire Channel, with a shallow bay where grounded icebergs collect. Inflatable boats cruise among them and leopard seals lie on the floes.
    • : A scatter of low rocks south of Petermann Island holding one of the more reachable Adelie Penguin colonies. Imperial Shag breeds on the same rocks.
    • : A Ukrainian station on Galindez Island in the Argentine Islands, an archipelago off the Antarctic Peninsula, taken over from a British base in 1996. The long record of measurements kept here led to the discovery of the ozone hole.
  • Also covers: Argentine Islands, Booth Island, Lemaire Channel, Petermann Island, Pleneau Island, Vernadsky Research Base, Yalour Islands
  • Also watch for: Crabeater and leopard seals rest on the floes that drift through the channel, and humpback whales feed in the same water in late summer.

The Weddell Sea and Snow Hill Island

The pack ice side of the peninsula, with the Adelie cities and an emperor colony far out on the ice

The Antarctic Sound opens into the north-western Weddell Sea, and heavy pack ice keeps ships out of it in many seasons. Paulet Island carries more than 100,000 pairs of Adelie Penguin, and Snow Petrel and Pintado Petrel nest on the cliff ledges at Brown Bluff. The Emperor Penguin colony on the fast ice off Snow Hill Island is reached by helicopter from an icebreaker in October and November, and the state of the sea ice decides whether it is reached at all.

  • Habitat: Pack ice and tabular icebergs over a shallow shelf, with volcanic cliffs and cobble beaches on the islands along the north-western edge.
  • When: October and November for the emperor colony. December to February for the Adelie colonies, when the chicks are on the beaches.
  • Getting there: Landings are made from an expedition ship and the sea ice governs the whole area. Snow Hill Island needs an icebreaker carrying helicopters, and voyages have turned back in successive seasons without reaching the colony.
  • Base yourself in: On board the ship
  • Look for: Adelie Penguin, Emperor Penguin, Snow Petrel, Pintado Petrel, Antarctic Petrel, Imperial Shag, Kelp Gull, Brown Skua, South Polar Skua, Snowy Sheathbill
  • Stops:
    • : More than 100,000 pairs of Adelie Penguin nest along the southern shore of this bay at the tip of the peninsula. The Esperanza station stands at its mouth and has families living in it all year.
    • : A cliff of brown volcanic rock 745 metres high on the mainland shore of the Antarctic Sound. Snow Petrel and Pintado Petrel nest on the ledges, and gentoo and Adelie penguins share the narrow beach below.
    • : A volcanic cone in the northern Weddell Sea carrying more than 100,000 pairs of Adelie Penguin. Imperial Shag breeds on the lower slopes and the stone hut from the 1903 Swedish expedition still stands.
    • : Devil Island is under two kilometres long and lies off the north coast of Vega Island, and about 8,000 Adelie Penguin nest on it. A short climb between its two peaks opens the view over the ice.
    • : The Emperor Penguin colony sits on the fast ice off the eastern side of the island and is reached by helicopter in October and November. Nordenskjold's 1902 hut stands on the island itself.
  • Also covers: Antarctic Sound, Brown Bluff, Devil Island, Hope Bay, James Ross Island, Paulet Island, Snow Hill Island, Vega Island
  • Also watch for: Leopard seals hunt the fledging penguins off Paulet Island in February, and Weddell seals lie on the gravel beaches through the summer.

The Ross Sea and McMurdo Sound

The far side of the continent on a month-long voyage, with the explorers' huts and the largest rookeries

The Ross Sea lies about 4,000 kilometres from the peninsula and is reached on voyages that sail south from Invercargill over roughly a month. Cape Adare carries the largest Adelie Penguin rookery on earth, and the Emperor Penguin colony under Cape Washington is among the largest anywhere. The huts left by Scott and Shackleton stand on Ross Island where the men walked out of them.

  • Habitat: Volcanic capes and moraine terraces along the edge of a frozen sea, with the Ross Ice Shelf closing the southern end in a wall of ice about 20 metres high.
  • When: January and February, which is when the pack ice opens far enough for a ship to get south.
  • Getting there: The voyage is made by ice-strengthened expedition ship and runs for about a month, with several days of open ocean at each end. Pack ice turns ships back from Cape Adare in many seasons and no landing on this coast is certain.
  • Base yourself in: On board the ship
  • Look for: Adelie Penguin, Emperor Penguin, South Polar Skua, Snow Petrel, Antarctic Petrel, Southern Fulmar, Pintado Petrel, Wilson's Storm Petrel, Antarctic Tern, Arctic Tern
  • Stops:
    • : The largest Adelie Penguin rookery on earth spreads across the beach at the western entrance to the Ross Sea. Borchgrevink's huts of 1899, the oldest buildings on the continent, stand among the birds.
    • : One of the largest Emperor Penguin colonies in the world sits on the sea ice under this headland in Terra Nova Bay. Ships pass it on the way south towards Ross Island.
    • : Shackleton's hut of 1907 stands on the westernmost point of Ross Island beside a colony of about 2,800 pairs of Adelie Penguin, which is the southernmost penguin colony in the world.
    • : An Adelie Penguin rookery and an Emperor Penguin colony sit at the eastern end of Ross Island where the Ross Ice Shelf meets the sea. Three men walked here from Cape Evans in the winter of 1911 to collect emperor eggs.
    • : A small volcanic island in the middle of the Ross Sea with an Adelie Penguin colony on its western beach. Ships call when the pack ice lets them approach it.
  • Also covers: Cape Adare, Cape Crozier, Cape Evans, Cape Royds, Cape Washington, Franklin Island, McMurdo Sound, Ross Island, Terra Nova Bay
  • Also watch for: Weddell seals breed on the fast ice in McMurdo Sound in October, and killer whales of the Ross Sea type hunt along the ice edge in January.

The Antarctic Circle and Marguerite Bay

South of 66 degrees, on the longer voyages, with sea ice and very few landings

Crossing the Antarctic Circle adds several days to a voyage, and the ships that do it work down the coast through Crystal Sound towards Marguerite Bay. Detaille Island is the usual goal, and wind and sea ice turn the landing back more often than they allow it. Adelie Penguin replaces Gentoo Penguin as the common penguin this far south, and Snow Petrel becomes a daily bird over the ice.

  • Habitat: Sea ice, ice-choked sounds and small rocky islets, with the mainland ice sheet reaching the shore along most of the coast.
  • When: Late January to early March, once the sea ice in Crystal Sound has broken up.
  • Getting there: The crossing is made by expedition ship on the longer peninsula voyages and the ice decides how far south a ship gets. A landing at Detaille Island is attempted rather than scheduled.
  • Base yourself in: On board the ship
  • Look for: Adelie Penguin, Gentoo Penguin, Snow Petrel, Antarctic Petrel, Southern Fulmar, Imperial Shag, South Polar Skua, Snowy Sheathbill, Kelp Gull, Wilson's Storm Petrel
  • Stops:
    • : The stretch of water the Antarctic Circle crosses, and the point at which most voyages turn around. Sea ice fills it into January and Snow Petrel feeds along its edges.
    • : A tiny island just south of the Antarctic Circle holding Base W, a British station abandoned in 1959 and left standing as it was. Wind and sea ice stop landings here more often than they allow them.
    • : The main British station on the peninsula, on Adelaide Island at the northern edge of Marguerite Bay. It is a working base rather than a landing site and a visit is arranged in advance.
    • : A rocky islet in Marguerite Bay with two abandoned bases on it, one British and one American. Sea ice reaches it in most seasons and few ships get this far south.
  • Also covers: Adelaide Island, Crystal Sound, Detaille Island, Marguerite Bay, Rothera Research Station, Stonington Island
  • Also watch for: Crabeater seals lie on the floes in Crystal Sound in numbers, and minke whales feed along the ice edge in February.

Frequently Asked Questions

When is the birding season in Antarctica?

Ships sail from late October to early March. October and November are the months for the emperor colony on the Weddell Sea ice and for penguins on eggs. December and January are the warmest weeks and the chicks are growing. February and March give the best whale watching and the clearest run south.

How do you get to Antarctica?

Almost every visitor sails from Ushuaia across the Drake Passage, which takes about two days each way. A small number of trips fly from Punta Arenas to the airstrip on King George Island and board a ship there. Ross Sea voyages sail from Invercargill and run for about a month.

How many bird species are there in Antarctica?

About forty species are seen on a peninsula voyage, and that figure counts the albatrosses, petrels and prions of the Drake Passage crossing. Only a handful of them breed on the continent itself, and one of those, the Emperor Penguin, breeds through the winter dark.

Can you see Emperor Penguins on an ordinary voyage?

No. The emperor colonies sit on fast ice well inside the pack, and the colony off Snow Hill Island needs an icebreaker carrying helicopters in October or November. Cape Washington in the Ross Sea is passed on the month-long voyages from Invercargill.

How close can you get to the wildlife?

The International Association of Antarctica Tour Operators sets minimum approach distances for every species and caps how many visitors may be ashore at a site at one time. Expedition guides enforce both, and the distances are revised from time to time, so the figure that counts is the one your expedition team gives on the day.

Do you need to be fit to go?

Every landing means climbing in and out of an inflatable boat in a swell, then walking on wet rock, deep snow or loose scoria. The walks are short and slow, and a visitor who is steady on their feet manages them.

Can I bird here with limited mobility?

Often yes, and it varies by site. A good deal of the birding here happens from roadsides, hides and lodge grounds rather than on long walks, and some reserves keep flat, surfaced trails. Ask a lodge or reserve directly about step free access, surfaces and seating before you book. The Birdability Map records those details for birding sites, and their Access Considerations guide lists the questions worth asking. Coverage is thin outside the United States, so read a blank area as unrecorded rather than unsuitable.
