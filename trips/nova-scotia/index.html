Birding in Nova Scotia

Maybank counts 464 species for the province and says more than 30 per cent of them are waifs and rarities, which is what a peninsula pushed out into the Atlantic halfway between the pole and the equator collects.

Birds that decide the trip

Bicknell's Thrush in the Cape Breton fir

One of two birds Maybank names as the province's specialities. It breeds in stunted summit fir and sings at the edges of the day.

When June, at dawn and dusk on the plateau.

Where The Cape Breton Highlands

The Ipswich Savannah Sparrow

The pale Ipswich race breeds only on Sable Island and winters along this coast. Maybank put it on his cover and left the island itself out of the book, because access is restricted.

When October to April, in coastal dune and beach grass.

Where Halifax and the eastern shore, Cape Sable Island and the south shore

Semipalmated Sandpiper at Evangeline Beach

The rising tide packs the southbound flocks into one roost on a single stretch of shingle, and when the shore floods they lift and go inland.

When August, on a rising tide.

Where The Minas Basin and the Fundy shore

Great Shearwater off Brier Island

The tide rip off the island end concentrates shearwaters and phalaropes, and the boats out of Westport reach it in under an hour.

When August and September, from the pelagic boats.

Where Brier Island and Digby Neck

Great Skua on the Fundy pelagics

It comes down from the north-east Atlantic in autumn and harries the shearwater flocks over the same tidal upwelling.

When September and October, from a boat.

Where Brier Island and Digby Neck

Harlequin Duck on the south shore rocks

It winters on broken rock in heavy surf, so the headlands rather than the harbours are where to look for it.

When December to March, on rock headlands.

Where Cape Sable Island and the south shore

Nelson's Sparrow in the eastern shore marsh

A thin hiss from deep salt marsh at dusk, and a high tide sometimes pushes it up onto the trail embankments in full view.

When June and July, in salt marsh at dusk.

Where Halifax and the eastern shore

Roseate Tern on the south shore islands

It nests among Common and Arctic Terns on a handful of islands here, at the northern end of its range on this coast.

When June and July, on the tern islands.

Where Cape Sable Island and the south shore

When to Go

Maybank makes the case for Nova Scotia in every season: cooler summers hold 22 breeding warblers among 180 breeding species, and a mild winter by Canadian standards means nearly 200 species are seen in the province between December and February. Autumn is when the rarities arrive.

The breeding season

June and early July

Twenty-two warblers breed here, and the Cape Breton highlands hold Bicknell's Thrush.

Go for The Cape Breton Highlands National Park trails and the interior forest.

Trade off The migrant traps are quiet and the shorebird flats are empty.

Typical daily average

The shorebird staging

Late July to early September

The Minas Basin tides concentrate Semipalmated Sandpipers into roosting flocks.

Go for Evangeline Beach on a rising tide, and Cape Sable Island.

Trade off Bloodworm harvesters and weekend walkers push the flocks off the beach.

Typical daily average

The autumn migrant traps

September to early November

Headlands and islands collect drift migrants and the rarities the province is known for.

Go for Hartlen Point, Brier Island and Cape Sable Island.

Trade off Fog is frequent on the Atlantic shore and can close a whole day.

Typical daily average

The winter coast

December to February

Nearly 200 species are recorded in the province across the three winter months.

Go for Halifax harbour, Chebucto Head and the south shore harbours.

Trade off Short days, and the highlands and interior are largely shut.

The autumn circuit

Halifax (YHZ) 2 nights, Wolfville 2 nights, Westport 3 nights, Barrington 2 nights, back to Halifax

Nine days from late August into September, on the three migrant traps and the shorebird roost. Halifax and Hartlen Point first, then the Minas Basin on a rising tide, then Brier Island for the pelagics, then the south-west corner. About 900 km and two cable ferries.

  1. 1Brier Island
  2. 2Westport
  3. 3Digby Neck

5 days

Brier Island and the Fundy pelagics

Halifax (YHZ), Westport 4 nights, back to Halifax

Four days on a basalt island at the end of Digby Neck in early September. Shearwaters, storm petrels and phalaropes over the tide rip on the boat days, and a migrant fall in the island thickets on the days the sea is too rough to sail.

  1. 1Cape Breton Highlands National Park
  2. 2Ingonish
  3. 3Cheticamp
  4. 4Pleasant Bay

6 days

The Cape Breton highlands in June

Sydney (YQY), Ingonish 2 nights, Cheticamp 3 nights, back to Sydney

Five days on the Cabot Trail in the middle of June for the breeding warblers and Bicknell's Thrush in the summit fir. Walk the Skyline and Benjies Lake trails at dawn, and drive the trail itself slowly, because the moose use it too.

Brier Island and Digby Neck

The last fragment of a basalt ridge, out in the Fundy tide where the seabirds feed

North Mountain runs the length of the Fundy shore and then breaks into three pieces at its western end, the last of which is Brier Island. Maybank calls it justifiably famous. It catches tired migrants, it looks straight out over the sea from the lighthouse point, and it is where the Fundy pelagic boats sail from, and the tide rip offshore concentrates shearwaters and phalaropes in numbers.

  • Habitat: Basalt headland and cobble shore, spruce and alder thicket, sphagnum bog and a lighthouse point, with a strong tide rip offshore.
  • When: Late August to early October for the pelagics and the migrant fall, with the boats running from June.
  • Getting there: Halifax is the airport, four hours away. Route 217 runs the length of Digby Neck, and two short cable ferries connect Long Island and Brier Island. They run on the half hour and neither takes a booking.
  • Base yourself in: Westport
  • Look for: Great Shearwater, Sooty Shearwater, Manx Shearwater, Wilson's Storm Petrel, Leach's Storm Petrel, Red-necked Phalarope, Pomarine Jaeger, Parasitic Jaeger, Great Skua, Black-legged Kittiwake, Razorbill, Blackpoll Warbler
  • Parks and reserves: Brier Island Conservation Lands (Protected area), Savary Provincial Park (State or provincial park), Annapolis Basin Look Off Provincial Park (State or provincial park), Lake Midway Provincial Park (State or provincial park), Smiths Cove Look Off Provincial Park (State or provincial park)
  • Also covers: Annapolis Basin Look Off Provincial Park, Brier Island Conservation Lands, Digby Neck, Lake Midway Provincial Park, Savary Provincial Park, Smiths Cove Look Off Provincial Park, Westport
  • Also watch for: Right whales, humpbacks and finbacks feed in the same tidal upwelling that draws the shearwaters, and the island whale boats run from the same wharf.

Halifax and the eastern shore

The best mainland migrant trap in the province, on a golf course beside a naval point

Hartlen Point guards the northern entrance to Halifax Harbour and pushes well out into the Atlantic, and Maybank rates it above every other headland on the Nova Scotia mainland for autumn migrants. The golf course is part of why: open fairways discourage a tired bird from dispersing inland. Cole Harbour further along the eastern shore matches Cape Sable Island for shorebirds and gets far less fog.

  • Habitat: Golf fairway and cobble beach, alder thicket and spruce bog, salt marsh and mud flat, and open Atlantic off the headland.
  • When: September and October for the migrant fall, and December to February for gulls and sea ducks in the harbour.
  • Getting there: Halifax airport is forty minutes away and the city is the base. Hartlen Point is defence land with tolerated access, so yield to golfers and stay off the fairways when play is on.
  • Base yourself in: Halifax
  • Look for: Nelson's Sparrow, Savannah Sparrow, Semipalmated Sandpiper, Ruddy Turnstone, Grey Plover, White-rumped Sandpiper, Northern Harrier, Osprey, Bald Eagle, Iceland Gull, Glaucous Gull, Bonaparte's Gull
  • Parks and reserves: Cole Harbour-Lawrencetown Coastal Heritage Park (Protected area), Duncans Cove Nature Reserve, Waverley - Salmon River Long Lake Wilderness Area (Protected area), Terence Bay Wilderness Area (Protected area), McNabs and Lawlors Island Provincial Park (State or provincial park)
  • Also covers: Chebucto Head, Cole Harbour-Lawrencetown Coastal Heritage Park, Conrad Beach, Duncans Cove Nature Reserve, Hartlen Point, McNabs and Lawlors Island Provincial Park, Terence Bay Wilderness Area, Waverley - Salmon River Long Lake Wilderness Area
  • Also watch for: Hartlen Point belongs to the Department of National Defence, and birders are tolerated there partly because their presence discourages illegal duck shooting.

The Minas Basin and the Fundy shore

Mud flats under the world's largest tides, where the rising water packs the sandpipers into one roost

The Minas Basin is the head of the Bay of Fundy and takes the highest tides on earth, and the churn enriches the mud flats enough to feed the southbound shorebird passage. The advancing tide concentrates the flocks along one stretch of shore at Evangeline Beach, where they form temporary roosts, and when the shoreline floods completely they lift and go inland.

  • Habitat: Tidal mud flat and shingle beach under extreme tides, salt marsh, diked farmland, and basalt cliff at the cape.
  • When: August into early September, on a rising tide. Avoid weekends, when walkers and dogs move the flocks off.
  • Getting there: Halifax is the airport, an hour east. Evangeline Beach is reached on Grand Pre Road with public parking at the end, cottages line the shore either side, and bloodworm harvesters walk the mud at medium tide.
  • Base yourself in: Wolfville
  • Look for: Semipalmated Sandpiper, Semipalmated Plover, Least Sandpiper, Short-billed Dowitcher, Red Knot, Hudsonian Whimbrel, Grey Plover, White-rumped Sandpiper, Peregrine Falcon, Bald Eagle, Common Tern, Willet
  • Parks and reserves: Blomidon Provincial Park (State or provincial park), Cape Split Provincial Park (State or provincial park), Wolfville Watershed Nature Preserve, Cheverie (Protected area), Scots Bay (Protected area)
  • Also covers: Blomidon Provincial Park, Cape Split Provincial Park, Cheverie, Evangeline Beach, Grand Pre, Scots Bay, Wolfville Watershed Nature Preserve
  • Also watch for: The basalt of North Mountain rises to 200 m at Blomidon and shelters the Annapolis Valley behind it, which is why the valley grows fruit at this latitude.

The Cape Breton Highlands

A boreal plateau over the sea, holding the one thrush that breeds nowhere but the north-east

The highlands are a high boreal plateau that drops straight to the sea on both coasts, and the Cabot Trail runs around the edge of them. Bicknell's Thrush breeds in the stunted summit fir, and pelagic trips from Pleasant Bay reach shearwater water within an hour.

  • Habitat: Stunted summit fir and spruce, boreal forest on the slopes, barrens and bog on the plateau, and sea cliff on both coasts.
  • When: June and the first half of July for the breeding birds, with the thrush singing at dawn and dusk.
  • Getting there: Sydney is the nearest airport, or Halifax and a five-hour drive. The national park charges a day fee, the Cabot Trail is paved throughout, and the Skyline and Benjies Lake trails are the standard highland walks.
  • Base yourself in: Cheticamp
  • Look for: Bicknell's Thrush, Blackburnian Warbler, Bay-breasted Warbler, Cape May Warbler, Canada Warbler, Mourning Warbler, Spruce Grouse, Boreal Chickadee, Black-backed Woodpecker, American Three-toed Woodpecker, Canada Jay, Northern Fulmar
  • Parks and reserves: Cape Breton Highlands National Park, Polletts Cove - Aspy Fault Wilderness Area (Protected area), Jim Campbells Barren Wilderness Area (Protected area), Cape Smokey Provincial Park (State or provincial park), North Bay (Protected area)
  • Also covers: Cape Breton Highlands National Park, Cape Smokey Provincial Park, Cheticamp, Ingonish, Jim Campbells Barren Wilderness Area, North Bay, Pleasant Bay, Polletts Cove - Aspy Fault Wilderness Area
  • Also watch for: Moose are abundant on the plateau and Maybank warns against approaching bulls in the autumn rut, and against driving the Cabot Trail at dawn or dusk.

Cape Sable Island and the south shore

The south-west corner, where the shorebird numbers match anywhere in the province and the strays pile up

The south-western tip is the first land a bird carried across the Atlantic reaches, and the province's rarity record is largely written here. Maybank rates Cape Sable Island's shorebird diversity and numbers as the equal of anywhere in Nova Scotia, with the caveat that the fog is worse. It is joined to the mainland by a causeway and still counts as an island.

  • Habitat: Sand beach and barrier bar, salt marsh and tidal flat, rock headland and cobble, and cold inshore water.
  • When: September and October for the shorebirds and the strays, and January and February for the sea ducks and alcids.
  • Getting there: Halifax is the airport, three hours away, or Yarmouth for anyone arriving in Nova Scotia on the ferry from Maine. The causeway to Cape Sable Island is free and open, and the beaches hold nesting plovers behind rope in summer.
  • Base yourself in: Barrington
  • Look for: Piping Plover, Roseate Tern, Purple Sandpiper, Red Knot, Harlequin Duck, King Eider, Barrow's Goldeneye, Long-tailed Duck, Little Auk, Iceland Gull, Great Black-backed Gull, Snowy Owl
  • Parks and reserves: Shelburne River Wilderness Area (Protected area), Bowers Meadows Wilderness Area (Protected area), Roseway River Wilderness Area (Protected area), Bon Portage Conservation Lands (Protected area), The Islands Provincial Park (State or provincial park)
  • Also covers: Baccaro Point, Bon Portage Conservation Lands, Bowers Meadows Wilderness Area, Cape Sable Island, Roseway River Wilderness Area, Shelburne River Wilderness Area, The Islands Provincial Park, Yarmouth
  • Also watch for: The cold Nova Scotia Current runs along this shore and holds sea ducks and alcids close inshore through the winter.

Frequently Asked Questions

When is the best time to go birding in Nova Scotia?

Late August into October. The shorebird staging on the Minas Basin runs through August, the migrant traps fill from September, and the rarities the province is known for arrive in that window. June is the other answer, for the breeding warblers and Bicknell's Thrush.

What is the best migrant trap?

Hartlen Point, on the north side of the entrance to Halifax Harbour. Maybank calls it the best on the mainland: it runs well out into the Atlantic, it holds mud flat, cobble, alder, spruce bog and hardwood in a small area, and its golf fairways discourage tired migrants from moving inland.

Can I visit Sable Island?

Not easily. It is a protected area with restricted access, which is why Maybank left it out of his guide despite its extraordinary list. The Ipswich race of the Savannah Sparrow breeds there and nowhere else, and winters on the coast, so the bird is reachable even when the island is not.

How do I time the Minas Basin shorebirds?

Arrive well before high water and let the tide do the work. The advancing water concentrates the flocks along one stretch at Evangeline Beach, and when the shoreline floods completely they fly inland to roost. Pick a weekday, because walkers and dogs move them off.

Is there anything in winter?

Nearly 200 species between December and February, which is a mild winter by Canadian standards. Sea ducks, alcids and white-winged gulls hold along the Nova Scotia south shore and in Halifax harbour, and the Ipswich sparrows are in the dunes.

Can I bird here with limited mobility?

Sites on this page differ from each other more than any rating could carry, so this is the framework the standard British guides use, and the reserve's own page and warden hold the answers.

  • Permission · some reserves open ground to disabled visitors that is closed to everyone else, and it is granted by asking.
  • Priority · parking bays, hides and the nearest paths are often held for disabled visitors. Ask what is reserved and how to claim it.
  • Parking · how close a car reaches the birds shapes the whole day. Ring ahead for the nearest hard standing to the water or the hide.
  • Paths · surfaces, gradients and gate widths vary inside a single reserve. Ask which trail is boarded, surfaced or level.
  • Perches · a hide with a low window, or a bench where the view opens, turns a viewpoint into a morning.
