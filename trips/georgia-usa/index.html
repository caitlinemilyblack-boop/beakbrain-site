Atlas / Georgia

Birding in Georgia

Georgia runs from the southern end of the Appalachians to the largest blackwater swamp in North America, and puts the best inland migration watch in the south-east in the middle of Atlanta on the way.

Birds that decide the trip

Cerulean Warbler at Kennesaw Mountain

It is a common autumn migrant on this one hill and largely unknown as a fall bird anywhere else, which is a discovery the guide's own author helped make. Spring counts here are high too.

When Mid-August to early October, on the summit and ridge trail at first light.

Where Kennesaw Mountain and the piedmont

Red-cockaded Woodpecker on the army ground

Fort Stewart held 215 active clusters in one season and Fort Benning 197, which is most of what the state has. Piedmont refuge and the forest beside it add fifty more and need no pass at all.

When May to early July, at first light around the cavity trees.

Where The longleaf pine and the woodpecker forests

Piping Plover on the Altamaha flats

Up to eighty winter on the delta, which is half the state total for the species. They feed on the intertidal flats and roost above the tideline on the island beaches.

When November to March, on a falling tide.

Where The coast and the barrier islands

Painted Bunting at Harris Neck

Males sing from the edges of the old runways and the live oak margins, and there are few easier places anywhere to see one well. They arrive in April and are gone by late summer.

When Late April to July, along the refuge loop road.

Where The coast and the barrier islands

Sandhill Crane over the Okefenokee

The Georgia swamp holds a resident population year round and takes a large wintering flock on top of it, and the calls carry a long way over flat peat and open water.

When December to February for the wintering birds, all year for the residents.

Where The Okefenokee and the south Georgia swamps

Swallow-tailed Kite over the swamp edge

It hunts low over the cypress and the pine flats without seeming to beat a wing, and drinks on the wing off the surface of the open water. Gone south again by early September.

When March to August, over the swamp margins and the river bottoms.

Where The Okefenokee and the south Georgia swamps

Bachman's Sparrow in the wiregrass

It sings a long clear whistle from open longleaf understorey that fire has kept clear, and stays out of sight almost completely once it stops. The same burns that hold the woodpeckers hold this.

When April to July, at dawn from recently burned ground.

Where The longleaf pine and the woodpecker forests

Canada Warbler at its southern limit in Georgia

Above two thousand feet in the north Georgia mountains the forest turns cool enough for it, and this is about as far south as the species breeds anywhere.

When Late April to July, in rhododendron thickets in the coves.

Where The Blue Ridge in north Georgia

When to Go

Georgia gives something in every month, but the peaks sit apart. Spring works everywhere at once in late April, autumn belongs to Kennesaw Mountain, and the coast and the swamp are at their best in the cool months when everywhere else has gone quiet.

Spring passage and breeding

April and May

Migrants move through the piedmont and the mountain and swamp breeders arrive together.

Go for Kennesaw Mountain in early April, then the Okefenokee, then the Blue Ridge from late April.

Trade off The coast is emptying of its wintering shorebirds.

Typical daily average

The breeding season

May to early July

The woodpecker clusters are at the nest and the Painted Buntings are singing on the coast.

Go for Piedmont refuge and the army installations, Harris Neck for buntings and heron colonies.

Trade off It is hot and humid, and the swamp insects are at their worst.

Typical daily average

The autumn passage

Mid-August to mid-October

Kennesaw Mountain takes the southbound landbirds, Cerulean Warbler among them.

Go for Kennesaw at first light, and the coast for returning shorebirds.

Trade off The mountains fill with leaf-colour traffic from late September.

Typical daily average

The cool months

November to March

Piping Plovers and Red Knots hold the Altamaha delta and Sandhill Cranes winter on the Okefenokee.

Go for Cumberland Island, the Altamaha delta, and the swamp for cranes.

Trade off The mountain breeders have gone and the piedmont is quiet.

10 days

Mountains, piedmont, swamp and coast

Atlanta (ATL), Blairsville 2 nights, Marietta 1 night, Macon 2 nights, Folkston 2 nights, Brunswick 3 nights, out of Savannah (SAV)

The full length of the state in one line, best run in the first half of May. Start in the Blue Ridge for the northern breeders at their southern limit, drop through Kennesaw and the Piedmont woodpecker forests, spend two nights on the Okefenokee, then finish on the barrier islands. About 700 miles of driving with no backtracking.

The pine forests and the Blue Ridge

Atlanta (ATL), Macon 3 nights, Blairsville 3 nights, back to Atlanta

A June week that pairs the two halves of Georgia holding breeding specialities. Piedmont refuge for Red-cockaded Woodpecker and Bachman's Sparrow with no permit needed, then north into the mountains for Canada Warbler and Ruffed Grouse at the southern edge of their range.

The coast and the barrier islands

Live oak, salt marsh and a hundred miles of undeveloped barrier island, holding half the state's wintering Piping Plovers

The Altamaha delta takes up to eighty wintering Piping Plovers, half the state total, and as many as twelve thousand Red Knots pass through at once. Cumberland is a national seashore reached only by ferry, and Harris Neck behind it puts heron colonies, freshwater swamp and singing Painted Buntings within one short loop road.

  • Habitat: Sandy beach and intertidal flat, dune grassland, salt marsh, maritime live oak forest behind the dunes, and bottomland hardwood along the river.
  • When: September to April for the shorebirds and waterbirds, and May to July for the Painted Buntings at Harris Neck.
  • Getting there: Jacksonville in Florida or Savannah are the airports and Brunswick the base. Cumberland is a 45-minute passenger ferry from St. Marys with reservations advised, and Wolf Island refuge in the delta is closed to the public.
  • Base yourself in: Brunswick
  • Look for: Piping Plover, Red Knot, Wilson's Plover, American Oystercatcher, Black Skimmer, Clapper Rail, Painted Bunting, Wood Stork, Roseate Spoonbill, King Rail, Marbled Godwit, Sanderling
  • Also covers: Altamaha River Delta, Cumberland Island National Seashore, Harris Neck National Wildlife Refuge, Jekyll Island, Tybee Island
  • Also watch for: Loggerhead turtles nest along the seashore beaches and feral horses range Cumberland Island.

The Okefenokee and the south Georgia swamps

Four hundred thousand acres of blackwater peat bog, entered by boardwalk or canoe

The Okefenokee is the largest blackwater swamp in North America, and it is the reason many people come to the state at all. Cypress prairie, peat islands and open water hold Sandhill Cranes through the winter. The smaller bays and lakes to the west offer much the same list with far fewer people.

  • Habitat: Blackwater peat bog with cypress prairie, peat batteries that float, open lakes, and longleaf pine on the sand ridges around the rim.
  • When: March to May, when the swamp birds are breeding and the heat and insects have not yet peaked. December to February for the cranes.
  • Getting there: Jacksonville is the airport and Folkston the base. The east entrance has a boardwalk and canoe hire, Stephen C. Foster on the west side is a long drive round, and repellent is not optional.
  • Base yourself in: Folkston
  • Look for: Sandhill Crane, Anhinga, Limpkin, Purple Gallinule, Least Bittern, Wood Duck, Prothonotary Warbler, Swallow-tailed Kite, Barred Owl, Red-headed Woodpecker, Bald Eagle, Wood Stork
  • Also covers: Banks Lake National Wildlife Refuge, Grand Bay Wildlife Management Area, Okefenokee National Wildlife Refuge
  • Also watch for: American alligators are abundant and easily seen on the Georgia swamp, and black bear use the drier peat islands.

The longleaf pine and the woodpecker forests

More than four hundred active Red-cockaded Woodpecker clusters, most of them on army ground

Fort Stewart held 215 active clusters in one breeding season and Fort Benning 197, which makes the two installations the most important ground for the species in the state by a wide margin. Piedmont refuge and the experimental forest beside it add another fifty or so and are far easier to visit.

  • Habitat: Longleaf pine and wiregrass sandhill kept open by fire, slash pine flatwoods, cypress and gum ponds, and riverine bottomland hardwood.
  • When: May to early July for the woodpeckers at the nest, though the pinewood birds are worth a visit at any time of year.
  • Getting there: Atlanta is the airport and Macon the base for Piedmont refuge, which needs no permit. Fort Stewart is open with an access pass and a fee from the Pass and Permit Office, and both installations close areas for training without notice.
  • Base yourself in: Macon
  • Look for: Red-cockaded Woodpecker, Bachman's Sparrow, Brown-headed Nuthatch, Wood Thrush, Loggerhead Shrike, Yellow-throated Vireo, Prothonotary Warbler, Northern Parula, Summer Tanager, Blue Grosbeak, Chuck-will's-widow, Swainson's Warbler
  • Also covers: Fort Benning, Fort Stewart, Hitchiti Experimental Forest, Piedmont National Wildlife Refuge
  • Also watch for: Gopher tortoise dig the sandhills and their burrows shelter indigo snakes and a long list of other animals.

Kennesaw Mountain and the Atlanta ponds

The best inland migration watch in the south-east, and an autumn Cerulean Warbler count found nowhere else

Kennesaw Mountain rises out of suburban Atlanta and catches migrant landbirds in numbers that no other inland site in the region matches. Its most surprising find is Cerulean Warbler in autumn, common here and largely unknown as a fall migrant anywhere else.

  • Habitat: Mature oak and hickory on a monadnock ridge, open battlefield grassland below it, and treated water ponds and constructed wetland on the piedmont south of the city.
  • When: Early April to mid-May and mid-August to mid-October. The autumn passage is the better of the two here.
  • Getting there: Atlanta is the airport, twenty miles away, and Marietta the base. The mountain-top road is closed to private cars at busy times, so the walk up is the usual approach.
  • Base yourself in: Marietta
  • Look for: Cerulean Warbler, Blackburnian Warbler, Bay-breasted Warbler, Tennessee Warbler, Chestnut-sided Warbler, Black-and-white Warbler, Scarlet Tanager, Rose-breasted Grosbeak, Wood Thrush, Worm-eating Warbler, Hooded Warbler, Veery
  • Also covers: E. L. Huie Land Application Facility, Kennesaw Mountain National Battlefield Park, Newman Wetlands Center
  • Also watch for: White-tailed deer are common on the battlefield and coyote work the field edges at dusk.

The Blue Ridge in north Georgia

Where the Appalachians run out, and with them the southern edge of a dozen northern breeding ranges

The mountains end in the top corner of the state, and Brasstown Bald at 4,784 feet is the highest ground in it. Above about two thousand feet the forest turns cool enough for birds that mostly breed much further north, and several reach their southern limit here.

  • Habitat: Mixed mesophytic forest on the slopes, oak and hickory on the ridges, rhododendron thickets in the coves, and small grassy balds on the highest ground.
  • When: Late April to July for the breeding birds. October brings heavy leaf-colour traffic and the roads fill.
  • Getting there: Atlanta is the airport, about two hours south, and Blairsville the base. A paved road climbs to a car park below Brasstown Bald with a steep half-mile walk to the summit, and the Cohutta is a wilderness reached on foot.
  • Base yourself in: Blairsville
  • Look for: Canada Warbler, Blackburnian Warbler, Veery, Black-throated Green Warbler, Chestnut-sided Warbler, Golden-winged Warbler, Ruffed Grouse, Northern Raven, Worm-eating Warbler, Hooded Warbler, Scarlet Tanager, Barred Owl
  • Also covers: Brasstown Bald, Chattahoochee National Forest, Cohutta Wilderness
  • Also watch for: Black bear are common through the national forest and the Cohutta wilderness holds one of the larger populations in the state.

Frequently Asked Questions

When is the best time to go birding in Georgia?

The first half of May works everywhere at once: mountain breeders, swamp birds and woodpeckers all at the same time. The second half of September is better if Kennesaw Mountain is the point of the trip, and November to March is the coast at its best.

Why is Kennesaw Mountain so good for migrants?

It is an isolated ridge rising out of suburban Atlanta with mature hardwood on it, so southbound landbirds concentrate there in numbers no other inland site in the south-east matches. Its Cerulean Warbler counts in autumn are the notable part, because the species is barely recorded as a fall migrant elsewhere.

Do I need a permit for Fort Stewart or Fort Benning?

Fort Stewart needs an access pass and a fee from the Pass and Permit Office, and Fort Benning is open with some areas restricted. Both close ground for training without notice. Piedmont National Wildlife Refuge holds the same birds and needs nothing at all, which makes it the easier first choice.

How do I get to Cumberland Island?

By passenger ferry from St. Marys, about 45 minutes, with reservations recommended. There is no vehicle crossing, and the island has marked trails and very little else.

Is the Okefenokee worth a full day?

Two, if you can. The east entrance near Folkston has a boardwalk and canoe hire and covers the open prairies, and the Stephen C. Foster entrance on the west side is a long drive round but reaches different water. Take insect repellent for either.

Can I bird here with limited mobility?

Sites on this page differ from each other more than any rating could carry, so this is the framework the standard British guides use, and the reserve's own page and warden hold the answers.

  • Permission · some reserves open ground to disabled visitors that is closed to everyone else, and it is granted by asking.
  • Priority · parking bays, hides and the nearest paths are often held for disabled visitors. Ask what is reserved and how to claim it.
  • Parking · how close a car reaches the birds shapes the whole day. Ring ahead for the nearest hard standing to the water or the hide.
  • Paths · surfaces, gradients and gate widths vary inside a single reserve. Ask which trail is boarded, surfaced or level.
  • Perches · a hide with a low window, or a bench where the view opens, turns a viewpoint into a morning.

Every reserve above with its own link carries its access detail there, and a warden answers what a page cannot.
