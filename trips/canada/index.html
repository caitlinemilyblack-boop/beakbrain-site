Birds that decide the trip

Atlantic Puffin at Witless Bay

Witless Bay holds more than 260,000 pairs, the largest Atlantic Puffin colony in North America, on four low islands a few kilometres off the Avalon peninsula. The islands can only be seen from the water, and the boats leave from Bay Bulls half an hour south of St John's.

When The nesting season runs from 1 April to 1 September, and boat regulations apply throughout it.

Where Witless Bay, St. John's

Northern Gannet on Bonaventure Island

Around 110,000 gannets breed on Bonaventure Island off Perce, which makes it the most accessible large colony of the species anywhere. The boat around the island also passes Black-legged Kittiwake, Razorbill, Atlantic Puffin, Black Guillemot and Common Murre.

When Gannets return at the beginning of April and stay to the end of September. Boats run from early June to mid October.

Where Bonaventure Island, Forillon National Park

Burrowing Owl in Grasslands National Park

Grasslands National Park is possibly the only place left in Saskatchewan where Burrowing Owl can be counted on. Look carefully in the black-tailed prairie dog colonies, because the owls are close to the prairie dogs in size and colour and are easy to miss.

When Late May through July, while the birds are nesting.

Where Grasslands National Park

Semipalmated Sandpiper at Mary's Point

Up to two million Semipalmated Sandpipers stage at the head of the Bay of Fundy in a season, close to three quarters of the world population, with as many as 200,000 present at once. They feed on the mud shrimp Corophium volutator, which reaches 60,000 per square metre here, and roughly double their weight before flying on.

When The first birds arrive in mid July and passage runs to October, with August the peak.

Where Mary's Point

Willow Ptarmigan around Churchill

Both Willow Ptarmigan and Rock Ptarmigan occur around Churchill, where the boreal forest gives out onto Hudson Bay. The road system is short enough that a few days covers every habitat the town reaches.

When Present year round, and most visible in autumn and early winter alongside Gyrfalcon and Snowy Owl.

Where Churchill, Cape Merry

Prothonotary Warbler in the Carolinian woods

The Carolinian forest of the Lake Erie shore carries a set of southern birds that reach Canada only here. Point Pelee and Rondeau are the two places to try, and the peninsula concentrates migrants onto a few square kilometres.

When May, when the park opens earlier and closes later to take the migration.

Where Point Pelee National Park, Rondeau Provincial Park

Sage Thrasher in the Okanagan

The antelope-brush and sagebrush of the southern Okanagan is the only shrub-steppe of its kind in Canada, and it carries Sage Thrasher, Brewer's Sparrow and Lark Sparrow at the northern edge of their range.

When Late May and June, while the shrub-steppe birds are singing.

Where Osoyoos, Oliver, Vaseux Lake

Gyrfalcon on the Dempster

The Dempster Highway is the one road into the arctic tundra, and it is a good route for the large predators. Gyrfalcon, Peregrine Falcon and Golden Eagle all hunt the country it crosses.

When Through the short summer season, when the tundra nesting birds are present.

Where Dempster Highway, Tombstone Territorial Park

When to Go

Canada spans six time zones and its birding year runs at different speeds across them. The southern British Columbia coast stays open all winter while the prairies and the north are frozen, and the subarctic season at Churchill and on the Dempster is only a few weeks long.

Spring passage

April to May

Migration moves north across the country and the southern parks fill before the breeders settle.

Go for Point Pelee and the Lake Erie shore take the migration the country is known for, the prairie potholes open, and the Okanagan shrub-steppe comes into song.

Trade off The north is still frozen, Churchill and the Yukon are out of reach, and May at Point Pelee is busy with visitors.

The breeding season

June to July

The short northern summer opens and every region is reachable at once.

Go for Churchill, the Dempster and the high mountains come into their few weeks, the seabird colonies are full, and the grassland birds are nesting.

Trade off This is peak tourist season in the mountain parks, and Riding Mountain and Banff are at their busiest.

August to October

Shorebirds come south first, then the seabird trips and the late passerine movement.

Trade off The tundra season is over by early September, and the mountain roads begin to close.

Victoria and southern Vancouver Island

Mild winters and a rocky shore that holds sea ducks within sight of the city

Victoria sits at the dry southern tip of Vancouver Island, where the rain shadow of the Olympic Mountains keeps winters mild and the sea stays open. Waterfowl, alcids and rocky-shore shorebirds gather within a short drive of downtown, and the lagoons and sewage ponds on the edge of town collect migrants and rarities.

  • Habitat: Coastal Douglas-fir and arbutus on dry hillsides, with Garry oak savannah surviving in fragments. The shoreline alternates rocky wave platforms with sheltered lagoons and tidal flats, and the Strait of Juan de Fuca offshore stays ice free all winter.
  • When: Winter is the strongest season, when sea ducks, loons, grebes and alcids fill the strait and the coast holds Black Turnstone, Surfbird and Rock Sandpiper. August and September bring shorebirds to Witty's Lagoon and the sewage ponds. Spring brings breeding Garry oak and coastal forest species.
  • Getting there: Victoria has its own airport and a ferry from Vancouver at Swartz Bay. Esquimalt Lagoon lies about 15 km west of downtown: take Douglas Street north onto Hwy 1, then Exit 10 toward Colwood. The Coho ferry from the Inner Harbour crosses to Port Angeles and the crossing itself produces alcids and gulls.
  • Base yourself in: Victoria
  • Look for: Harlequin Duck, Surf Scoter, Black Oystercatcher, Black Turnstone, Surfbird, Rock Sandpiper, Pigeon Guillemot, Marbled Murrelet, Rhinoceros Auklet, Bewick's Wren, Hutton's Vireo, Spotted Towhee, Chestnut-backed Chickadee, Anna's Hummingbird
  • National park: Witty's Lagoon Regional Park
  • Also covers: Cowichan Valley, Duncan, Saanich Peninsula, Sooke, Victoria, Witty's Lagoon Regional Park
  • Also watch for: Harbour and Steller sea lions haul out on the rocks, and orca and grey whale pass through the strait.

The west coast of Vancouver Island

Surf, rainforest and the pelagic trips that run out of Tofino and Ucluelet

The outer coast faces the open Pacific across Pacific Rim National Park Reserve. Long surf beaches take shorebirds and gulls, the rainforest behind them holds the wet-coast songbirds, and the boats that run offshore from Tofino and Ucluelet reach true pelagic water.

  • Habitat: Old-growth coastal temperate rainforest of western hemlock, western redcedar and Sitka spruce, fronted by long sand beaches, rocky headlands and estuaries. The continental shelf lies close enough offshore that seabird trips reach deep water in a few hours.
  • When: Autumn is the best time for pelagic trips, when seabird numbers are high and the weather stays settled. Shorebirds pass in spring and again from July to September, and Ucluelet turns up rarities in autumn. The Wickaninnish Interpretive Centre opens mid-March to mid-October.
  • Getting there: Highway 4 crosses the island from Port Alberni to the Ucluelet and Tofino junction. The Wickaninnish Interpretive Centre is signed 4.7 km northwest of that junction. The Tofino sewage lagoons sit 1 km further along the highway behind a yellow gate on the ocean side, and access there has tightened in recent years.
  • Base yourself in: Tofino
  • Look for: Sooty Shearwater, Pink-footed Shearwater, Fork-tailed Storm Petrel, Black-footed Albatross, Sanderling, Wandering Tattler, Cassin's Auklet, Tufted Puffin, American Pipit, Varied Thrush, Pacific Wren, Townsend's Warbler
  • National park: Pacific Rim National Park Reserve
  • Also covers: Pacific Rim National Park Reserve, Port Alberni, Port Renfrew, Tofino, Ucluelet
  • Also watch for: Grey whales pass on migration, black bears work the beaches at low tide, and sea otters have returned to the outer coast.

The Fraser delta and Vancouver

The winter raptor concentration that draws birders to Boundary Bay

The Fraser delta spreads into tidal flats, farmland and marsh south of Vancouver. Boundary Bay holds one of the largest winter gatherings of hawks, falcons, eagles and owls in North America, and the George C. Reifel sanctuary on Westham Island takes the Snow Goose flocks.

  • Habitat: Intertidal mudflat and salt marsh behind a dyke system, backed by farmland, weedy field margins and remnant stands of old trees. The delta stays unfrozen through winter, which is what concentrates the birds.
  • When: Winter is the season this region is known for. Upwards of 100,000 Dunlin feed on the tidal flats alongside Northern Pintail and American Wigeon, and the raptors follow them. Snow Goose flocks feed along the shoreline into autumn and winter. Wilson's Snipe, Pectoral Sandpiper and the occasional Sharp-tailed Sandpiper use the flooded paddocks from mid-September to mid-October.
  • Getting there: Vancouver International Airport sits on the delta itself. Reifel is reached through Ladner and across to Westham Island. The outer dyke is open at the western end of Westminster Highway, at the south end of Terra Nova Rural Park, and at the west end of Steveston Highway, where a small parking area serves a farm paddock that floods.
  • Base yourself in: Vancouver
  • Look for: Snow Goose, Trumpeter Swan, Northern Pintail, American Wigeon, Dunlin, Wilson's Snipe, Pectoral Sandpiper, Short-eared Owl, Snowy Owl, Northern Harrier, Peregrine Falcon, Bald Eagle, Sandhill Crane
  • National parks: George C. Reifel Migratory Bird Sanctuary, Boundary Bay Wildlife Management Area
  • Also covers: Boundary Bay Wildlife Management Area, George C. Reifel Migratory Bird Sanctuary, Iona Island, Reifel Bird Sanctuary, Richmond, Vancouver
  • Also watch for: Harbour seals use the river mouth, and coyote hunt the dyke edges through the winter.

The Okanagan and Similkameen

Canada's pocket desert, and the only place in the country for several southern birds

The Okanagan valley runs north from the Washington border as a chain of lakes between dry hillsides. Antelope-brush and sagebrush on the valley floor carry birds that reach Canada nowhere else, and the slopes climb through ponderosa pine into subalpine forest within a few kilometres.

  • Habitat: Sagebrush and antelope-brush shrub-steppe on the valley bottom, with ponderosa pine and Douglas-fir on the slopes above and interior cliffs and talus in the side valleys. Vaseux Lake and the river channels hold marsh and riparian cottonwood.
  • When: Late May and June are the peak, when the shrub-steppe species are singing and the mountain roads have opened. Winter brings Bohemian Waxwing flocks into town and Redhead, Greater Scaup and American Coot onto the unfrozen southern lakes.
  • Getting there: Highway 97 runs the length of the valley from Osoyoos at the border through Oliver, Okanagan Falls, Penticton, Kelowna and Vernon. Penticton and Kelowna both have airports. Manning Park lies west on Highway 3 and carries the subalpine species.
  • Base yourself in: Penticton
  • Look for: Sage Thrasher, Brewer's Sparrow, Lark Sparrow, Grasshopper Sparrow, Lazuli Bunting, Say's Phoebe, Western Kingbird, Canyon Wren, Rock Wren, White-headed Woodpecker, Lewis's Woodpecker, Common Poorwill, Bobolink, Long-billed Curlew
  • National parks: Vaseux Lake National Wildlife Area, E.C. Manning Provincial Park
  • Also covers: E.C. Manning Provincial Park, Manning Park, Okanagan Falls, Oliver, Osoyoos, Penticton, Vaseux Lake National Wildlife Area
  • Also watch for: Bighorn sheep on the valley cliffs, along with rattlesnake, badger and the rare Great Basin spadefoot.

The Kootenays and the British Columbia Rockies

A wetland in a mountain trench, and the passes over into Alberta

The Creston valley holds a large managed wetland between mountain ranges, and the highways east climb through Yoho, Kootenay and Mount Robson into the Rockies. The combination puts marsh birds and high-mountain species within a day of each other.

  • Habitat: Managed marsh and wet meadow on the Kootenay river flats at Creston, with mixed interior forest on the slopes. East of there the Rocky Mountain parks carry montane forest, subalpine fir and alpine tundra above the treeline.
  • When: May and June for the marsh at Creston and for the montane forest. The alpine opens later, from July, once the snowpack has gone.
  • Getting there: Highway 3 runs east from the Okanagan through the Kettle Valley and the West Kootenays to Creston. Highway 95 and the Trans-Canada carry on into Yoho and Kootenay national parks and over into Alberta. Mount Robson Provincial Park sits on Highway 16 west of Jasper.
  • Base yourself in: Creston
  • Look for: American Dipper, Harlequin Duck, Barrow's Goldeneye, Spruce Grouse, Dusky Grouse, White-tailed Ptarmigan, Grey-crowned Rosy Finch, Clark's Nutcracker, Boreal Chickadee, Northern Pygmy Owl, Forster's Tern, Black Tern
  • National parks: Yoho National Park, Kootenay National Park, Mount Robson Provincial Park, Creston Valley Wildlife Management Area
  • Also covers: Creston Valley Wildlife Management Area, Kootenay National Park, Mount Robson Provincial Park, Yoho National Park
  • Also watch for: Grizzly and black bear, elk, mountain goat and moose across the Rocky Mountain parks.

Haida Gwaii and the North Coast

Seabird colonies, an endemic subspecies and the wettest forest in Canada

Haida Gwaii lies far enough off the north coast to have gone its own way, with distinct forms of several birds and large seabird colonies on the outer islands. Prince Rupert and the Skeena valley on the mainland give the same wet forest with easier access.

Churchill and Hudson Bay

The spring migration everyone has heard of, at the southern tip of Canada

Point Pelee reaches further south than northern California, and the peninsula funnels migrants onto a few square kilometres of woodland and marsh. The park is a magnet for rare birds, with vagrants arriving from across the continent, and in May it fills with thousands of visiting birders.

  • Base yourself in: St. John's
  • Look for: Atlantic Puffin, Northern Gannet, Common Murre, Thick-billed Murre, Razorbill, Black-legged Kittiwake, Leach's Storm Petrel, Great Cormorant, Willow Ptarmigan, Boreal Chickadee, Pine Grosbeak
  • National parks: Witless Bay Ecological Reserve, Cape St. Mary's Ecological Reserve, Gros Morne National Park
  • Also covers: Cape St. Mary's Ecological Reserve, Gros Morne National Park, St. John's, Witless Bay Ecological Reserve
  • Also watch for: Humpback whales feed off the Avalon in summer, and moose and caribou are widespread inland.

The Yukon and the Dempster Highway

The only road into the arctic tundra, and a short summer to work in

The Dempster Highway is the one road that reaches Tombstone Territorial Park and carries on to the Arctic Circle and beyond. It crosses boreal forest, alpine tundra and arctic tundra in a single run, and the tundra nesting season it serves is very short.

  • Habitat: Boreal spruce forest at the southern end giving way to alpine and arctic tundra through Tombstone, then the Peel and Mackenzie lowlands further north. Kluane in the southwest is a large mountain wilderness with lakes, rivers and over 180 bird species recorded.
  • When: Early summer for the nesting ducks, geese, shorebirds and songbirds that use the brief tundra season, and for young animals. Midsummer brings the midnight sun. Autumn arrives early this close to the Arctic Circle and the tundra colours usually begin by late August.
  • Getting there: Whitehorse is the territorial airport. The Dempster leaves the Klondike Highway east of Dawson City and runs 736 km to Inuvik, unpaved the whole way, and it is the only road access to Tombstone. Kluane is reached from Whitehorse on the Alaska Highway toward Haines Junction.
  • Base yourself in: Whitehorse
  • Look for: Gyrfalcon, Peregrine Falcon, Golden Eagle, Willow Ptarmigan, Rock Ptarmigan, White-tailed Ptarmigan, Long-tailed Jaeger, American Golden Plover, Surfbird, Wandering Tattler, Smith's Longspur, Northern Wheatear, Bluethroat, Arctic Warbler, Harris's Sparrow
  • National parks: Tombstone Territorial Park, Kluane National Park and Reserve
  • Also covers: Dempster Highway, Haines Junction, Kluane National Park and Reserve, Tombstone Territorial Park, Whitehorse
  • Also watch for: Barren-ground caribou, grizzly bear, Dall sheep, moose and Arctic ground squirrel along the highway.

Can I bird here with limited mobility?

Sites on this page differ from each other more than any rating could carry, so this is the framework the standard British guides use, and the reserve's own page and warden hold the answers.

  • Permission · some reserves open ground to disabled visitors that is closed to everyone else, and it is granted by asking.
  • Priority · parking bays, hides and the nearest paths are often held for disabled visitors. Ask what is reserved and how to claim it.
  • Parking · how close a car reaches the birds shapes the whole day. Ring ahead for the nearest hard standing to the water or the hide.
  • Paths · surfaces, gradients and gate widths vary inside a single reserve. Ask which trail is boarded, surfaced or level.
  • Perches · a hide with a low window, or a bench where the view opens, turns a viewpoint into a morning.
