At a glance

Best months
April to late October, which is the window in which the escarpment forests are drivable
Regions
Kissama and the Luanda coastGabela and the Kumbira forestThe Seles road and BangoMount Moco and the Huambo highlandsTundavala and the Lubango escarpmentMalanje and the northern miombo
Routes
The Kumbira scarp11 daysMalanje and the northern miombo7 daysThe southern highlands: Tundavala and Mount Moco9 days
Getting in
Luanda takes the international flights and is the usual way in. The alternative is a long drive north in a 4WD from the south.
Getting around
Travel is expensive and a visitor has to be self sufficient. Portuguese is the working language and a 4WD is needed off the coast road. Landmines are a danger over large parts of the country and local advice on safe ground matters before leaving a made road.

When to Go

The scarp forests run on one clear rule. Kumbira and the Seles road are reliably drivable from April to late October, and the rains between November and March turn the Conda road to a quagmire and cut the forest off to anyone on foot. The coastal plain around Luanda and Kissama stays open all year.

Dry

April to October

The escarpment roads hold up and the forest is drivable, which is the whole window for the endemics.

Go for Kumbira for the Gabela birds, Red-crested Turaco and Pulitzer's Longbill, and the Seles road for more of the same at lower density.

Trade off The interior is hot and dry and the highland grassland above Mount Moco is at its bleakest.

Trade off The forests that hold almost every endemic are effectively out of reach without a long walk.

Kissama and the Luanda coast

Riparian forest, dry woodland and a lagoon coast south of the capital

Quiçama National Park, which many accounts still write as Kissama, lies between the Kissama and Longa rivers on the coastal plain and reopened to visitors only in the last few years. Its riparian forest and thicket carry several Angolan endemics within a morning of the international airport. Behind the Mussulo peninsula a long lagoon holds waders and terns, and the drive south from Luanda passes the whole of it.

  • Habitat: Riparian forest and thicket follow the Kissama and Longa rivers, dry woodland covers the ground behind them, and well grassed savanna runs out to rank vegetation around the wetland margins. A sand peninsula closes off a long shallow lagoon on the coast north of the park.
  • When: All year. Golden-backed Bishop is far easier to pick out of a flock while the males hold breeding plumage.
  • Getting there: The park lies about 75 km south of Luanda on the coast road. A local guide from the park camp is needed for Grey-striped Spurfowl, because the guides imitate its whistling call and bring the bird out of dense grass. The riverine vegetation on the southern boundary is reached by a short boat trip.
  • Base yourself in: Luanda
  • All year: Grey-striped Spurfowl, White-fronted Wattle-eye, Red-backed Mousebird, Rufous-tailed Palm Thrush, Golden-backed Bishop, Pale-olive Greenbul, Bubbling Cisticola, Gabela Helmetshrike, Swamp Boubou, Angola Batis, Palm-nut Vulture
  • In the breeding season: Golden-backed Bishop
  • National park:
    • Quiçama National Park, The park between the two rivers, and the easiest place in the country to see Grey-striped Spurfowl and White-fronted Wattle-eye.
  • Stops:
    • : A sand peninsula that closes off a shallow lagoon south west of the city. Waders and terns use the sheltered water behind it.
    • : Gardens and larger parks in the city hold Rufous-tailed Palm Thrush and Red-backed Mousebird, which is as much as the capital itself offers.
  • Also covers: Luanda, Mussulo, Quiçama National Park
  • Also watch for: Quicama's elephants are the ones Operation Noah's Ark brought back from Botswana and South Africa in 2001, after the war emptied the park, and Humpback Whales pass the coast offshore.

Gabela and the Kumbira forest

Scarp forest on Njelo Mountain, and the densest gathering of endemics in the country

Three birds carry the name of the small town of Gabela, and all three live on the western flank of Njelo Mountain above Conda. Kumbira runs from about 800 to 1,000 metres, with grassland and rocky outcrops on the slopes above and derelict shade coffee below the forest edge. Every western Angolan endemic except Swierstra's Spurfowl occurs somewhere on this one slope.

  • Habitat: Scarp forest covers the mountain flank between about 800 and 1,000 metres. Grassland with rocky outcrops runs above it, and a mixture of subsistence farming and abandoned shade coffee plantation lies below the forest edge.
  • When: April to late October. The road from Conda towards Sumbe turns to a quagmire in the rains.
  • Getting there: Gabela is reached on a tar road that leaves the coast road about 20 km north of Sumbe and follows the Keve River, then breaks up as it climbs the escarpment beyond the Keve Falls. Kumbira is reached from Conda on a track leaving the north western side of the town. A local guide is needed inside the forest for the network of footpaths and old plantation tracks, and permission from the villagers is needed to camp there. In the rains the forest is a 9 km walk from Conda.
  • Base yourself in: Conda
  • Look for: Red-crested Turaco, Gabela Akalat, Angola Cave Chat, Pulitzer's Longbill, Gabela Bushshrike, Monteiro's Bushshrike, Angola Slaty Flycatcher, White-fronted Wattle-eye, Hartert's Camaroptera, Falkenstein's Greenbul, Gabon Coucal, Yellow-throated Nicator
  • Stops:
    • : The forest on the western flank of Njelo Mountain, and the site that holds almost the whole western endemic list.
    • : The town the forest track leaves from. The more open ground around it holds Compact Weaver, Dusky Twinspot and Red-headed Bluebill.
    • : The town that gives its name to an akalat, a bushshrike and a helmetshrike, all three confined to this corner of Cuanza Sul.
    • : The coastal town where the road inland to the escarpment begins.
  • Also covers: Conda, Gabela, Kumbira, Sumbe
  • Also watch for: Kumbira has a bushbaby of its own, Galagoides kumbirensis, described in 2017 and named for the forest.

The Seles road and Bango

Unpromising patches of secondary bush on the southern scarp, holding the endemics anyway

Many of the Kumbira birds also live along the road that runs inland from Sumbe to Seles. The bush around the small village of Bango looks thin and carries large numbers of Pulitzer's Longbill along with a few Gabela Akalat. West of Bango the road drops through tall forest and dense woodland, and the patches on the coastal side of the village hold much the same list.

  • Habitat: Secondary bush and thicket line the road on the plateau, and taller forest and dense woodland cover the lower ground to the west.
  • When: April to late October, in step with the rest of the escarpment.
  • Getting there: Bango lies about 14 km west of Seles and can be reached directly from the coast at Sumbe. The road from Conda to Seles runs through Ganja and a local guide or an interpreter is needed to follow it. Road conditions on this stretch are gruelling in every season.
  • Base yourself in: Sumbe
  • Look for: Pulitzer's Longbill, Gabela Akalat, Red-crested Turaco, White-fronted Wattle-eye
  • Stops:
    • : A small village west of Seles. The roadside bush around it carries more Pulitzer's Longbill than anywhere else on the scarp.
    • : The plateau town at the inland end of the road, formerly called Oku.
  • Also covers: Bango, Ganja, Seles

Mount Moco and the Huambo highlands

The highest ground in the country, with grassland and forest pockets above the woodland

Mount Moco reaches 2,582 metres and is the highest point in Angola. It is the one reliable site for Swierstra's Spurfowl, which turned up again here after a gap of thirty years. Little of the massif has been explored in recent decades, and the ground above 1,900 metres carries small patches of Afromontane forest among the grass. The wider Bailundu highlands inland of the escarpment hold Black-chinned Weaver.

  • Habitat: Miombo (the broad-leaved woodland that covers the central plateau) climbs the mountain to about 1,900 metres and gives way above that to grassland with small patches of Afromontane forest. Spectacular inselbergs stand out of the highland around it.
  • When: April to October, while the highland tracks hold. Swierstra's Francolin is the reason to come, and the remnant forest patches on the massif are small enough to work in a day.
  • Getting there: Huambo is the town that opens the mountain and is reached from Lobito on a poor road. The turn-off from the main road between Huambo and Lobito is easily overlooked. A vehicle reaches a point about 10 km north of the peak at 1,750 metres and the mountain has to be walked from there. Landmines are a danger over large parts of the country, so local advice on safe ground is needed before leaving the roads.
  • Base yourself in: Huambo
  • Look for: Swierstra's Spurfowl, Red-crested Turaco, Margaret's Batis, Black-chinned Weaver
  • Stops:
    • : The highest mountain in Angola, and the only dependable site for Swierstra's Spurfowl.
    • : The highland city that gives access to the massif, and the base for the drive out to the turn-off.
  • Also covers: Huambo, Mount Moco
  • Also watch for: Large mammals are scarce on the massif, and what is endemic to it is small: Heinrich's Hylomyscus, a rodent described from here, and two near-endemic shrews.

Tundavala and the Lubango escarpment

A rock escarpment above the southern plateau, with cave chat on the outcrops

Tundavala stands where the southern plateau breaks away in cliffs west of Lubango. Angola Cave Chat is common on the rocky outcrops here and perches on lichen encrusted stone in the open. The site sits a long way south of the endemic forests and suits anyone arriving overland from the south.

  • Habitat: Rock outcrops and cliffs mark the lip of the escarpment, small forest patches survive in the gullies below it, and one stand of miombo woodland remains inside the reserve.
  • When: April to October, in the dry window when the escarpment roads are passable. The Leba pass below the rim drops through the whole altitude range in one drive.
  • Getting there: Tundavala lies about 16 km from Lubango on a good road. Camping is allowed at the picnic site in the remaining stand of woodland.
  • Base yourself in: Lubango
  • Look for: Angola Cave Chat, Angola Slaty Flycatcher, Cinderella Waxbill
  • Stops:
    • : The gap in the escarpment west of the city, where Angola Cave Chat lives on the rock.
    • : The plateau city below the escarpment, and the base for the road out to the viewpoint.
  • Also covers: Lubango, Tundavala

Malanje and the northern miombo

Plateau woodland east of the capital, with a national park in it and forest to the north east

Miombo woodland covers most of Angola away from the escarpment, and the country around Malanje is the part of it a visitor can reach on a made road. Cangandala National Park protects about 600 square kilometres of that woodland south east of the town. The forest north east of Malanje holds White-headed Robin-Chat, a bird known from northern Angola and the southern Congo and from nowhere else.

  • Habitat: Miombo woodland covers the plateau soils, and patches of forest survive on the higher ground to the north east of the town.
  • When: May to October in the dry season for the roads, with May to July the pick, when the Lucala is still high and the falls at Kalandula are full.
  • Getting there: Cangandala National Park lies south east of Malanje and the town is the base for it. Landmines are a danger over large parts of the country, so local advice on safe ground is needed before leaving the main roads.
  • Base yourself in: Malanje
  • Look for: Pale-billed Hornbill, Anchieta's Barbet, Souza's Shrike, African Spotted Creeper, Miombo Tit, Red-capped Crombec, White-headed Robin-Chat
  • National park:
    • Cangandala National Park, About 600 square kilometres of miombo woodland south east of the town, which holds Anchieta's Barbet, Souza's Shrike and African Spotted Creeper.
  • Stops:
    • : The provincial town on the plateau, and the base for both the park and the forest to the north east.
  • Also covers: Cangandala National Park, Malanje
  • Also watch for: Cangandala was declared in 1970 for the Giant Sable Antelope, which lives here and in the Luando reserve and nowhere else on earth.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I bird here with limited mobility?

Often yes, and it varies by site. A good deal of the birding here happens from roadsides, hides and lodge grounds rather than on long walks, and some reserves keep flat, surfaced trails. Ask a lodge or reserve directly about step free access, surfaces and seating before you book. The Birdability Map records those details for birding sites, and their Access Considerations guide lists the questions worth asking. Coverage is thin outside the United States, so read a blank area as unrecorded rather than unsuitable.
