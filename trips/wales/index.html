When to Go

The Welsh birding year has three peaks rather than one. Seabirds fill the cliffs from March to July, migration runs along the coasts through August to October, and the estuaries hold their biggest flocks from November to February.

Spring

March to June

Seabirds come back to the ledges in March, summer migrants arrive through April, and by May every woodland is singing from before five in the morning.

Go for Breeding seabirds, full song, and the longest days of the year in the north.

Trade off Rooms cost the most from Easter onward, and island boats need booking weeks ahead in June.

Typical daily average
  • March: 3 to 9 °C / 37 to 48 °F
  • April: 4 to 12 °C / 39 to 54 °F
  • May: 7 to 15 °C / 45 to 59 °F
  • June: 10 to 18 °C / 50 to 64 °F

Autumn

July to October

Wader passage builds from July, the coasts fill with migrants through September, and October is when a rarity can turn up almost anywhere.

Go for Big wader counts, seabirds moving past in an onshore wind, and the best chance of a bird from a long way off course.

Trade off Song has stopped, so birds are identified on plumage and call, and the seabird colonies are empty by August.

Typical daily average
  • July: 12 to 19 °C / 54 to 66 °F
  • August: 12 to 19 °C / 54 to 66 °F
  • September: 10 to 17 °C / 50 to 63 °F
  • October: 8 to 13 °C / 46 to 55 °F

Winter

November to February

Geese, swans and waders pack the estuaries, starlings gather over the reedbeds at dusk, and raptors are easy over open ground.

Go for Six figure goose and wader flocks, murmurations, and reserves you have largely to yourself.

Trade off Daylight is short, and the boats to Skomer and Bardsey stop for the winter.

Typical daily average
  • November: 5 to 10 °C / 41 to 50 °F
  • December: 3 to 8 °C / 37 to 46 °F
  • January: 2 to 7 °C / 36 to 45 °F
  • February: 2 to 7 °C / 36 to 45 °F

The Length of Wales

Twelve days work south down the coast and the uplands, and take in every region above. The island boats and the mountain days both want settled weather, so keep the order flexible.

Anglesey and the north Wales coast

Sea cliffs, shingle lagoons and dune forest around the Menai Strait, three hours from London by train to Bangor

A low island of sea cliff, shingle lagoon and dune forest, with mountains behind it across the Menai Strait. Chough are genuinely easy here, and the lagoon at Cemlyn holds one of the few Sandwich tern colonies in Britain.

  • Habitat: Sea cliff and heath at South Stack over a low limestone island, with the Cemlyn shingle lagoon and the Conwy and Dee estuaries.
  • When: April to July for the South Stack auks and chough and the Cemlyn tern colony. Winter fills the Dee and Conwy with waders.
  • Getting there: The bridge road crosses to Anglesey and South Stack is signed through Holyhead. Cemlyn is reached on lanes off the coast road, and the car park is small.
  • Look for: Red-billed Chough, Atlantic Puffin, Razorbill, Peregrine Falcon, Sandwich Tern, Manx Shearwater
  • Parks and reserves:
    • South Stack Cliffs RSPB reserve
    • Cemlyn Bay nature reserve
    • Newborough Warren and Ynys Llanddwyn National Nature Reserve
    • Conwy RSPB reserve, An RSPB reserve with a visitor centre, boardwalk and entry fee, built on land reclaimed during construction of the road tunnel. Waders and wildfowl use two lagoons, and starlings murmurate in winter.
  • Stops:
    • South Stack Cliffs : An RSPB reserve with a visitor centre and a seasonal information point at Ellin's Tower. A clifftop path runs towards the lighthouse, with puffins, guillemots, razorbills and chough on the cliffs.
    • Cemlyn Bay : A shingle lagoon reserve run jointly by the National Trust and North Wales Wildlife Trust, free to visit and watched from the coast road. It is one of Britain's few Sandwich tern colonies, with Arctic and common terns alongside.
    • : A lighthouse headland, a short walk from a roadside pull-in and free at any time. It is a noted seabird watchpoint for passing Manx shearwaters and harbour porpoise.
    • : A National Trust car park and headland looking across to Puffin Island, an offshore seabird colony with no public landing. Guillemots, razorbills and a cormorant colony hold it, with grey seals below.
    • Newborough Warren and Ynys Llanddwyn : A National Nature Reserve crossed by boardwalks and forest tracks from the beach car park, with a small parking fee. Chough and stonechat hold the dune heath and red squirrels the pines.

The Gower and Carmarthen Bay

Dune, saltmarsh and limestone headland on Britain's first Area of Outstanding Natural Beauty, forty minutes from Swansea

A limestone peninsula of dune, cliff and saltmarsh with a wide sandy bay east of it. The estuaries hold big winter wader flocks and a wetland centre sits on the shore at Llanelli.

  • Habitat: Carboniferous limestone cliff and dune over saltmarsh, with the Burry Inlet and Loughor estuary behind the peninsula.
  • When: October to March for the Burry Inlet waders and wildfowl, which is one of the largest oystercatcher wintering grounds in Britain.
  • Getting there: The A4118 runs the length of Gower from Swansea, half an hour to Oxwich. The Burry Inlet is watched from Penclawdd and Llanelli on the north shore.
  • Look for: Red-billed Chough, Eurasian Curlew, Eurasian Oystercatcher, Common Shelduck, Pied Avocet, Peregrine Falcon
  • Parks and reserves:
    • Gower Peninsula (Gŵyr) (reserve)
    • Whiteford Burrows National Nature Reserve
    • Oxwich National Nature Reserve, An NRW National Nature Reserve of dune, marsh and woodland behind Oxwich Bay beach, with a lakeside hide on a boardwalk. It is open at any time, with some parking charge.
    • Carmarthen Bay (reserve)
  • Stops:
    • : A Ramsar-listed estuary of sand and mudflats between Llanelli and Gower. Watch from the WWT hides or the coast path at any time.
    • Whiteford Burrows : A National Trust National Nature Reserve of dune and pine on Gower's north shore. Walk from the car park at Cwm Ivy at any time.
    • Worm's Head : A tidal limestone headland at Gower's western tip. Walk from the National Trust car park at Rhossili across a causeway exposed only at low tide, and check the tide times before crossing.
    • Rhossili Bay and Down : A National Trust beach and open clifftop down at Gower's western end, from the Rhossili car park. It is open at any time, with a parking charge.
    • : An eight mile beach and dune system on Carmarthen Bay inside Pembrey Country Park. The country park car parks reach it at any time, with a parking charge.
    • : A coastal forest and saltmarsh on the north shore of Carmarthen Bay. Walk in from the country park at any time.

Frequently Asked Questions

When is the best time to go birding in Wales?

May and June for the seabird islands and the oakwood migrants together. September and October for passage along the coast. December to February for waders and wildfowl on the estuaries.

Do I need a car?

For most of Wales, yes. The exceptions are real though: the coast railway reaches Anglesey, Aberystwyth and Pembrokeshire, RSPB Conwy has its own station, and the Dyfi reserves are within a short taxi ride of Machynlleth.

How many places to stay are above?

172, every one of them carrying a Green Tourism award confirmed in the scheme's own register on the date shown on its chip. That is a much smaller number than the accommodation available near these reserves, and it is deliberate: a certification you cannot check is a claim rather than evidence.

How do I get to Skomer or Skokholm?

Day boats to Skomer run from Martin's Haven and sell a limited number of landing tickets, released online in advance. Skokholm takes overnight guests rather than day visitors. Both are weather dependent and both fill for June and July months ahead.

Which Welsh names should I know?

Eryri is the name of the national park and the mountains it covers. Bannau Brycheiniog is the range in the south. Ynys Llanddwyn is the island at Newborough. The English names are still in use and signage carries both.

What is the weather like on the summit ridges?

Wet and fast changing. Eryri holds some of the highest rainfall in Britain and the crags can be in cloud when the coast is clear, so an upland day wants a forecast and a fallback at sea level.

Where can I be sure of seeing a red kite?

At a feeding station. Gigrin Farm near Rhayader feeds at a set time each afternoon and several hundred birds gather, which is a different experience from the ones you see over the road all across mid Wales.

Is Welsh birding good for a beginner?

It is one of the easiest places to start. Skomer gives close views of a dozen seabird species in an afternoon without any skill in finding them, and the reserves have hides, paths and staff.

When are the seabirds actually there?

Puffins and razorbills are on the ledges from late April to late July and gone by August. Manx shearwaters stay into September but only come ashore at night. Gannets pass Grassholm from March to September.

Can I bird here with limited mobility?

Sites on this page differ from each other more than any rating could carry, so this is the framework the standard British guides use, and the reserve's own page and warden hold the answers.

  • Permission · some reserves open ground to disabled visitors that is closed to everyone else, and it is granted by asking.
  • Priority · parking bays, hides and the nearest paths are often held for disabled visitors. Ask what is reserved and how to claim it.
  • Parking · how close a car reaches the birds shapes the whole day. Ring ahead for the nearest hard standing to the water or the hide.
  • Paths · surfaces, gradients and gate widths vary inside a single reserve. Ask which trail is boarded, surfaced or level.
  • Perches · a hide with a low window, or a bench where the view opens, turns a viewpoint into a morning.

Every reserve above with its own link carries its access detail there, and a warden answers what a page cannot.
