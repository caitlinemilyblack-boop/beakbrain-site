Birding in Florida

Florida is the only place in the United States with West Indian birds, a bird that lives in this state and no other, and a tern colony seventy miles out in open sea.

Birds that decide the trip

Florida Scrub Jay, the state’s only endemic

The Florida Scrub Jay lives on the sand ridges of this state and nowhere else on earth. It is tame to the point of landing on people, and it depends on scrub that burns, so the reserves holding it are burned deliberately.

When All year, and easiest in the cool months when the scrub is comfortable to walk.

Where Three Lakes Wildlife Management Area, Merritt Island National Wildlife Refuge

The Sooty Tern colony on the Dry Tortugas

Bush Key holds the only Sooty Tern colony in the United States, tens of thousands of birds seventy miles beyond Key West. The islands are reached by boat or seaplane and the noise carries across the water before the island does.

When April to July, while the colony is on the island.

Where Dry Tortugas National Park

Snail Kite over the sawgrass

A kite that eats one thing, the apple snail, and has a bill shaped to get it out of the shell. It quarters low over open marsh and its United States range is effectively this state’s central and southern wetlands.

When All year, with the dry season easiest as the water concentrates.

Where Everglades National Park, Lake Apopka Restoration Area

Roseate Spoonbills at Merritt Island

The impoundments behind the space centre hold spoonbills through the winter, feeding in shallow water within metres of a gravel drive. They were shot almost to nothing for their wings a century ago.

When November to April, on the Black Point drive.

Where Merritt Island National Wildlife Refuge, Everglades National Park

Red-cockaded Woodpeckers in the panhandle longleaf

Apalachicola and Eglin between them hold more Red-cockaded Woodpeckers than any other ground in the world. They nest in living pines and the trees are marked with white bands so foresters and visitors can find the clusters.

When All year, and best at dawn when the birds leave the roost trees.

Where Apalachicola National Forest, Eglin Air Force Base

Mangrove Cuckoo in the Keys

A West Indian bird that reaches the United States only along this coast, and one that sits still in dense mangrove and says nothing for hours. It is one of the hardest common birds in the country to actually see.

When April to July, when the birds call.

Where National Key Deer Refuge, Everglades National Park

Limpkin on the spring runs

A bird that looks like a rail and screams like something being murdered, feeding on apple snails along clear spring runs and marsh edges. Florida is the only state where it is reliably found.

When All year, and loudest at dusk and after dark.

Where Lake Apopka Restoration Area

Wood Storks in the dry-season pools

The only stork that breeds in the United States, and it needs falling water: it feeds by touch, so it can only catch fish once they are crowded. A wet winter means a failed breeding season.

When December to April, as the water drops.

Where Everglades National Park, Arthur R. Marshall Loxahatchee National Wildlife Refuge

When to Go

Florida works in the dry season. From December to April the Everglades water drops and concentrates the wading birds, the wintering ducks are in, and the heat and the mosquitoes are bearable. April and May add the Dry Tortugas and the spring crossing.

The dry season

December to April

Water levels drop across the south and the wading birds concentrate where the fish are left.

Go for Anhinga Trail and the Everglades, Merritt Island for the duck drive, and the central scrub for the jays.

Trade off The Dry Tortugas terns have not arrived and the panhandle is quiet.

The Tortugas and the crossing

April to May

The Sooty Tern colony is at its height and migrants cross the Gulf of Mexico onto the Keys.

Go for Dry Tortugas by boat, the Keys for West Indian strays, and the panhandle woods for the crossing.

Trade off The Everglades is filling again and the heat is building.

The wet season

June to November

Rain spreads the water and the birds with it, and the hurricane season runs from June.

Go for The panhandle longleaf for the woodpeckers, which are resident, and the marshes of the Gulf of Mexico shore.

Trade off The south is hot, wet and full of mosquitoes, and the wading bird concentrations are gone.

The Everglades and Big Cypress

Snail Kite over sawgrass, and a wading bird colony you can walk into

The Everglades is a river of grass sixty miles wide moving a few centimetres an hour, and the dry season concentrates its fish into shrinking pools with the wading birds standing over them. Anhinga Trail is a boardwalk through that, at arm’s length.

  • Habitat: Sawgrass marsh on a limestone shelf, with tree islands of hardwood hammock, dwarf cypress, mangrove at the coast and slash pine on the rock ridges.
  • When: December to April. Falling water concentrates fish into shrinking pools and the wading birds with them, and the mosquitoes are bearable. The wet season spreads the birds and makes the south hard work.
  • Getting there: Miami is the airport and Homestead the base. The main park road runs from the Ernest Coe entrance to Flamingo, with Anhinga Trail a boardwalk a few minutes inside it. Big Cypress is crossed by the Tamiami Trail and Loxahatchee is reached from Boynton Beach.
  • Base yourself in: Homestead
  • Look for: Snail Kite, Limpkin, Wood Stork, Roseate Spoonbill, Purple Gallinule, Anhinga, Short-tailed Hawk, Swallow-tailed Kite, Mottled Duck, American White Ibis, Tricolored Heron, Black Rail
  • Parks and reserves: Everglades National Park, Big Cypress National Preserve, Arthur R. Marshall Loxahatchee National Wildlife Refuge, Marjory Stoneman Douglas Wilderness (Protected area), Water Conservation Area 1 (Protected area)
  • Also covers: Arthur R. Marshall Loxahatchee National Wildlife Refuge, Big Cypress National Preserve, Everglades National Park, Marjory Stoneman Douglas Wilderness, Water Conservation Area 1
  • Also watch for: American alligator and American crocodile overlap here and nowhere else, and the Florida panther holds Big Cypress.

The Florida Keys and the Dry Tortugas

A sooty tern colony seventy miles out, on a fort in open sea

The Keys carry West Indian birds that reach the United States nowhere else, and the Dry Tortugas sit seventy miles beyond the last of them with the country’s only Sooty Tern and Brown Noddy colonies on them. Getting there is a boat or a seaplane, and it is the trip of the state.

  • Habitat: Tropical hardwood hammock and mangrove on the keys, with sand and coral rubble islands, seagrass flats and open Gulf water beyond them.
  • When: April to July for the Sooty Tern colony on Bush Key, and the same window for spring migrants crossing onto the keys. Winter is quieter but comfortable.
  • Getting there: Key West is the base, four hours from Miami down the Overseas Highway. The Dry Tortugas are seventy miles beyond it and reached only by the park ferry or a seaplane, both of which book up well ahead in spring.
  • Base yourself in: Key West
  • Look for: Sooty Tern, Brown Noddy, Masked Booby, Magnificent Frigatebird, Roseate Tern, White-crowned Pigeon, Mangrove Cuckoo, Black-whiskered Vireo, Antillean Nighthawk, Reddish Egret, Great Blue Heron, Piping Plover
  • Parks and reserves: Great White Heron National Wildlife Refuge, National Key Deer Refuge (Protected area), John Pennekamp Coral Reef State Park (State or provincial park), Florida Keys National Marine Sanctuary (Reserve), Everglades National Park
  • Also covers: Crocodile Lake National Wildlife Refuge, Dry Tortugas National Park, Everglades National Park, Florida Keys National Marine Sanctuary, Great White Heron National Wildlife Refuge, John Pennekamp Coral Reef State Park, Key West National Wildlife Refuge, National Key Deer Refuge
  • Also watch for: Key deer are a dwarf race of white-tailed deer found only on these islands, and American crocodile nests on the northern keys.

Merritt Island and the Atlantic coast

A refuge wrapped around a rocket range, and one of the best duck drives in the country

Merritt Island exists because the space centre needed a buffer, and the impoundments inside that buffer are now one of the densest waterbird sites on the Atlantic coast. Black Point Wildlife Drive runs through them on gravel.

  • Habitat: Brackish and freshwater impoundments behind a barrier island, with salt marsh, mosquito-control dykes, oak and palm hammock and undeveloped Atlantic beach.
  • When: November to April, when the wintering ducks and waders are in. Spoonbills feed on the impoundments through the winter and the beach holds nesting terns from spring.
  • Getting there: Orlando is the airport and Titusville the base, an hour east. Black Point Wildlife Drive is a one-way gravel loop through the impoundments, driveable in any car, and Canaveral National Seashore runs north from it.
  • Base yourself in: Titusville
  • Look for: Roseate Spoonbill, Wood Stork, Reddish Egret, Mottled Duck, Painted Bunting, Least Tern, Black Skimmer, Piping Plover, Marbled Godwit, Red Knot, Wilson's Plover, Florida Scrub Jay
  • Parks and reserves: Merritt Island National Wildlife Refuge, Canaveral National Seashore (National site), Saint Johns National Wildlife Refuge, Turnbull Hammock Conservation Area (Protected area), Enchanted Forest Sanctuary (Reserve)
  • Also covers: Canaveral National Seashore, Enchanted Forest Sanctuary, Merritt Island National Wildlife Refuge, Saint Johns National Wildlife Refuge, Turnbull Hammock Conservation Area
  • Also watch for: Manatees winter in the warm water outfalls and loggerhead turtles nest on the seashore beaches.

The central Florida scrub

Florida Scrub Jay, the only bird that lives in this state and no other

The sand ridges down central Florida carry a scrub of dwarf oak that burns and regrows, and the Florida Scrub Jay lives in it and nowhere else on earth. The same ridges hold the last Florida Grasshopper Sparrows.

  • Habitat: Dwarf oak scrub on relict sand ridges, with sand pine, wiregrass flatwoods, dry prairie and shallow lakes between them.
  • When: All year for the scrub jays, with the cool months from November to April far more comfortable. The prairie sparrows are easiest when singing in spring.
  • Getting there: Orlando is the airport. Three Lakes and Avon Park lie south of it towards Sebring, Lake Apopka is a paved and gravel wildlife drive on the city edge, and Avon Park is an active bombing range with public access limited by a permit system.
  • Base yourself in: Sebring
  • Look for: Florida Scrub Jay, Grasshopper Sparrow, Bachman's Sparrow, Red-cockaded Woodpecker, Crested Caracara, Burrowing Owl, Sandhill Crane, Limpkin, Snail Kite, Wood Stork, Swallow-tailed Kite, Painted Bunting
  • Parks and reserves: Ocala National Forest (Forest or grassland), Three Lakes Wildlife Management Area (National wildlife refuge), Lake Apopka Restoration Area (Protected area), North Shore Restoration Area (Protected area), Kissimmee River (Protected area)
  • Also covers: Avon Park Air Force Range, Kissimmee River, Lake Apopka Restoration Area, North Shore Restoration Area, Ocala National Forest, Three Lakes Wildlife Management Area
  • Also watch for: The scrub burns on a natural cycle and the jays depend on it, so the reserves are burned deliberately.

The Gulf coast and the Big Bend

Salt marsh with no development behind it, for a hundred miles

Where the peninsula meets the panhandle the coast turns to marsh and oyster bar with almost nothing built on it, which is why the Big Bend holds birds the rest of the Gulf of Mexico shore has lost. St Marks has a lighthouse, a levee and a great deal of water.

  • Habitat: Salt marsh and oyster bar with almost no development behind it, backed by pine flatwoods, coastal hammock and spring-fed rivers running to the coast.
  • When: October to April for wintering waterbirds and comfortable temperatures. Summer is hot, wet and heavy with insects.
  • Getting there: Tampa or Tallahassee are the airports. St Marks has a lighthouse road and a levee system reached from Highway 98, and Cedar Key sits at the end of a causeway three hours north of Tampa.
  • Base yourself in: St Marks
  • Look for: Reddish Egret, Wood Stork, Seaside Sparrow, Black Rail, Marbled Godwit, Wilson's Plover, Snowy Plover, American Oystercatcher, Mottled Duck, Roseate Spoonbill, Florida Scrub Jay, Least Tern
  • Parks and reserves: Lower Suwannee National Wildlife Refuge, Cedar Key Scrub State Reserve, Saint Marks National Wildlife Refuge, Saint Marks Wilderness (Protected area), Big Bend Wildlife Management Area (National wildlife refuge)
  • Also covers: Big Bend Wildlife Management Area, Cedar Key Scrub State Reserve, Cedar Keys National Wildlife Refuge, Lower Suwannee National Wildlife Refuge, Saint Marks National Wildlife Refuge, Saint Marks Wilderness, St. Marks National Wildlife Refuge
  • Also watch for: The Gulf sturgeon runs up the Suwannee and manatees use the spring runs in winter.

The panhandle

The largest population of Red-cockaded Woodpeckers anywhere, in longleaf pine

The panhandle is the southern end of the great longleaf pine forest, and Apalachicola and Eglin between them hold more Red-cockaded Woodpeckers than any other ground in the world. The barrier islands off it are white quartz sand.

  • Habitat: Longleaf pine savannah kept open by fire, with pitcher plant bogs in the wet swales, titi swamp, blackwater rivers and white quartz barrier islands offshore.
  • When: All year for the woodpeckers, which are resident, and dawn is when they leave the roost trees. April and May add the migrants and the breeding warblers.
  • Getting there: Tallahassee is the airport. Apalachicola National Forest begins immediately south-west of the city on paved and forest roads, and the cavity trees are marked with white bands. Eglin needs a recreation permit bought online.
  • Base yourself in: Tallahassee
  • Look for: Red-cockaded Woodpecker, Bachman's Sparrow, Henslow's Sparrow, LeConte's Sparrow, Yellow Rail, Brown-headed Nuthatch, Swainson's Warbler, Prothonotary Warbler, Seaside Sparrow, Snowy Plover, Least Tern, Bald Eagle
  • Parks and reserves: Apalachicola National Forest (Forest or grassland), Gulf Islands National Seashore (National site), Blackwater River State Forest (Forest or grassland), Saint Marks National Wildlife Refuge, Tate's Hell Wildlife Management Area (National wildlife refuge)
  • Also covers: Apalachicola National Forest, Blackwater River State Forest, Eglin Air Force Base, Gulf Islands National Seashore, Ochlockonee River State Park, Saint Marks National Wildlife Refuge, Tate's Hell Wildlife Management Area
  • Also watch for: The longleaf understorey holds pitcher plants in bogs, and the forest is burned every two or three years to keep it.

The north Florida swamps

Blackwater under cypress, and a corridor stitched back together

Pinhook Swamp is the corridor that links Osceola National Forest north towards the Okefenokee, so the whole block works as one piece of country again. It is blackwater under cypress and pine flatwoods, walked from a boardwalk or paddled.

  • Habitat: Blackwater under bald cypress and tupelo, with peat prairies, sandhill longleaf on the higher ground and pine flatwoods between.
  • When: October to April, before the heat and the insects. The cranes are loudest in late winter and the swamp is best from a boat.
  • Getting there: Jacksonville is the airport and Lake City the base. Osceola National Forest is reached from Interstate 10, and Pinhook Swamp lies north of it on the corridor towards the Georgia line.
  • Base yourself in: Lake City
  • Look for: Sandhill Crane, Red-cockaded Woodpecker, Bachman's Sparrow, Prothonotary Warbler, Red-headed Woodpecker, Swallow-tailed Kite, Anhinga, Barred Owl, Wood Stork, Limpkin, Brown-headed Nuthatch, Swainson's Warbler
  • Parks and reserves: Osceola National Forest (Forest or grassland), Big Gum Swamp Wilderness (Protected area), Okefenokee National Wildlife Refuge
  • Also covers: Big Gum Swamp Wilderness, Okefenokee National Wildlife Refuge, Osceola National Forest, Pinhook Swamp
  • Also watch for: The swamp holds several thousand American alligators and the peat is deep enough to burn in a drought.

Frequently Asked Questions

When is the best time to go birding in Florida?

December to April. The dry season drops the water across the south and concentrates the wading birds where the fish are left, the wintering ducks are in, and the heat and mosquitoes are bearable. Late April adds the Dry Tortugas tern colony and the spring crossing onto the Keys.

Which birds can I see in Florida and nowhere else in the country?

Florida Scrub Jay lives in this state and no other. Snail Kite, Limpkin, Short-tailed Hawk, White-crowned Pigeon, Mangrove Cuckoo, Black-whiskered Vireo and Antillean Nighthawk all reach the United States only here, and the Dry Tortugas hold the country’s only Sooty Tern and Brown Noddy colonies.

How do I get to the Dry Tortugas?

A boat or a seaplane from Key West, and nothing else. The islands are seventy miles out, the crossing takes over two hours by ferry, and both the ferry and the flights book up well ahead in spring.

Is the Everglades better in the wet or dry season?

Dry, without question. Wood Stork feeds by touch and can only catch fish once falling water has crowded them, so a wet winter means empty pools and a failed breeding season. December to April is when the birds are concentrated and visible.

Where do I go for Red-cockaded Woodpecker?

The panhandle. Apalachicola National Forest and Eglin Air Force Base between them hold more of them than any other ground in the world. The birds nest in living pines and the cavity trees are marked with white bands, so the clusters are findable.

Do I need to worry about mosquitoes?

In the south from May to October, yes, seriously. Everglades and the Keys in summer are genuinely difficult without repellent and covered arms, which is another reason the dry season is the birding season.

Can I bird here with limited mobility?

Sites on this page differ from each other more than any rating could carry, so this is the framework the standard British guides use, and the reserve's own page and warden hold the answers.

  • Permission · some reserves open ground to disabled visitors that is closed to everyone else, and it is granted by asking.
  • Priority · parking bays, hides and the nearest paths are often held for disabled visitors. Ask what is reserved and how to claim it.
  • Parking · how close a car reaches the birds shapes the whole day. Ring ahead for the nearest hard standing to the water or the hide.
  • Paths · surfaces, gradients and gate widths vary inside a single reserve. Ask which trail is boarded, surfaced or level.
  • Perches · a hide with a low window, or a bench where the view opens, turns a viewpoint into a morning.
