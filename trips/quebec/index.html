Birding in Quebec

A hundred thousand Northern Gannets breed on one island cliff at Bonaventure, and a boardwalk brings visitors to within a few metres of the colony, which no other gannetry on earth allows.

Birds that decide the trip

Northern Gannet on Bonaventure

One of the largest colonies in the world, and the only one anywhere with a walking trail that ends at the colony edge. The birds are a few metres away and take no notice.

When June and July, on the island cliff top.

Where Bonaventure and the Gaspe, The Iles de la Madeleine

Snow Goose on the Cap Tourmente flats

The whole Greater Snow Goose population passes up and down the St Lawrence and stops here to feed on bulrush rhizomes, which is what the reserve was created for.

When April and October, on the tidal flats.

Where Cap Tourmente and the estuary

Piping Plover on the Madeleine sand

The long spits joining the islands hold one of the more important populations in the country, on beaches that are roped and wardened through the summer.

When May to August, on roped beach sections.

Where The Iles de la Madeleine

Two-barred Crossbill over the Tadoussac dunes

In an irruption autumn the dunes count crossbills, grosbeaks and redpolls in numbers that make the site one of the more remarkable finch watchpoints anywhere.

When October and November, from the dunes observatory.

Where Tadoussac and the Saguenay

Atlantic Puffin on the gulf stacks

Rocher aux Oiseaux and the Bonaventure ledges both hold puffins among the murres and kittiwakes, and both are watched from a boat rather than landed on.

When June and July, from the colony boats.

Where The Iles de la Madeleine, Bonaventure and the Gaspe

Spruce Grouse in the Laurentian fir

It sits still and lets people walk past, which is why it is missed far more often than it is absent. The park roads north of the city are the way to find one.

When All year, most easily in June on quiet forest roads.

Where The Laurentians and the boreal north

Connecticut Warbler in the Abitibi bog

It walks rather than hops, sings from high in a tamarack and then vanishes, and the bogs of the north-west hold it in as good numbers as anywhere in the east.

When June, in tamarack and spruce bog at dawn.

Where The Laurentians and the boreal north

Nelson's Sparrow in the estuary marsh

It sings a hissing note from brackish marsh at dusk, and the St Lawrence estuary and the Madeleine lagoons both hold breeding birds.

When June and July, in brackish marsh at dusk.

Where Cap Tourmente and the estuary, The Iles de la Madeleine

When to Go

Quebec is a summer and an autumn destination. The seabird colonies run from June, the Tadoussac dunes count one of the more remarkable autumn flights in the east from late September, and the snow geese fill the estuary twice a year on their way past.

The goose passage

April and October

Greater Snow Geese stage on the estuary bulrush flats in tens of thousands.

Go for Cap Tourmente and Montmagny, and Baie-du-Febvre inland.

Trade off The seabird colonies are empty and the north is still frozen in April.

Typical daily average

The seabird season

June and July

Bonaventure holds its gannets and the Madeleine cliffs their auks and kittiwakes.

Go for Bonaventure Island by boat, Forillon, and the Madeleine islands.

Trade off The islands book out and the crossings depend on the weather.

Typical daily average

The Tadoussac flight

Late September to early November

The dunes above the fjord count raptors, finches and passerines forced along the north shore.

Go for The Tadoussac dunes observatory on a north-west wind.

Trade off The weather turns fast and the whale-watching crowds are still in town.

Typical daily average

The boreal breeding season

June and early July

The Laurentians and the northern spruce hold their warblers and finches.

Go for Parc de la Jacques-Cartier and the Abitibi bogs.

Trade off Blackflies are severe and the far north has very few roads.

Bonaventure and the Gaspe

A hundred thousand gannets on one cliff, with a path that ends a few metres from them

Bonaventure Island holds one of the largest Northern Gannet colonies in the world and, unusually, a walking trail that ends at the colony edge. The birds are within a few metres and entirely unbothered, which no other gannetry anywhere offers. Forillon on the mainland adds cliff, forest and seal-covered rock.

  • Habitat: Sandstone sea cliff with grass top, spruce and fir forest, cobble and boulder shore, and open gulf water.
  • When: June and July, when the colony is at its fullest. The boats stop running in October.
  • Getting there: Gaspe has a small airport, and Perce is a long drive from Quebec City. Boats run to the island from Perce through the summer, the park charges a fee, and the trail to the colony takes about an hour on foot.
  • Base yourself in: Perce
  • Look for: Northern Gannet, Black-legged Kittiwake, Common Murre, Razorbill, Black Guillemot, Atlantic Puffin, Great Cormorant, Common Eider, Bald Eagle, Peregrine Falcon, Blackpoll Warbler, Fox Sparrow
  • Parks and reserves: Refuge d'oiseaux migrateurs de l'Île-Bonaventure-et-du-Rocher-Percé (Reserve), Parc national de l'Île-Bonaventure-et-du-Rocher-Percé, Parc national de Forillon, Réserve faunique de la Rivière-Saint-Jean (Reserve)
  • Also covers: Bonaventure Island, Forillon National Park, Gaspe, Parc national de Forillon, Parc national de l'Île-Bonaventure-et-du-Rocher-Percé, Refuge d'oiseaux migrateurs de l'Île-Bonaventure-et-du-Rocher-Percé, Réserve faunique de la Rivière-Saint-Jean
  • Also watch for: Grey and harbour seals haul out below the cliffs, and minke and humpback whales feed in the bay through the summer.

Tadoussac and the Saguenay

A dune ridge above a fjord mouth, where the observatory counts raptors and finches pressed against the water

The Saguenay meets the St Lawrence at Tadoussac, and birds moving south down the north shore are pressed against the water and funnelled over the dunes above the village. The observatory there counts raptors, and in an irruption year the winter finch totals are extraordinary.

  • Habitat: Sand dunes above a fjord mouth, spruce and fir forest inland, rock shore and deep cold water, and salt marsh at the head of the bay.
  • When: Late September to early November for the flight, and June to September for the whales and the resident seabirds.
  • Getting there: Quebec City is the airport, three hours south-west, and Tadoussac the base. A free ferry crosses the fjord, and the dunes observatory is a short drive above the village with an entry fee.
  • Base yourself in: Tadoussac
  • Look for: Rough-legged Buzzard, American Goshawk, Golden Eagle, Sharp-shinned Hawk, Pine Grosbeak, Two-barred Crossbill, Red Crossbill, Bohemian Waxwing, Boreal Chickadee, Black-backed Woodpecker, Common Eider, Black Guillemot
  • Parks and reserves: Parc national du Fjord-du-Saguenay, Parc marin du Saguenay–Saint‑Laurent (Protected area), Réserve de biodiversité de la Vallée-de-la-Rivière-Sainte-Marguerite (Reserve), Zec Buteux–Bas-Saguenay (Protected area)
  • Also covers: Baie-Sainte-Catherine, Parc marin du Saguenay–Saint‑Laurent, Parc national du Fjord-du-Saguenay, Réserve de biodiversité de la Vallée-de-la-Rivière-Sainte-Marguerite, Saguenay Fjord, Tadoussac dunes, Zec Buteux–Bas-Saguenay
  • Also watch for: The fjord mouth holds beluga whales all year and blue whales feed in the estuary in late summer.

Cap Tourmente and the estuary

Tens of thousands of Greater Snow Geese on bulrush flats, twice a year

The whole Greater Snow Goose population passes up and down the St Lawrence, and Cap Tourmente is where they stop to feed on the bulrush rhizomes in the tidal flats. The numbers are in the tens of thousands and the reserve was created for them.

  • Habitat: Tidal bulrush flats and salt marsh on the estuary, farmland and orchard on the terrace behind, and forested escarpment above.
  • When: April and October for the goose staging, with the autumn peak usually in the second half of October.
  • Getting there: Quebec City is the airport, forty minutes west, and the city is the base. The wildlife area charges an entry fee and has an interpretation centre and trails, and Baie-du-Febvre is two hours upstream.
  • Base yourself in: Quebec City
  • Look for: Snow Goose, Canada Goose, Tundra Swan, Northern Pintail, American Black Duck, Bald Eagle, Peregrine Falcon, Snowy Owl, Sandhill Crane, Short-eared Owl, Nelson's Sparrow, American Bittern
  • Parks and reserves: Refuge d’oiseaux migrateurs de Montmagny (Reserve), Refuge d'oiseaux migrateurs de Nicolet (Reserve), Réserve nationale de faune du Cap-Tourmente (Reserve), Refuge d’oiseaux migrateurs de Cap-Saint-Ignace (Reserve)
  • Also covers: Baie-du-Febvre, Cap Tourmente National Wildlife Area, Isle-aux-Grues, Refuge d’oiseaux migrateurs de Cap-Saint-Ignace, Refuge d’oiseaux migrateurs de Montmagny, Refuge d'oiseaux migrateurs de Nicolet, Réserve nationale de faune du Cap-Tourmente
  • Also watch for: The reserve holds one of the larger concentrations of wintering raptors on the estuary, and the flats carry an unusual tidal bulrush community.

The Laurentians and the boreal north

Glaciated valleys and spruce plateau, with the boreal breeding list running north from them

The Laurentians are the southern edge of the Canadian Shield, glaciated into steep valleys with spruce and fir on the slopes. North of them the province turns to unbroken boreal forest, bog and lake, which is where the warblers that most people chase in migration actually breed.

  • Habitat: Spruce and fir on glaciated shield, northern hardwood in the valleys, sphagnum bog and tamarack, and thousands of lakes and reservoirs.
  • When: June and early July for the breeding birds, which is also when the blackflies are at their worst.
  • Getting there: Quebec City or Montreal are the airports. The provincial parks charge a day fee and have paved access roads, and the Abitibi bogs are eight hours north-west on the highway.
  • Base yourself in: Quebec City
  • Look for: Spruce Grouse, Black-backed Woodpecker, American Three-toed Woodpecker, Canada Jay, Boreal Chickadee, Cape May Warbler, Bay-breasted Warbler, Blackpoll Warbler, Connecticut Warbler, Rusty Blackbird, Great Grey Owl, Boreal Owl
  • Parks and reserves: Parc national du Mont-Tremblant, Parc national de la Jacques-Cartier, Réserve écologique Jackrabbit (Reserve), Parc Éco-Laurentides (Protected area), Domaine Saint-Bernard (Protected area)
  • Also covers: Abitibi, Domaine Saint-Bernard, Parc Éco-Laurentides, Parc national de la Jacques-Cartier, Parc national du Mont-Tremblant, Réserve écologique Jackrabbit, Reservoir Gouin
  • Also watch for: Moose are common through the parks and woodland caribou hold a small and closely watched population in the Jacques-Cartier highlands.

The Iles de la Madeleine

Red sandstone islands in the middle of the gulf, with the country's best Piping Plover beaches

The Madeleine islands sit far out in the Gulf of St Lawrence, joined to each other by long sand spits, and the beaches between them hold one of the more important Piping Plover populations in the country. Rocher aux Oiseaux offshore is a seabird stack with gannets, murres and puffins on it.

  • Habitat: Red sandstone cliff and stack, long sand spit and dune joining the islands, lagoon and salt marsh behind, and open gulf water.
  • When: June and July for the seabirds and the nesting plovers, when the boats and the flights run most often.
  • Getting there: The islands are reached by air from Montreal or Quebec City, or by a five-hour ferry from Prince Edward Island. Rocher aux Oiseaux is closed to landing and watched from a boat.
  • Base yourself in: Havre-aux-Maisons
  • Look for: Piping Plover, Northern Gannet, Atlantic Puffin, Razorbill, Common Murre, Black-legged Kittiwake, Common Eider, Willet, Nelson's Sparrow, Red-throated Loon, Great Cormorant, Leach's Storm Petrel
  • Parks and reserves: Réserve écologique de l'Île-Brion (Reserve)
  • Also covers: Havre-aux-Maisons, Ile Brion, Iles de la Madeleine, Réserve écologique de l'Île-Brion, Rocher aux Oiseaux
  • Also watch for: Grey seals pup on the ice off the islands in late winter in one of the largest gatherings in the world.

Frequently Asked Questions

When is the best time to go birding in Quebec?

June and July, if the seabird colonies are the point: Bonaventure and the Madeleine cliffs are both at their fullest and the boats are running. Late October is the other date, for the Tadoussac flight and the snow geese on the estuary.

What makes Bonaventure different from other gannet colonies?

The trail. One of the largest colonies in the world sits on the island top, and a footpath ends at the edge of it, so the birds are a few metres away and entirely unbothered. No other gannetry anywhere allows that.

Why do birds funnel over Tadoussac?

The Saguenay meets the St Lawrence there, and birds moving south down the north shore are pressed against the water and pushed over the dunes above the village. The observatory counts raptors and, in irruption years, extraordinary numbers of winter finches.

How do I get to the Iles de la Madeleine?

By air from Montreal or Quebec City, or a five-hour ferry from Prince Edward Island. The islands are joined by long sand spits, a hire car covers all of them, and the seabird stack offshore is closed to landing and watched from a boat.

Where do the migrant warblers actually breed?

North of the Laurentians, in the unbroken boreal forest and bog that covers most of the province. The Cape May Warbler, the Bay-breasted, the Blackpoll and the Connecticut all nest there, which is a very different experience from meeting them on passage.

Can I bird here with limited mobility?

Sites on this page differ from each other more than any rating could carry, so this is the framework the standard British guides use, and the reserve's own page and warden hold the answers.

  • Permission · some reserves open ground to disabled visitors that is closed to everyone else, and it is granted by asking.
  • Priority · parking bays, hides and the nearest paths are often held for disabled visitors. Ask what is reserved and how to claim it.
  • Parking · how close a car reaches the birds shapes the whole day. Ring ahead for the nearest hard standing to the water or the hide.
  • Paths · surfaces, gradients and gate widths vary inside a single reserve. Ask which trail is boarded, surfaced or level.
  • Perches · a hide with a low window, or a bench where the view opens, turns a viewpoint into a morning.

Every reserve above with its own link carries its access detail there, and a warden answers what a page cannot.
