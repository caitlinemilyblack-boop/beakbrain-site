Birding in Nunavut

Richards and Gaston open their avifauna by admitting how much of the territory is known only from the air: long stretches of coast where the seabirds and geese have been counted from a plane and never on the ground.

Birds that decide the trip

Thick-billed Murre on the Lancaster Sound cliffs

The Prince Leopold Island sanctuary holds a population of international importance, on limestone cliffs above the richest marine water in the Canadian Arctic.

When Late June and July, from a boat.

Where Bylot Island and Lancaster Sound

Ivory Gull at the ice edge

It lives its whole life within reach of sea ice and is one of the few birds a warming Arctic has nowhere to move to. Seymour Island holds one of the studied colonies.

When June and July, along the floe edge.

Where Ellesmere and the High Arctic, Bylot Island and Lancaster Sound

Ross's Gull in the High Arctic

A small pink gull that breeds in scattered pairs across the north and is one of the hardest birds on the continent to plan for. Seymour and Nasaruvaalik islands are the studied sites.

When July, on High Arctic islands.

Where Ellesmere and the High Arctic

Ross's Goose on the McConnell River

The sanctuary was protected in 1960 for this goose and the Canada Goose, on wet sedge tundra along the Hudson Bay coast.

When Late June and July, on the coastal tundra.

Where The Kivalliq coast

Gyrfalcon at Sylvia Grinnell

The territorial park inside the Iqaluit town boundary is listed for it, which makes this the most reachable Gyrfalcon in the Arctic.

When June and July, over the river valley.

Where Iqaluit and southern Baffin

King Eider on the tundra ponds

The drake is the most improbably decorated duck in the Arctic, and pairs sit on small tundra ponds through late June before the females go to nest.

When Late June, on tundra ponds.

Where Foxe Basin and Southampton Island, Ellesmere and the High Arctic

Little Auk off north Baffin

It breeds in scree colonies of extraordinary density around the northern sounds and feeds where the currents concentrate plankton at the ice edge.

When June and July, at the ice edge.

Where Bylot Island and Lancaster Sound

Red Phalarope on the Foxe Basin meadows

The female is the bright one and leaves the male to the eggs, and the sedge meadows and shallow ponds of the limestone lowland are full of them.

When July, on sedge meadow ponds.

Where Foxe Basin and Southampton Island, The Kivalliq coast

When to Go

Everything here happens in about six weeks. The sea ice holds into June across most of the territory, the birds arrive as it goes, and the young are flying by the middle of August. The one earlier window is the floe edge, which is at its best while the ice is still in.

The floe edge

Late May and early June

The ice edge draws seabirds, narwhal and bears to one line in the sea.

Go for Pond Inlet, out onto the Lancaster Sound floe edge by snowmobile.

Trade off Camping on sea ice, and the season ends when the ice breaks up.

The colony season

Late June and July

Thick-billed Murres, fulmars and kittiwakes fill the Lancaster Sound cliffs.

Go for Sirmilik and the Prince Leopold Island sanctuary by boat.

Trade off Ice conditions decide whether a boat can reach any of it.

The tundra breeding season

Late June to early August

Geese, waders and longspurs nest across the coastal lowlands.

Go for The Kivalliq coast and the Foxe Basin sanctuaries.

Trade off Mosquitoes are severe on the mainland and there is no cover from them.

The High Arctic window

July

Ellesmere thaws for a few weeks and its short breeding list is on the ground.

Go for Quttinirpaaq, by charter from Resolute.

Iqaluit and southern Baffin

The one place in the territory where arctic birds are a walk from a scheduled flight

Iqaluit is the capital and the only community most visitors reach, and the tundra starts at the edge of it. Sylvia Grinnell Territorial Park is 38 square kilometres of river valley and heath inside the town boundary, and Richards and Gaston list Gyrfalcon, Common Ringed Plover and Northern Wheatear for it.

  • Habitat: River valley and rapids, tundra heath and boulder field, coastal flat and tidal bay with a very large range, and rock outcrop.
  • When: Late June to early August. The park is walkable from town and the road system is a few kilometres long.
  • Getting there: Iqaluit has daily jets from Ottawa and Montreal. Sylvia Grinnell is a short walk or drive from the centre with marked trails, and everything beyond the town needs a licensed outfitter.
  • Base yourself in: Iqaluit
  • Look for: Gyrfalcon, Common Ringed Plover, Northern Wheatear, Rock Ptarmigan, Lapland Longspur, Snow Bunting, American Golden Plover, Baird's Sandpiper, Long-tailed Jaeger, Red-throated Loon, Iceland Gull, Redpoll
  • Also covers: Frobisher Bay, Iqaluit, Katannilik Territorial Park, Sylvia Grinnell Territorial Park
  • Also watch for: Ringed seals use the bay ice through the winter and polar bears follow them, so the tundra outside the town is bear country like everywhere else here.

Bylot Island and Lancaster Sound

Seabird cliffs on a sound where the ice edge concentrates everything that lives there

Lancaster Sound is the richest arctic marine water in the country, and the cliffs around it hold seabird colonies of international importance. Richards and Gaston name the Prince Leopold Island sanctuary for its Thick-billed Murres, Northern Fulmars and Black-legged Kittiwakes, and Bylot carries a Snow Goose colony as well as its own murre cliffs.

  • Habitat: Sandstone and limestone seabird cliff, glaciated island and ice cap, tundra lowland with a goose colony, and floe edge and open lead.
  • When: Late May and early June for the floe edge, and late June and July for the colonies once the boats can move.
  • Getting there: Pond Inlet is reached by scheduled flight through Iqaluit. Everything beyond the community goes with an outfitter, on snowmobile in spring and by boat in summer, and Prince Leopold Island is a long crossing.
  • Base yourself in: Pond Inlet
  • Look for: Thick-billed Murre, Northern Fulmar, Black-legged Kittiwake, Black Guillemot, Little Auk, Ivory Gull, Glaucous Gull, Snow Goose, King Eider, Long-tailed Duck, Purple Sandpiper, Arctic Tern
  • National park: Sirmilik National Park
  • Also covers: Bylot Island, Pond Inlet, Prince Leopold Island, Sirmilik National Park
  • Also watch for: Narwhal, beluga, bowhead, ringed seal and polar bear all use the same ice edge as the seabirds, which is why the sound is protected.

The Kivalliq coast

Mainland tundra along Hudson Bay, with the goose sanctuaries and a town full of nesting falcons

The Nunavut coast of Hudson Bay is low tundra, sedge meadow and esker, and it carries the mainland's breeding geese and waders. The McConnell River sanctuary was protected in 1960 for Canada and Ross's Geese, Peregrine Falcon, Short-eared Owl and Rusty Blackbird, and the peregrines at Rankin Inlet have been studied for decades.

  • Habitat: Wet sedge meadow and tundra pond, esker ridge and gravel, rocky coast and tidal flat, and low willow along the rivers.
  • When: Late June to early August, when the geese are on the sanctuaries and the waders are on the meadows.
  • Getting there: Rankin Inlet and Arviat both have scheduled flights. The sanctuaries have no facilities of any kind and are reached by boat or charter with a licensed outfitter.
  • Base yourself in: Rankin Inlet
  • Look for: Ross's Goose, Cackling Goose, Snow Goose, Peregrine Falcon, Short-eared Owl, Rusty Blackbird, Horned Lark, American Pipit, Dunlin, White-rumped Sandpiper, Pacific Loon, Parasitic Jaeger
  • Also covers: Arviat, Baker Lake, McConnell River Bird Sanctuary, Rankin Inlet
  • Also watch for: Barren-ground caribou of the Qamanirjuaq herd calve inland from this coast and move through it in summer, and polar bears come ashore as the bay ice goes.

Foxe Basin and Southampton Island

Limestone lowland and reef, holding eider and goose colonies on ground barely above the sea

The Foxe Basin coast is flat limestone that the sea has broken into thousands of islands, reefs and inlets, which Richards and Gaston call exceptional habitat for Common Eiders and Black Guillemots. The East Bay sanctuary on Southampton Island was protected in 1959 for Brant, Snow and Cackling Geese and Common Eider.

  • Habitat: Flat limestone reef and island, wet sedge meadow, tundra pond and coastal lagoon, and shallow ice-scoured sea.
  • When: July, when the eiders and geese are on the colonies and the ice has cleared enough for a boat.
  • Getting there: Coral Harbour and Igloolik have scheduled flights through Rankin Inlet or Iqaluit. The sanctuaries are boat access only, with an outfitter, and there are no facilities.
  • Base yourself in: Coral Harbour
  • Look for: Common Eider, King Eider, Black Guillemot, Brant Goose, Snow Goose, Cackling Goose, Red Phalarope, Red-necked Phalarope, Dunlin, Sabine's Gull, Thick-billed Murre, Snowy Owl
  • Also covers: Coral Harbour, East Bay Bird Sanctuary, Igloolik, Naujaat
  • Also watch for: Walrus haul out on the Foxe Basin reefs in some of the largest groups in the Canadian Arctic, and the shallow water over the limestone is why.

Ellesmere and the High Arctic

Polar desert at the top of the continent, with a short breeding list and a long history of counting it

Quttinirpaaq covers the northern end of Ellesmere and is the second largest park in Canada, mostly ice cap and polar desert. The first census of breeding birds ever compiled in Nunavut was made in Owl Valley at the southern end of it in 1953. Seymour Island further west holds Ivory and Ross's Gulls.

  • Habitat: Polar desert and frost-shattered rock, ice cap and glacier, thermal oasis valley with sedge and willow, and permanent sea ice.
  • When: July, and only July. The park is staffed for about six weeks and frozen the rest of the year.
  • Getting there: Resolute is reached by scheduled flight and is the staging point. Quttinirpaaq is a charter flight beyond that, needs registration with Parks Canada, and weather regularly cancels flights for several days.
  • Base yourself in: Resolute
  • Look for: Ivory Gull, Ross's Gull, Rock Ptarmigan, Snowy Owl, Long-tailed Jaeger, Sanderling, Ruddy Turnstone, Red Knot, Baird's Sandpiper, Snow Bunting, Yellow-billed Loon, King Eider
  • National park: Quttinirpaaq National Park
  • Also covers: Grise Fiord, Quttinirpaaq National Park, Resolute, Seymour Island
  • Also watch for: Muskoxen, Peary caribou and arctic wolves live on Ellesmere, and the wolves there have almost no experience of people and show no fear of them.

Frequently Asked Questions

When is the best time to go birding in Nunavut?

Late June to early August, which is very nearly the whole season. The sea ice holds into June across most of the territory, the birds arrive as it goes, and the young are flying by the middle of August. Late May and early June is the exception, for the floe edge, which needs the ice to still be there.

Can I do this independently?

Only in Iqaluit, and barely there. No two communities in the territory are joined by a road, so travel is scheduled flights between towns and a licensed outfitter beyond them. Sylvia Grinnell Territorial Park at Iqaluit is the one place a visitor can walk to arctic birds unaided.

What is the floe edge?

The line where the landfast ice meets open water. Everything that eats in the sea concentrates along it, so a few days camped there gives murres, fulmars, kittiwakes and Ivory Gulls, along with narwhal, beluga and polar bears. It exists only until the ice breaks up.

How much of the territory has actually been surveyed?

Less than you would expect. Richards and Gaston say ground surveys away from the national parks are sparse, and that there are long stretches of coast where the presence of seabirds and geese is known only from aerial surveys.

What does it cost to reach the High Arctic?

More than anywhere else in the country. Quttinirpaaq needs a scheduled flight to Resolute and a charter beyond it, registration with Parks Canada, and enough slack in the itinerary for weather to cancel flights at both ends.

Can I bird here with limited mobility?

Sites on this page differ from each other more than any rating could carry, so this is the framework the standard British guides use, and the reserve's own page and warden hold the answers.

  • Permission · some reserves open ground to disabled visitors that is closed to everyone else, and it is granted by asking.
  • Priority · parking bays, hides and the nearest paths are often held for disabled visitors. Ask what is reserved and how to claim it.
  • Parking · how close a car reaches the birds shapes the whole day. Ring ahead for the nearest hard standing to the water or the hide.
  • Paths · surfaces, gradients and gate widths vary inside a single reserve. Ask which trail is boarded, surfaced or level.
  • Perches · a hide with a low window, or a bench where the view opens, turns a viewpoint into a morning.

Every reserve above with its own link carries its access detail there, and a warden answers what a page cannot.
