Birding in the Yukon

The Dempster Highway is the only public road in Canada that crosses the Arctic Circle, and it runs 740 km through tundra, alpine pass and taiga with the birds of all three beside it.

Birds that decide the trip

Bluethroat on the Dempster willows

A Eurasian chat that crosses the Bering Strait to breed, and the willow scrub along the highway is one of very few places in North America to meet it.

When Mid-June, from the tops of the willows.

Where The Dempster and the Tombstones

Surfbird on the alpine scree

It winters on rocky sea coasts from Alaska to Chile and nests on bare mountain-top gravel, and the Yukon passes off the Dempster are where the two ends of that life meet.

When Mid-June to early July, on high scree.

Where The Dempster and the Tombstones

Trumpeter Swan at Marsh Lake

Thousands stage on the first open water of the year south of Whitehorse, weeks before anything else in the territory thaws.

When The second half of April.

Where Whitehorse and the southern lakes

Smith's Longspur on the tussock

It breeds on open tussock tundra in a narrow band across the north and is missed far more often than it is absent, because it sits still and lets people walk past.

When Mid-June to early July, on tussock.

Where The Dempster and the Tombstones

Gyrfalcon over the pass

It nests on the crags above the highway and hunts ptarmigan across the tundra below, which is the largest falcon in the world doing the plainest thing.

When June and July, on the pass crags.

Where The Dempster and the Tombstones, Old Crow and the far north

White-tailed Ptarmigan on the Kluane ridges

The smallest of the three ptarmigan and the one that stays highest, on the bare alpine ridges above the treeline all year.

When July, on the alpine ridges.

Where Kluane and the south-west

Long-tailed Jaeger over the tundra

It hunts lemmings over open tundra rather than chasing seabirds, and hovers like a huge tern while it does. Its numbers rise and fall with the lemming cycle.

When Mid-June to July, over open tundra.

Where The Dempster and the Tombstones, Old Crow and the far north

Northern Hawk-Owl on a spruce spire

It hunts by day from the top of a dead spruce, which makes it one of the easier owls to find, and burnt taiga along the roads is where to look.

When All year, most visible in June.

Where Dawson and the Yukon River, Kluane and the south-west

When to Go

The Yukon's birding year is short and sharp. Almost everything worth crossing the continent for happens between the middle of June and the middle of July, with one earlier exception: the swans that stage on the first open water south of Whitehorse in April.

The swan staging

The second half of April

Trumpeter and Tundra Swans gather in thousands on the first open water of the year.

Go for Marsh Lake and M'Clintock Bay, south-east of Whitehorse.

Trade off The rest of the territory is still frozen and the north is unreachable.

The tundra breeding season

Mid-June to mid-July

The Dempster tundra and the alpine passes hold their whole breeding list at once.

Go for Tombstone Territorial Park and the North Fork Pass.

Trade off Mosquitoes are severe and the road can close for hours after heavy rain.

The alpine window

Late June and July

Snow clears from the Kluane ridges and the high-country birds are reachable on foot.

Go for Kluane National Park from Haines Junction.

Trade off The walk to the alpine is long, and grizzly precautions apply everywhere.

The autumn move

Late August and September

Tundra turns red, raptors move south along the ranges and the first swans return.

Go for The Dempster and the southern lakes.

Trade off Snow can close the passes from the middle of September.

The Dempster and the Tombstones

The only public road in Canada across the Arctic Circle, with tundra birds on the verge

The Dempster runs 740 km from the Klondike to the Mackenzie Delta, and Tombstone Territorial Park sits across its first hundred kilometres. The North Fork Pass at kilometre 71 puts a car park in alpine tundra, which is why this is the one place in the country where the high arctic breeding birds can be watched without a charter.

  • Habitat: Alpine tundra and scree above a granite range, dwarf birch and willow, sedge tundra and tussock, and black spruce taiga below.
  • When: Mid-June to mid-July, when everything is singing and the road is open its whole length.
  • Getting there: Dawson City is the base and the last full service before the highway starts. The Dempster is gravel for 740 km with fuel at Eagle Plains alone, 370 km in, and two spare tyres is the usual advice.
  • Base yourself in: Dawson City
  • Look for: Long-tailed Jaeger, American Golden Plover, Surfbird, Wandering Tattler, Bluethroat, Northern Wheatear, Smith's Longspur, Willow Ptarmigan, Rock Ptarmigan, Gyrfalcon, Lapland Longspur, Arctic Warbler
  • Parks and reserves: Tombstone Territorial Park (State or provincial park)
  • Also covers: Dempster Highway, Eagle Plains, North Fork Pass, Tombstone Territorial Park
  • Also watch for: Grizzly bears, caribou of the Porcupine herd and Dall sheep all use the pass country, and the caribou migration crosses the highway in autumn.

Kluane and the south-west

The largest icefield outside the poles, with alpine ridges reachable on foot from the road

Kluane holds the highest mountains in Canada and the largest non-polar icefield on earth, both of them in the south-western corner of the Yukon, and along its eastern edge the front ranges rise straight off the highway. The alpine there is walkable, which is unusual in this part of the world, and the lake shores below hold migrant waterfowl.

  • Habitat: Icefield and glacier, alpine ridge and scree, subalpine willow and shrub, boreal spruce and aspen, and a large glacial lake.
  • When: Late June and July, once the snow has cleared from the ridges and the trails are open.
  • Getting there: Whitehorse is the airport, ninety minutes east of Haines Junction. The park is free to enter and has a visitor centre, and the alpine is reached on foot from marked trailheads on the highway.
  • Base yourself in: Haines Junction
  • Look for: White-tailed Ptarmigan, Golden Eagle, American Dipper, Varied Thrush, Say's Phoebe, Harlequin Duck, Barrow's Goldeneye, Trumpeter Swan, Bohemian Waxwing, Northern Hawk-Owl, Spruce Grouse, Canada Jay
  • Parks and reserves: Kluane National Park and Reserve, Kluane / Wrangell-St. Elias / Glacier Bay / Tatshenshini-Alsek (Protected area)
  • Also covers: Haines Junction, Kathleen Lake, Kluane / Wrangell-St. Elias / Glacier Bay / Tatshenshini-Alsek, Kluane Lake, Kluane National Park and Reserve
  • Also watch for: Dall sheep are visible on the Sheep Mountain slopes above Kluane Lake from the road, and the park holds one of the densest grizzly populations in the north.

Whitehorse and the southern lakes

The first open water of the northern spring, and the swans that wait on it

The M'Clintock river keeps a patch of Marsh Lake open weeks before the rest of the southern lakes thaw, and every spring Trumpeter and Tundra Swans gather there in thousands on their way north. The territory runs an interpretation centre on the shore for the few weeks it lasts.

  • Habitat: Lake shore and river mouth that stays ice-free, boreal spruce and aspen, wetland and marsh, and glacial dune.
  • When: The second half of April for the swan staging, and May and June for the breeding birds around the lakes.
  • Getting there: Whitehorse has the territory's only large airport. Marsh Lake is forty minutes south-east on the paved highway, and the swan viewing centre opens for the staging weeks only.
  • Base yourself in: Whitehorse
  • Look for: Trumpeter Swan, Tundra Swan, Sandhill Crane, Barrow's Goldeneye, Bald Eagle, Northern Harrier, Short-eared Owl, Boreal Owl, Great Grey Owl, Bohemian Waxwing, White-crowned Sparrow, Redpoll
  • Parks and reserves: Millenium Trail riverfront (Protected area)
  • Also covers: Carcross, M'Clintock Bay, Marsh Lake, Millenium Trail riverfront, Whitehorse
  • Also watch for: The Carcross desert nearby is a square mile of dune left by a glacial lake, and is often called the smallest desert in the world.

Dawson and the Yukon River

Taiga along a great northern river, in country the gold rush cut over and the forest took back

The Yukon River valley around Dawson is boreal forest, river bar and old placer working, and it holds the taiga breeding birds that the tundra further north does not. The Top of the World Highway climbs out of it onto an open ridge that runs west to the Alaskan border.

  • Habitat: White and black spruce taiga, aspen and balsam poplar on the river bars, willow scrub on old workings, and open ridge tundra above.
  • When: June for the breeding birds, and early September when the Sandhill Cranes pass over the valley in flocks.
  • Getting there: Dawson City has a small airport and a paved highway from Whitehorse, about six hours. The Top of the World Highway is gravel and closed from October to May.
  • Base yourself in: Dawson City
  • Look for: Boreal Owl, Northern Hawk-Owl, Spruce Grouse, Canada Jay, Grey-cheeked Thrush, Blackpoll Warbler, Fox Sparrow, White-crowned Sparrow, Bohemian Waxwing, Redpoll, Sandhill Crane, Pacific Loon
  • Parks and reserves: Tr’ondëk-Klondike (Protected area), Jëjik Dhä Dënezhu Kek’it (Protected area)
  • Also covers: Dawson City, Jëjik Dhä Dënezhu Kek’it, Top of the World Highway, Tr’ondëk-Klondike, Yukon River
  • Also watch for: The Klondike placer workings left tailing ridges along the creeks that are visible from the air and now carry willow and aspen scrub.

Old Crow and the far north

A wetland of half a million hectares above the Arctic Circle, reached only by air

The Old Crow Flats are a vast system of shallow lakes on the north slope. Hundreds of thousands of waterfowl use them, and so does the Porcupine caribou herd. There is no road to any of it. Old Crow is the territory's only fly-in community and the base for everything up here.

  • Habitat: Shallow thaw lake and wet sedge tundra, low willow, tussock on the slopes, and coastal tundra and sea ice at Herschel.
  • When: Late June and July, which is the whole season. The lakes are frozen for eight months of the year.
  • Getting there: Old Crow is reached by scheduled flight from Whitehorse or Dawson, and there is no road. Vuntut and Herschel Island both need a charter and permission, and visits are arranged through the Vuntut Gwitchin government.
  • Base yourself in: Old Crow
  • Look for: Tundra Swan, Snow Goose, Brant Goose, Pacific Loon, Red-throated Loon, Long-tailed Jaeger, Red Phalarope, Red-necked Phalarope, Buff-breasted Sandpiper, Pectoral Sandpiper, Snowy Owl, Peregrine Falcon
  • Parks and reserves: Herschel Island - Qikiqtaruk Territorial Park (State or provincial park), Vuntut National Park, Ivvavik National Park
  • Also covers: Herschel Island - Qikiqtaruk Territorial Park, Ivvavik National Park, Old Crow Flats, Vuntut National Park
  • Also watch for: The Porcupine caribou herd calves on the coastal plain north of here, one of the last great land migrations left in North America.

Frequently Asked Questions

When is the best time to go birding in the Yukon?

Mid-June to mid-July. The season is short and it holds nearly the whole list: the tundra and alpine birds are all singing, the light barely goes, and the Dempster is open its whole length. The other date is late April, for the swan staging at Marsh Lake.

What do I need for the Dempster Highway?

A vehicle you are willing to damage and at least one spare tyre, ideally two. It is 740 km of gravel with fuel only at Eagle Plains, 370 km in. Heavy rain turns sections to clay, there is no mobile signal for most of it, and the tyre repair advice is not folklore.

Where do I stand for the tundra birds?

The North Fork Pass, at kilometre 71 of the Dempster in Tombstone Territorial Park. It puts a car park in alpine tundra, which is why Bluethroat, Surfbird, Smith's Longspur and American Golden Plover are all reachable here without a charter flight.

Can I get to Old Crow?

By scheduled flight from Whitehorse or Dawson, and no other way. There is no road. Vuntut National Park and Herschel Island both need a charter and permission, arranged through the Vuntut Gwitchin government.

How bad are the mosquitoes?

Bad enough to change the trip. On the tundra in late June a head net is standard equipment rather than a precaution, and the same weeks that hold every breeding bird hold the worst of them.

Can I bird here with limited mobility?

Sites on this page differ from each other more than any rating could carry, so this is the framework the standard British guides use, and the reserve's own page and warden hold the answers.

  • Permission · some reserves open ground to disabled visitors that is closed to everyone else, and it is granted by asking.
  • Priority · parking bays, hides and the nearest paths are often held for disabled visitors. Ask what is reserved and how to claim it.
  • Parking · how close a car reaches the birds shapes the whole day. Ring ahead for the nearest hard standing to the water or the hide.
  • Paths · surfaces, gradients and gate widths vary inside a single reserve. Ask which trail is boarded, surfaced or level.
  • Perches · a hide with a low window, or a bench where the view opens, turns a viewpoint into a morning.
