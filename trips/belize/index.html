At a glance

Best months
  • February to May for the country as a whole
  • April for the Crooked Tree concentrations
  • January to March if the Red Bank macaws are the reason for the trip

Routes

Getting in
Philip Goldson International (BZE) outside Belize City takes the flights from the United States, and there is no long-haul service from Europe. Internal hops to Punta Gorda and Placencia save most of a day on the Southern Highway.
Getting around
A hired vehicle covers the mainland on surfaced highways, though the Chiquibul Road and the Rio Bravo tracks need clearance, and English plus a Belize dollar fixed at two to the US dollar make this the easiest country in the region to drive yourself.

When to Go

Belize runs a dry season from February to May and a wet one from June to November, and the split is far sharper in the north than in Toledo, which is wet in every month. The northern lagoons work the opposite way round to the southern wetlands: Crooked Tree concentrates birds as it dries out, and Aguacaliente fills.

Peak

February to May

The dry season. Tracks into the Rio Bravo and the Chiquibul are firm, the dry forest at Shipstern is leafless and open, and Crooked Tree draws down until the fish are trapped in the last of the water.

Go for Jabiru on nests, the lagoon concentrations at Crooked Tree in April, Ocellated Turkey displaying, and the wintering warblers still in until the end of April.

Trade off The busiest and priciest months, and Easter fills the cayes.

Green

June to August

The rains arrive and the country empties of visitors. Afternoon storms are the pattern rather than all-day rain, and the mornings are usually workable.

Go for Breeding activity everywhere, Aguacaliente filling and holding herons and storks, and rates at their lowest.

Trade off Unsurfaced roads into the Chiquibul and the Rio Bravo become a real question, and the hurricane season opens in June.

Migration

September to January

The wettest stretch in the south, and the arrival of the North American migrants through the cayes and the coastal forest.

Go for Migration through the cayes in autumn, Jabiru back at their nest trees from November, and the Red Bank macaws arriving in January.

Trade off September and October are the peak of the hurricane season, and Toledo is at its wettest.

Crooked Tree and the northern lagoons

A Ramsar lagoon system an hour from the airport, holding the largest breeding population of Jabiru in Central America

Crooked Tree is a village on a causeway between lagoons, and the Jabiru nest in the pines around it from November. The water drops through the dry season and concentrates everything that eats fish into a shrinking margin, which is why April is the month people come.

  • Habitat: Seasonally flooded lagoon, logwood swamp and broadleaf forest on low limestone, with pine savanna on the ridges between.
  • When: February to May, and April is the peak as the lagoons draw down. Jabiru are on nests from November, and the wintering North American waterfowl arrive with them.
  • Getting there: Thirty-three miles north of Belize City on the Philip Goldson Highway, then three miles west on the causeway, which floods in a hard wet season.
  • Look for: Jabiru, Snail Kite, Limpkin, Northern Jacana, Boat-billed Heron, Black-collared Hawk
  • National park: Crooked Tree Wildlife Sanctuary
  • Stops:
    • Crooked Tree causeway : The road across the lagoon. A car works as a hide and the water lies on both sides.
    • Community Baboon Sanctuary : Riverine forest along the Belize River, run by seven villages. Howler monkeys are habituated along the trails.
    • : A Maya site in dry broadleaf forest. It is quiet early and good for the Yucatan species at the plaza edges.
  • Visitor centres: Crooked Tree Wildlife Sanctuary Visitor Centre
  • Also covers: Altun Ha, Bermudian Landing, Burrell Boom, Community Baboon Sanctuary, Crooked Tree causeway, Crooked Tree Wildlife Sanctuary Visitor Centre
  • Also watch for: Black howler monkeys, called baboons here, at the Community Baboon Sanctuary at Bermudian Landing, and Morelet's crocodile in the lagoons.

The Rio Bravo and Orange Walk

Two hundred and fifty thousand acres of private conservation land in the northwest, with two field stations inside it

The Rio Bravo Conservation and Management Area is the largest private reserve in the country and the two lodges inside it, La Milpa and Chan Chich, sit in forest rather than beside it. Ocellated Turkey walk the clearings at both, and the New River lagoon at Lamanai is worked by boat.

  • Habitat: Semi-deciduous broadleaf forest on limestone, with escarpment, bajo swamp forest and the New River lagoon along its eastern edge.
  • When: February to May for the driest tracks and the clearest forest mornings. Ocellated Turkey display from March, and the migrant warblers hold through to April.
  • Getting there: Three to four hours from Belize City by road through Orange Walk to Blue Creek, and the last stretch is unpaved. Lamanai is usually reached by boat up the New River from Orange Walk Town, which is the birding half of the journey.

Shipstern and the Corozal coast

Dry forest and a saline lagoon in the far northeast of Belize, holding birds that reach the country nowhere else

Shipstern is the Belizean end of a dry forest that runs north over the border, and its species list reflects that more than it does the rest of the country. Black Catbird and the peninsula's endemic jay are both here, and the lagoon takes waders and terns in numbers.

  • Habitat: Semi-deciduous dry forest and hardwood hammock over limestone, with a shallow saline lagoon and mangrove along its edge.
  • When: February to May, when the tracks are firm. The dry forest is deciduous, so the birds are far easier to see before the rains green it up.
  • Getting there: Three hours from Belize City to Sarteneja by road through Orange Walk, or by the Corozal water taxi. The reserve entrance is three miles south of the village.
  • Look for: Black Catbird, Yucatan Jay, Yucatan Nightjar, Roseate Spoonbill, Rufous-tailed Jacamar, Olive-throated Parakeet
  • National park: Shipstern Conservation and Management Area
  • Stops:
    • Shipstern botanical trail : A short labelled loop through dry forest behind the visitor centre. It is the best introduction to the Yucatan species.

The Cayes and the Barrier Reef

The longest barrier reef in the northern hemisphere, with a red-footed booby colony on an atoll beyond it

Half Moon Caye was the first nature reserve declared in Belize, in 1928, and its four thousand Red-footed Boobies nest in orange-flowered ziricote where a walkway puts you level with them. The inner cayes are a different trip: mangrove and littoral forest that catch migrants crossing the Gulf of Mexico.

  • Habitat: Coral caye with littoral forest, coconut and ziricote, ringed by mangrove and seagrass over the reef platform.
  • When: April and May for the spring migration through the inner cayes, and the boobies are on Half Moon Caye all year with chicks from December.
  • Getting there: Water taxis from Belize City to Caye Caulker and Ambergris take under an hour. Half Moon Caye is on Lighthouse Reef, fifty miles offshore and reached on a dive boat or a chartered day trip.
  • Look for: Red-footed Booby, Magnificent Frigatebird, Brown Booby, White-tailed Tropicbird, Royal Tern, Black Catbird
  • National parks: Half Moon Caye Natural Monument, Caye Caulker Forest Reserve
  • Stops:
    • Half Moon Caye booby colony : A raised walkway into the canopy of the ziricote. It puts you level with four thousand nesting Red-footed Booby.
    • : The protected north end of the island, walked from the Split. It is one of the last stands of littoral forest and it holds Black Catbird.
    • : The northern tip of Ambergris, where Belize meets Mexico. A boat runs there from San Pedro.
  • Also covers: Ambergris Caye and Bacalar Chico, Belize City, Caye Caulker Forest Reserve, Caye Caulker littoral forest, Half Moon Caye booby colony, Half Moon Caye Natural Monument, Lighthouse Reef, San Pedro
  • Also watch for: The Blue Hole sits inside Lighthouse Reef, and the whole reef system is a World Heritage site.

Cayo and the Mountain Pine Ridge

Granite uplands of Caribbean pine over broadleaf gorges, holding one of the last accessible Orange-breasted Falcon populations

The Pine Ridge is granite rather than limestone, and everything about it differs from the rest of Belize, the birds included. Orange-breasted Falcon hunt the gorges at Thousand Foot Falls and the Macal, and the road on into Chiquibul reaches Caracol, where the forest is tall and the Ocellated Turkey are tame.

  • Habitat: Caribbean pine savanna covers granite and quartzite, with broadleaf gallery forest in the gorges. It gives way to tall wet forest in the Chiquibul.
  • When: February to May for the roads, which are unsurfaced beyond the reserve gate. The falcons are on the cliffs year round and easiest to find when they are feeding young from March.
  • Getting there: San Ignacio is two hours from Belize City on the George Price Highway. The Pine Ridge gate is an hour south on the Chiquibul Road, and Caracol another two hours beyond it on a road that needs clearance.
  • Look for: Orange-breasted Falcon, Stygian Owl, Ocellated Turkey, King Vulture, Keel-billed Toucan, Collared Aracari
  • National parks:
    • Mountain Pine Ridge Forest Reserve
    • Chiquibul National Park
    • Blue Hole National Park, A collapsed cave pool and forest trail on the Hummingbird Highway. Walk between St Herman's Cave and the pool.
  • Stops:
    • : A viewpoint across a granite gorge. It is the most reliable place in the country to look for Orange-breasted Falcon.
    • Caracol : The largest Maya site in Belize, in tall forest at the end of the Chiquibul road. Turkeys walk the plazas at first light.
    • : A small lodge on a Maya plazuela above the Roaring Creek valley. Forest trails leave from the door.

The Maya Mountains and Cockscomb

The first jaguar preserve declared anywhere, and a village that fills with Scarlet Macaws each spring

Cockscomb Basin was set aside for jaguars in 1986 and the birding is what tall wet forest gives: mixed flocks along the trails and both manakins lekking within a short walk of the entrance. Toledo and the deep south

The wettest corner of Belize, where foothill species that reach the country only here cross in from over the border

Toledo gets more rain than anywhere else in Belize and holds a set of foothill birds found nowhere else in it. Aguacaliente is a seasonal wetland the herons and storks use as it fills, and the Sarstoon Temash mangroves are the least visited protected area in the country.

Frequently Asked Questions

When is the best time to go birding in Belize?

February to May, when the north is dry, the tracks into the Rio Bravo and the Chiquibul are firm and the Crooked Tree lagoons have drawn down enough to concentrate everything that eats fish. April is the single best month. If the Red Bank macaws are the reason for the trip, come January to March instead.

How many bird species does Belize have?

Around 590 species have been recorded, which is a large list for a country this small. The reason is position: Belize sits where the dry-forest birds of the peninsula reach their southern limit and the Central American forest birds reach their northern one, so a two-hour drive crosses between two avifaunas.

Do I need a guide, or can I bird Belize independently?

Independently, more easily than anywhere else in the region. English is the official language of Belize, the highways are surfaced and the reserves are signed. A guide is worth it for the Chiquibul road, for Red Bank, and for anything reached by boat.

Where do I see a Jabiru?

Crooked Tree Wildlife Sanctuary, an hour north of the airport. Belize holds the largest breeding population in Central America and the birds use the same nest trees each year, so ask at the visitor centre which are occupied before setting out.

Is Belize expensive?

More than its neighbours. The Belize dollar is fixed at two to the US dollar, prices are quoted in both, and lodges inside protected areas carry a premium for being there. The saving is that you can drive yourself, which most of the region does not allow so easily.

What is the malaria and mosquito situation?

Malaria risk is low and concentrated in the rural south and west rather than on the cayes or in Belize City. Mosquitoes and biting flies are worst in the wet season and on the coast, and long sleeves matter more in the mangrove than repellent does.

Can I combine Belize with Guatemala?

Easily, and the two guides overlap on purpose. The Peten forest runs straight across the border, so Tikal and the Rio Bravo hold much the same avifauna from two sides, and the drive west from San Ignacio reaches the Guatemalan border in half an hour.

How wet is the wet season really?

It depends entirely where you are. Punta Gorda takes about four times the rain Corozal does, and Toledo has no dry month at all. The north is genuinely dry February to May; the south is wet with a less wet stretch.

Are the cayes worth it for birds?

For two things. Half Moon Caye's booby colony is a set piece nothing on the Belizean mainland matches, and the littoral forest on Caye Caulker and Ambergris catches migrants crossing the Gulf of Mexico in spring. Otherwise the mainland holds the list.

What field guide should I take?

Birds of Belize by H Lee Jones, illustrated by Dana Gardner. It is the only guide written for the country alone and covers the full national list, which the regional Mexico and Central America guides do at greater weight.

Can I bird here with limited mobility?

Often yes, and it varies by site. A good deal of the birding here happens from roadsides, hides and lodge grounds rather than on long walks, and some reserves keep flat, surfaced trails. Ask a lodge or reserve directly about step free access, surfaces and seating before you book. The Birdability Map records those details for birding sites, and their Access Considerations guide lists the questions worth asking. Coverage is thin outside the United States, so read a blank area as unrecorded rather than unsuitable.
