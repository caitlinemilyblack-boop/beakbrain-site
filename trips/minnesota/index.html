Birding in Minnesota

Minnesota puts Great Grey, Northern Hawk and Boreal Owls on roadside perches in daylight an hour from an airport, and holds the largest peatland in the lower states two hundred miles west of them.

Birds that decide the trip

Great Grey Owl at Sax-Zim

In an irruption winter it hunts voles from roadside perches in broad daylight, and the bog is the most reliable place on the continent to find one. Some winters it is common and some it is almost absent.

When January and February, along the county roads.

Where Sax-Zim Bog and the Duluth winter

Northern Hawk-Owl on a spruce top

It perches on the very tip of a dead spruce and hunts by day like a small falcon, which makes it findable by scanning skylines rather than by walking.

When December to February, on exposed spruce tops.

Where Sax-Zim Bog and the Duluth winter

Yellow Rail in the Red Lake peat

It calls like two stones tapped together, only after dark, from sedge so thick that seeing one is close to impossible. The northern peatlands are the stronghold for it.

When June, after dark in wet sedge.

Where Agassiz and the northern peatlands

Greater Prairie Chicken on the beach ridge

The males boom on traditional leks on the last unploughed tallgrass along an old glacial lake shoreline, and Felton is the classic place to watch it from a blind.

When Mid-April to mid-May, from a blind before first light.

Where The prairie and the pothole country

Connecticut Warbler in the tamarack

It walks rather than hops, sings from high in a tamarack and then vanishes, and the state's bogs hold as good a population as anywhere. Heard far more often than seen.

When June, in tamarack and spruce bog at dawn.

Where Agassiz and the northern peatlands, The north shore and the Boundary Waters

Boreal Owl in an irruption year

It moves south only when the vole crash forces it, and in those winters the north shore takes birds that hunt in the open by day, weak enough with hunger to be approached far too closely.

When January to March in an irruption winter, along the shore road.

Where Sax-Zim Bog and the Duluth winter, The north shore and the Boundary Waters

Cerulean Warbler on the river bluffs

The Mississippi bluff forest carries hardwood birds the boreal north of the state has none of, and this is the one most people come to the bluffs for.

When Mid-May to early July, from the canopy in the coulees.

Where The Mississippi and the southern bluffs

Sprague's Pipit over the prairie remnant

It song-flights very high over open grassland for minutes at a time, and reaches its eastern limit on the same beach ridge remnants that hold the prairie chickens.

When May and June, in flight over unploughed grassland.

Where The prairie and the pothole country

When to Go

Minnesota is one of very few places where the best month is January. The owls come south in winter and hunt in the open, and everything else here works in the other half of the year, which makes it a state to visit twice rather than once.

The owl winter

January and February

Sax-Zim holds Great Grey, Northern Hawk and Boreal Owls hunting from roadside perches in daylight.

Go for Sax-Zim Bog on the county roads, and the Duluth harbour for gulls and Snowy Owl.

Trade off It is severely cold, the days are short and everything else in the state is shut.

Typical daily average

The prairie leks

Mid-April to mid-May

Greater Prairie Chickens are booming on the beach ridge remnants at dawn.

Go for Felton Prairie and Rothsay from a booked blind, and the pothole wetlands around them.

Trade off The northern bogs are still frozen and the forest breeders have not arrived.

Typical daily average

The breeding season

June and early July

The boreal forest and the peatland hold their full list, Yellow Rail and Connecticut Warbler included.

Go for The Gunflint Trail, Big Bog boardwalk, and Agassiz refuge after dark.

Trade off Blackflies and mosquitoes are severe and the owls have gone north.

Typical daily average

The autumn passage

Mid-August to November

Hawk Ridge counts the raptor stream as the lake turns it, and Rice Lake fills with ducks.

Go for Hawk Ridge above Duluth, then Rice Lake and the Mississippi bluffs.

Trade off The breeding birds have finished singing and the first snow can arrive in October.

The owl winter

Duluth (DLH) 4 nights, back to Duluth

Four days in late January, and the single best owl watching in the country when the winter is right. Drive the Sax-Zim county roads slowly from first light, check the feeder stations the volunteers keep, and take the afternoons on the Duluth waterfront for gulls. Dress for minus twenty and carry a shovel.

The Mississippi bluffs

Minneapolis (MSP), Wabasha 3 nights, back to Minneapolis

A late May trip for the hardwood birds that the north of the state does not have. Work the bluff coulees at Frontenac and Whitewater for Cerulean Warbler, Acadian Flycatcher and Louisiana Waterthrush, then take the river overlooks on Highway 61 for eagles and swans.

Sax-Zim Bog and the Duluth winter

The best winter owl watching in the country, on gravel roads through a peat bog an hour from an airport

Sax-Zim is a spruce and tamarack bog north-west of Duluth, and in an irruption winter it holds Great Grey, Northern Hawk and Boreal Owls hunting from the roadside in daylight. Hawk Ridge above the city counts the autumn raptor migration as it is turned by the lake.

  • Habitat: Black spruce and tamarack peat bog, aspen and birch upland, hay meadow and roadside feeder stations, with a high basalt ridge above the lake.
  • When: January and February for the owls and the winter finches, and mid-August to November for the Hawk Ridge count.
  • Getting there: Duluth is the airport and the base. Sax-Zim is an hour north-west on gravel county roads that are plowed but icy, and the volunteer welcome centre posts current sightings through the winter.
  • Base yourself in: Duluth
  • Look for: Great Grey Owl, Northern Hawk-Owl, Boreal Owl, Snowy Owl, Canada Jay, Boreal Chickadee, Black-backed Woodpecker, Bohemian Waxwing, Pine Grosbeak, Evening Grosbeak, Redpoll, Northern Shrike
  • Also covers: Duluth Harbor, Hawk Ridge Nature Reserve, Sax-Zim Bog
  • Also watch for: Wolves and pine marten both use the bog, and moose are occasional on the quieter roads.

The north shore and the Boundary Waters

A million acres of canoe country, with the boreal breeding list that goes with it

The Superior forest runs from Minnesota to the Canadian border, and the wilderness inside it is water rather than road: over a thousand lakes reached by portage. The Gunflint Trail cuts fifty miles into it on tarmac, which is how most visitors reach the breeding boreal birds without a canoe.

  • Habitat: Spruce, fir and jack pine, aspen and birch, sphagnum bog and beaver flowage, with a thousand lakes and the basalt shore of Lake Superior.
  • When: June and early July for the breeding birds. Blackflies peak in June and mosquitoes take over in July.
  • Getting there: Duluth is the airport, two hours south, and Grand Marais the base. The Gunflint Trail is paved for fifty miles inland, and the wilderness itself needs a permit and a canoe with entry quotas by date.
  • Base yourself in: Grand Marais
  • Look for: Spruce Grouse, American Three-toed Woodpecker, Black-backed Woodpecker, Canada Jay, Boreal Chickadee, Connecticut Warbler, Golden-winged Warbler, Common Loon, Broad-winged Hawk, Ruffed Grouse, Two-barred Crossbill, Bohemian Waxwing
  • Also covers: Boundary Waters Canoe Area Wilderness, Gunflint Trail, Superior National Forest
  • Also watch for: Wolves, moose and black bear are all present, and the lakes carry one of the densest Common Loon populations anywhere.

Agassiz and the northern peatlands

The largest peatland in the lower states, with a boardwalk a mile into it

The peat north of Red Lake runs for hundreds of square miles and holds Yellow Rail, Connecticut Warbler and nesting Sharp-tailed Grouse. Agassiz refuge on its western edge adds open marsh and impoundments, with moose and a resident wolf pack.

  • Habitat: Open sedge and sphagnum peatland with stunted black spruce and tamarack, marsh and impoundment, aspen parkland and hay meadow.
  • When: Late May to early July. Yellow Rail calls after dark, so the peatland is worth a night as well as a morning.
  • Getting there: Grand Forks in North Dakota or Bemidji are the airports and Thief River Falls is the base. Agassiz has a refuge tour route, and Big Bog has a mile-long boardwalk out over open peat that is the only easy way into that habitat anywhere.
  • Base yourself in: Thief River Falls
  • Look for: Yellow Rail, Connecticut Warbler, LeConte's Sparrow, Nelson's Sparrow, Sharp-tailed Grouse, Sandhill Crane, American Bittern, Trumpeter Swan, Black-billed Magpie, Marbled Godwit, Wilson's Phalarope, Bobolink
  • Also covers: Agassiz National Wildlife Refuge, Big Bog State Recreation Area, Red Lake Peatland
  • Also watch for: A wolf pack is resident on the refuge and moose use the bog margins.

The prairie and the pothole country

The last unploughed tallgrass in the state, holding prairie chickens on their booming grounds

A handful of prairie remnants along the old Lake Agassiz beach ridge still carry Greater Prairie Chicken leks, and Felton is the classic place to watch them. Chestnut-collared Longspur and Sprague's Pipit reach their eastern limit on the same ground.

  • Habitat: Tallgrass prairie on gravel beach ridge, wet meadow and pothole wetland, with aspen bluffs on the margins.
  • When: Mid-April to mid-May at first light for the prairie chicken leks, and June for the grassland breeders.
  • Getting there: Fargo in North Dakota is the airport, half an hour west, and Moorhead the base. The leks are watched from a booked blind or from the road at dawn, and the prairie tracks turn to mud after rain.
  • Base yourself in: Moorhead
  • Look for: Greater Prairie Chicken, Chestnut-collared Longspur, Sprague's Pipit, Upland Sandpiper, Marbled Godwit, Wilson's Phalarope, Bobolink, Grasshopper Sparrow, Dickcissel, Western Meadowlark, Sharp-tailed Grouse, Sandhill Crane
  • Also covers: Bluestem Prairie Scientific and Natural Area, Felton Prairie, Rothsay Wildlife Management Area
  • Also watch for: Badger and coyote work the beach ridge, and the pothole wetlands hold muskrat in numbers.

The Mississippi and the southern bluffs

Wooded river bluffs and a lake that takes a hundred thousand Ring-necked Ducks

Rice Lake stages very large numbers of Ring-necked Duck on the autumn passage, and the Mississippi bluffs south of the cities carry the hardwood breeding birds that the north of the state does not have, Cerulean Warbler and Acadian Flycatcher among them.

  • Habitat: Wooded limestone bluff and coulee, floodplain forest and backwater along the river, wild rice lake, and dry goat prairie on the south-facing slopes.
  • When: May and June for the bluff breeders, and October and November for the duck and swan passage on the river.
  • Getting there: Minneapolis is the airport, ninety minutes north-west, and Wabasha the base. Highway 61 runs the length of the bluffs with frequent river overlooks, and Whitewater has a state park visitor centre.
  • Base yourself in: Wabasha
  • Look for: Cerulean Warbler, Acadian Flycatcher, Louisiana Waterthrush, Golden-winged Warbler, Trumpeter Swan, Bald Eagle, Sandhill Crane, American Bittern, Henslow's Sparrow, Dickcissel, Ruffed Grouse, Common Loon
  • Also covers: Frontenac State Park, Rice Lake National Wildlife Refuge, Whitewater Wildlife Management Area
  • Also watch for: Timber rattlesnake still occur on the dry bluff prairies, and the river bottoms hold beaver and otter.

Frequently Asked Questions

When is the best time to go birding in Minnesota?

January if the owls are the point, because Sax-Zim in a good winter is the best owl watching on the continent. June for the boreal forest, the peatland and Yellow Rail. Late April for the prairie chicken leks. The three do not overlap at all, so pick one.

Are the Sax-Zim owls there every winter?

No. Great Grey and Northern Hawk Owl numbers depend on the vole cycle further north, so some winters hold dozens and some hold almost none. Check recent reports before booking, and the volunteer welcome centre in the bog posts current sightings.

How cold does it get?

Minus twenty Celsius is an ordinary January morning in the bog and it can be much colder. Cars need winter tyres and a shovel, batteries fail, and the practical limit on a day is how long you can stand outside the car rather than how much daylight there is.

Do I need a canoe for the Boundary Waters?

Not for the birds. The Gunflint Trail runs fifty paved miles into the forest with side roads and trails off it, and the boreal breeding list is all available from that. A canoe and a quota permit are needed only to go into the wilderness itself.

Where do I hear a Yellow Rail?

The northern peatlands after dark in June. Stand on a road or a boardwalk edge and listen: it sounds like two stones tapped together. Seeing one is another matter, and most people who record it never do.

Can I bird here with limited mobility?

Sites on this page differ from each other more than any rating could carry, so this is the framework the standard British guides use, and the reserve's own page and warden hold the answers.

  • Permission · some reserves open ground to disabled visitors that is closed to everyone else, and it is granted by asking.
  • Priority · parking bays, hides and the nearest paths are often held for disabled visitors. Ask what is reserved and how to claim it.
  • Parking · how close a car reaches the birds shapes the whole day. Ring ahead for the nearest hard standing to the water or the hide.
  • Paths · surfaces, gradients and gate widths vary inside a single reserve. Ask which trail is boarded, surfaced or level.
  • Perches · a hide with a low window, or a bench where the view opens, turns a viewpoint into a morning.

Every reserve above with its own link carries its access detail there, and a warden answers what a page cannot.
