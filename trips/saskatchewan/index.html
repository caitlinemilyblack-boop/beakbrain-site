Birding in Saskatchewan

Grasslands National Park is the only place in the country with a black-tailed prairie dog town, and the sky above it is dark enough to be a designated preserve.

Birds that decide the trip

Sprague's Pipit over Grasslands

It song-flights very high over unbroken native prairie for minutes at a time, and the park protects the last large piece of that in the country.

When Late May to July, in flight over native prairie.

Where Grasslands and the south-west

Baird's Sparrow in the mixed grass

It needs native mixed-grass that has been grazed rather than ploughed, and the park and the ranch country around it are where that survives.

When Late May to July, singing in the first hour of light.

Where Grasslands and the south-west

Burrowing Owl in a prairie dog town

The only black-tailed prairie dog colonies in the country are here, and the owls nest down the old burrows and stand on the mounds in daylight.

When May to August, at the dog towns on the ecotour road.

Where Grasslands and the south-west

Sanderling on the Chaplin salt flats

The saline flats stage shorebirds in numbers that made this a hemispheric reserve, and Sanderling in mid-May is the species the site is best known for.

When Mid-May, on the salt flats.

Where The southern lakes

Whooping Crane on the autumn passage

The Wood Buffalo flock crosses the province twice a year and the southern lakes are traditional stopping points, which puts a bird of a few hundred within reach of a road.

When Late September and October, on passage.

Where The southern lakes

American White Pelican at Lavallee Lake

The colony inside Prince Albert National Park is one of the larger ones in the country, and it is closed to visitors, which is why it is still that size.

When May to August, from the park lakes at a distance.

Where The aspen parkland

Connecticut Warbler in the northern muskeg

It walks rather than hops, sings from high in a tamarack and then vanishes, and the shield muskeg holds it in as good numbers as anywhere.

When June, in tamarack and spruce bog at dawn.

Where The boreal north

Great Grey Owl at the parkland edge

It hunts voles from low perches at the edges of meadows in the last hour of light, and the parkland belt between forest and prairie is where that habitat is.

When All year, at meadow edges at dusk.

Where The aspen parkland, The boreal north

When to Go

Saskatchewan gives about ten weeks. The grassland birds arrive in mid-May and stop singing by late July, the boreal north runs a few weeks behind, and the great waterfowl passages are in April and September when almost nothing is breeding.

The spring passage

April and early May

Snow Geese and cranes move north through the shallow lakes in very large numbers.

Go for Last Mountain Lake and Chaplin Lake, and the Quill Lakes.

Trade off The grassland breeders have not arrived and the north is still frozen.

Typical daily average

The grassland season

Mid-May to July

Sprague's Pipit, Baird's Sparrow and the longspurs hold the unbroken native prairie.

Go for Grasslands National Park and the Cypress Hills.

Trade off It is hot and shadeless by July and the park roads turn to gumbo after rain.

Typical daily average

The boreal season

June and early July

The northern forest and muskeg hold their warblers and the lakes their loons.

Go for Prince Albert National Park and the Churchill River country.

Trade off Blackflies and mosquitoes in the north are severe.

Typical daily average

The autumn passage

September and October

Chaplin Lake takes shorebirds on the salt flats and the geese come back through.

Go for Chaplin and Reed Lake, and Last Mountain Lake for the geese.

Trade off The breeding birds have gone and the first hard weather can arrive in October.

Prairie to shield

An open-jaw run north in the middle of June, from the last unbroken prairie in the country to Precambrian shield. Three days at Grasslands for the pipits and the prairie dog towns, then the saline lakes, the parkland and a finish on the Churchill River. About 1,100 km.

The parkland and the north

Saskatoon (YXE), Prince Albert 2 nights, La Ronge 2 nights, back to Saskatoon

Four days in late June across the transition from parkland to shield. Bison and pelicans in the national park, then north into spruce and muskeg for Connecticut Warbler, Spruce Grouse and Canada Jay. Take a head net.

Grasslands and the south-west

The only black-tailed prairie dog town in the country, under a dark-sky preserve

Grasslands National Park protects the last large piece of unbroken mixed-grass prairie in the country, with the only black-tailed prairie dog colonies north of the border and bison put back on the same ground. It is also a dark-sky preserve, which is a fair measure of how few people are in it.

  • Habitat: Unbroken mixed-grass and short-grass prairie, eroded coulee and badland along the Frenchman River, prairie dog town, and lodgepole and aspen on the Cypress Hills plateau.
  • When: Mid-May to early July for the breeding birds. The clay roads become impassable after rain and there is no shade at all.
  • Getting there: Regina is the airport, four hours east, and Val Marie the base. The park is free with gravel ecotour roads, and there are no services inside it.
  • Base yourself in: Val Marie
  • Look for: Sprague's Pipit, Baird's Sparrow, Chestnut-collared Longspur, Thick-billed Longspur, Lark Bunting, Burrowing Owl, Ferruginous Hawk, Golden Eagle, Prairie Falcon, Long-billed Curlew, Mountain Plover, Sage Grouse
  • Also covers: Cypress Hills Interprovincial Park, Frenchman River, Grasslands National Park, Val Marie
  • Also watch for: Bison were returned to the park in 2005, and the prairie dog towns support black-footed ferret releases and the burrowing owls.

The southern lakes

Saline lakes that stage shorebirds and geese in numbers that make one of them a hemispheric reserve

Chaplin Lake is a saline lake mined for sodium sulphate, and the shallow water around the workings stages shorebirds in numbers that made it a hemispheric shorebird reserve. Last Mountain Lake north of it is the oldest bird sanctuary in North America, set aside in 1887.

  • Habitat: Shallow saline and alkaline lakes with salt flats, sodium sulphate workings, marsh and wet meadow, and cropland and stubble around.
  • When: Mid-May for the northbound shorebirds and April and September for the geese and cranes.
  • Getting there: Regina is the airport and the base. Chaplin has an interpretive centre on the highway with a viewing platform, and Last Mountain Lake has a gravel refuge road.
  • Base yourself in: Regina
  • Look for: Sanderling, Semipalmated Sandpiper, Baird's Sandpiper, Stilt Sandpiper, American Avocet, Piping Plover, Snow Goose, Ross's Goose, Sandhill Crane, Whooping Crane, American White Pelican, Franklin's Gull
  • Also covers: Chaplin Lake, Last Mountain Lake, Quill Lakes, Reed Lake
  • Also watch for: The lakes hold muskrat and the surrounding stubble carries one of the larger white-tailed deer populations in the province.

The aspen parkland

The belt where prairie turns to forest, with a pelican colony and a bison herd inside a park

The parkland is aspen bluff and pothole between the prairie and the boreal forest, and Prince Albert National Park sits across that transition with a free-ranging plains bison herd and a large American White Pelican colony on Lavallee Lake.

  • Habitat: Aspen bluff and pothole wetland, mixed boreal and parkland forest, large lakes with island colonies, and fescue grassland in the south of the park.
  • When: Late May to July for the breeding birds, and April and October for the waterfowl passage.
  • Getting there: Saskatoon is the airport and the base. The national park charges an entry fee, the Lavallee Lake colony is closed and watched from a distance, and Redberry Lake is a free biosphere reserve.
  • Base yourself in: Saskatoon
  • Look for: American White Pelican, Western Grebe, Red-necked Grebe, Common Loon, Black Tern, Sandhill Crane, Sharp-tailed Grouse, Great Grey Owl, Barred Owl, Broad-winged Hawk, Bobolink, LeConte's Sparrow
  • Also covers: Duck Lake, Prince Albert National Park, Redberry Lake, Saskatoon
  • Also watch for: Prince Albert holds a free-ranging plains bison herd and one of the more studied wolf populations in the country.

The boreal north

Shield, muskeg and a canoe river, with the largest active sand dunes this far north anywhere

North of the parkland the province turns to Precambrian shield, spruce and ten thousand lakes, with the Churchill River running east through the middle of it. The Athabasca dunes on the far northern lake are the largest active sand surface at this latitude anywhere on earth.

  • Habitat: Spruce and jack pine on Precambrian rock, muskeg and sedge fen, thousands of lakes and a large canoe river, and active sand dunes on the far northern shore.
  • When: June and early July for the breeding birds, which is also when the blackflies are worst.
  • Getting there: Saskatoon is the airport and La Ronge the road end. Everything beyond is float plane or canoe, and the Athabasca dunes are a fly-in park with no facilities at all.
  • Base yourself in: La Ronge
  • Look for: Common Loon, Bald Eagle, Osprey, Spruce Grouse, Black-backed Woodpecker, American Three-toed Woodpecker, Canada Jay, Boreal Chickadee, Connecticut Warbler, Cape May Warbler, Bay-breasted Warbler, Boreal Owl
  • Also covers: Athabasca Sand Dunes, Churchill River, La Ronge, Prince Albert
  • Also watch for: Woodland caribou hold the northern shield and the Athabasca dunes carry ten plant species found nowhere else on earth.

The Qu’Appelle and the south-east

A glacial meltwater valley cut into flat cropland, with woodland birds in the bottom of it

The Qu’Appelle is a broad valley cut by meltwater at the end of the ice age, and it holds a chain of lakes and enough woodland on its slopes to carry birds the cropland above has none of. Moose Mountain to the south-east is an upland aspen island in the same way.

  • Habitat: Broad meltwater valley with a chain of lakes, aspen and elm woodland on the slopes, cropland on the plain above, and aspen upland at Moose Mountain.
  • When: Late May to July for the woodland breeders, and September for the passage down the valley.
  • Getting there: Regina is the airport and the base, with the valley immediately north of it. The provincial parks charge a small vehicle fee and the valley roads are paved.
  • Base yourself in: Regina
  • Look for: Baltimore Oriole, Rose-breasted Grosbeak, Ovenbird, Veery, Red-headed Woodpecker, Eastern Bluebird, Sharp-tailed Grouse, Upland Sandpiper, Bobolink, Western Meadowlark, Black-crowned Night Heron, Double-crested Cormorant
  • Also covers: Buffalo Pound Provincial Park, Estevan, Moose Mountain Provincial Park, Qu’Appelle Valley
  • Also watch for: Moose hold the aspen uplands and the valley lakes carry one of the province’s better pelican and cormorant nesting concentrations.

Frequently Asked Questions

When is the best time to go birding in Saskatchewan?

Mid-June, when the grassland birds are still singing and the boreal north has come into song. Mid-May is the answer if the Chaplin Lake shorebird passage is the point, and late September for the Whooping Cranes on the southern lakes.

What is at Grasslands National Park?

The last large piece of unbroken mixed-grass prairie in the country, the only black-tailed prairie dog colonies north of the border, reintroduced bison, and a dark-sky preserve. There are no services inside it and the clay roads fail after rain.

Why does Chaplin Lake matter?

It is a saline lake mined for sodium sulphate, and the shallow water around the workings stages shorebirds in numbers that made it a hemispheric shorebird reserve. Mid-May is the peak and the highway interpretive centre is the place to start.

How far north can I drive?

La Ronge is the practical road end. Beyond it the shield is float plane and canoe country, and the Athabasca Sand Dunes on the far northern lake are a fly-in park with no facilities of any kind.

Where do I see Whooping Cranes?

On passage in late September and October, at Last Mountain Lake and the other southern lakes. The birds are moving between Wood Buffalo and Texas, sightings are coordinated and reported, and the numbers involved are a few hundred in the whole world.

Can I bird here with limited mobility?

Sites on this page differ from each other more than any rating could carry, so this is the framework the standard British guides use, and the reserve's own page and warden hold the answers.

  • Permission · some reserves open ground to disabled visitors that is closed to everyone else, and it is granted by asking.
  • Priority · parking bays, hides and the nearest paths are often held for disabled visitors. Ask what is reserved and how to claim it.
  • Parking · how close a car reaches the birds shapes the whole day. Ring ahead for the nearest hard standing to the water or the hide.
  • Paths · surfaces, gradients and gate widths vary inside a single reserve. Ask which trail is boarded, surfaced or level.
  • Perches · a hide with a low window, or a bench where the view opens, turns a viewpoint into a morning.
