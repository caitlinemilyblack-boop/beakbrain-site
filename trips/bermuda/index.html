Birds that decide the trip

The cahow, back from eighteen pairs

The Bermuda petrel was thought extinct for three centuries until eighteen pairs were found on islets in Castle Harbour in 1951. The recovery programme moved birds to Nonsuch Island and the species now breeds there again.

When At the colony from October to June, and ashore only at night.

Where Nonsuch Island, Castle Harbour

White-tailed Tropicbird on the cliffs

Tropicbirds nest in holes in the limestone cliffs around the island and are known locally as longtails. They are the most conspicuous seabird here and are visible from the south shore all summer.

When March to October, while the birds are on the cliffs.

Where Spittal Pond, Cooper's Island

Eastern Bluebird in the nest boxes

Bermuda's bluebirds are a resident population maintained largely by an island-wide nest box scheme, since introduced sparrows and starlings took the natural cavities.

When Present year round, and breeding from March to July.

Where Spittal Pond, Somerset

North American strays in autumn

Bermuda sits far enough out in the Atlantic that autumn westerlies bring North American migrants to it in numbers, and Spittal Pond is where most of the scarce ones are found.

When September to November, on the autumn westerlies.

Where Spittal Pond

Terns on the harbour islets

Common and Roseate Terns nest on the small islets in Castle Harbour and around the island, on the same rocks the cahow recovery works from.

When May to August, while the terns are breeding.

Where Castle Harbour, Nonsuch Island

Yellow-crowned Night Heron in the mangroves

Yellow-crowned Night Herons were reintroduced to Bermuda to control the introduced land crab population, and they are now widespread in the mangrove ponds.

When Present year round.

Where Somerset, Spittal Pond

Warblers in the coppice

North American warblers pass through in both directions and winter in small numbers in the woodland and gardens. Northern Parula, American Redstart and Common Yellowthroat are the most regular.

When September to November and again in April and May.

Where Spittal Pond, Somerset

Osprey over the harbours

Ospreys hunt the shallow harbour water and are regular on passage, using the same shorelines the terns and tropicbirds work.

When Mainly on passage in spring and autumn.

Where Castle Harbour, Somerset

When to Go

Bermuda has two seasons that matter. Seabirds hold the island from spring into summer, and autumn westerlies bring North American strays, which is what most visiting birders come for.

The seabird season

March to August

Tropicbirds return to the cliffs and the terns to the harbour islets.

Go for Longtails are visible from any south shore road, terns are on the islets, and the cahows finish their season in June.

Trade off Land bird interest is low, and the vagrants that make the island famous are months away.

Typical daily average

Autumn passage

September to November

Westerlies carry North American migrants out to the island.

Go for This is the vagrant season, and Spittal Pond is where the scarce birds are found. The cahows return to the colony in October.

Trade off Hurricane season overlaps it, and a passing storm closes everything for a few days.

Winter

December to February

Quiet, mild and dominated by wintering waterfowl.

Go for Ducks and herons on the ponds, lingering warblers in the coppice, and cahows courting at the colony after dark.

Trade off The seabirds have gone and passage is over.

The island in a long weekend

Bermuda (BDA), Hamilton 2 nights, St George's 2 nights, back to Bermuda

Bermuda is small enough that a few days covers it. Spittal Pond takes the mornings, Cooper's Island gives tropicbirds and the view over Castle Harbour, and the western ponds fill the rest. Autumn is when the North American strays arrive.

Spittal Pond and the south shore

The largest nature reserve on the island, and the first place any vagrant is found

Spittal Pond is the biggest nature reserve in Bermuda and its brackish pond and surrounding woodland take almost every scarce migrant recorded on the island. Park rangers run a free guided tour of it, and the trail circuit takes about an hour.

  • Habitat: A brackish pond behind a rocky south shore, with casuarina and mixed woodland around it and open grassland on the seaward side. The south shore beyond is limestone cliff and sand.
  • When: September to November for autumn migrants, which is when North American strays arrive on the westerlies. Winter holds waterfowl on the pond and spring passage is lighter.
  • Getting there: Bermuda has one airport at St George's and nowhere on the island is more than a short drive. Spittal Pond is on South Road in Smith's Parish, with car parks at both the east and west ends.
  • Base yourself in: Hamilton
  • Look for: White-tailed Tropicbird, Great Blue Heron, Snowy Egret, Belted Kingfisher, Northern Parula, American Redstart, Common Yellowthroat, Grey Catbird, Eastern Bluebird, White-eyed Vireo, Common Ground Dove
  • National park: Spittal Pond Nature Reserve
  • Also covers: Devonshire, Hamilton, Spittal Pond Nature Reserve, Warwick
  • Also watch for: The Bermuda skink, the only endemic land vertebrate, survives at a handful of coastal sites.

Nonsuch Island and Castle Harbour

The cahow, brought back from fourteen pairs on four rocky islets

The Bermuda petrel, known locally as the cahow, was thought extinct for three hundred years until eighteen pairs were found on islets in Castle Harbour in 1951. The recovery programme moved birds to Nonsuch Island, and the colony there is the reason the species still exists.

  • Habitat: Small limestone islets and a restored island of native cedar and palmetto, surrounded by shallow harbour water and reef. Nonsuch is managed as a living museum of pre-colonial Bermudian habitat.
  • When: The cahow is present at the colony from October to June and comes ashore only at night. Access to Nonsuch is by permit and the nesting islets are closed.
  • Getting there: St George's is the nearest town and the airport is beside it. Cooper's Island Nature Reserve is open and gives the best chance of seeing tropicbirds and the harbour from land. Nonsuch itself needs official permission.
  • Base yourself in: St George's
  • Look for: Bermuda Petrel, White-tailed Tropicbird, Common Tern, Roseate Tern, Great Blue Heron, Yellow-crowned Night Heron, Osprey, Peregrine Falcon, Ruddy Turnstone, Sanderling
  • National parks: Nonsuch Island Nature Reserve, Cooper's Island Nature Reserve
  • Also covers: Castle Harbour, Cooper's Island Nature Reserve, Nonsuch Island Nature Reserve, St George's
  • Also watch for: Green turtles feed in the harbour seagrass, and the restored island carries the endemic Bermuda cedar and palmetto.

The western parishes and Somerset

Mangrove ponds and a long walking trail on an old railway bed

The western end of the island is quieter than the centre and holds several small mangrove ponds that take herons and waders. The old railway trail runs along much of it and gives access to woodland edge that roads do not reach.

  • Habitat: Mangrove-fringed ponds and inlets, with casuarina and mixed woodland, limestone hillside and sheltered harbour water.
  • When: September to November for migrants, and December to March for wintering herons and waterfowl on the ponds.
  • Getting there: Somerset is about forty minutes from the airport by road, or reachable by ferry from Hamilton. The railway trail is walked or cycled and runs the length of the parish.
  • Base yourself in: Somerset
  • Look for: Yellow-crowned Night Heron, Green Heron, Little Blue Heron, Belted Kingfisher, Northern Waterthrush, Blackpoll Warbler, Palm Warbler, Great Kiskadee, European Goldfinch, Eastern Bluebird
  • National park: Somerset Long Bay Nature Reserve
  • Also covers: Ely's Harbour, Royal Naval Dockyard, Sandys, Somerset Long Bay Nature Reserve
  • Also watch for: Land crabs are common in the mangrove edges, and the Bermuda cedar is recovering after the blight.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I bird here with limited mobility?

Sites on this page differ from each other more than any rating could carry, so this is the framework the standard British guides use, and the reserve's own page and warden hold the answers.

  • Permission · some reserves open ground to disabled visitors that is closed to everyone else, and it is granted by asking.
  • Priority · parking bays, hides and the nearest paths are often held for disabled visitors. Ask what is reserved and how to claim it.
  • Parking · how close a car reaches the birds shapes the whole day. Ring ahead for the nearest hard standing to the water or the hide.
  • Paths · surfaces, gradients and gate widths vary inside a single reserve. Ask which trail is boarded, surfaced or level.
  • Perches · a hide with a low window, or a bench where the view opens, turns a viewpoint into a morning.
