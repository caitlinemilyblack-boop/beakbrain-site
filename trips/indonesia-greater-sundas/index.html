At a glance

Best months
June to September for Java, Bali and the Sumatran lowlands, January to March if Gunung Leuser is the point
Regions
Gunung Gede-Pangrango and the Bogor highlandsGunung Halimun-SalakUjung Kulon and the western tipBaluran, Alas Purwo and East JavaBali Barat and the Bali StarlingWay Kambas and the Sumatran lowlandsKerinci Seblat and the Sumatran highlandsGunung Leuser and AcehTanjung Puting and southern KalimantanBalikpapan, Sungai Wain and East Kalimantan
Getting in
Jakarta (CGK) for West Java, Surabaya (SUB) or Banyuwangi (BWX) for the east, Denpasar (DPS) for Bali, Bandar Lampung (TKG) and Padang (PDG) for Sumatra, Medan (KNO) for Leuser, and Pangkalan Bun (PKN) or Balikpapan (BPN) for Kalimantan
Getting around
A hired car and driver on Java and Sumatra, a wooden klotok on the Sekonyer, and domestic flights between the islands

When to Go

The Greater Sundas do not share a season either, though the split is simpler than in Wallacea: the further east you go the more seasonal it gets. Sumatra and Kalimantan are equatorial and wet in every month; Java dries out from June; and Bali's western tip has a genuine dry season. What every part of the Greater Sundas shares is that the birding is done at dawn, because by nine in the morning the lowland forest has gone quiet.

Peak

June to September

The dry half of the year for Java, Bali and the Sumatran lowlands, and the season every operator sells.

Go for Gilimanuk falls to 33 mm in August, Banyuwangi to 50 mm, and Way Kambas to 67 mm. The Javan mountain trails are dry and the Baluran savanna is open and brown.

Trade off Bogor still takes over 200 mm in its driest month, so West Java is never actually dry, and July nights at Kersik Tuo drop to 13 C.

Typical daily average Way Kambas 22 to 30 °C / 72 to 86 °F, Kersik Tuo 13 to 22 °C / 55 to 72 °F, Balikpapan 23 to 31 °C / 73 to 88 °F

Sumatra

January to March

The window that suits northern Sumatra, which runs on its own calendar.

Go for Bukit Lawang records its driest month in February at 139 mm against 342 mm in October, so Leuser and the Alas valley are at their best when the rest of the region is not.

Trade off Java and the southern Sumatran lowlands are in the monsoon, with Bogor at 384 mm in January, so a chain itinerary in these months is a compromise.

Typical daily average Bukit Lawang 21 to 31 °C / 70 to 88 °F, Bogor 20 to 29 °C / 68 to 84 °F, Kumai 22 to 31 °C / 72 to 88 °F

Quiet

October to December

The build-up, and the half of the year with the fewest departures.

Go for Java and Bali stay dry into October and the pressure on guides and rooms is at its lowest of the year.

Trade off Bukit Lawang peaks at 342 mm in October, Kumai at 296 mm in December, and by then the Javan mountain trails are slippery again.

Typical daily average Banyuwangi 22 to 31 °C / 72 to 88 °F, Kersik Tuo 14 to 23 °C / 57 to 73 °F, Kumai 22 to 31 °C / 72 to 88 °F

Gunung Gede-Pangrango and the Bogor highlands

Two volcanoes and a montane national park two hours from Jakarta, and the most worked forest in Indonesia

Gede-Pangrango has been birded since the nineteenth century and it is still the standard first stop in Indonesia. The trail from Cibodas climbs from a botanic garden at 1,400 m into mossy forest and on to the crater rim, and almost every Javan montane endemic is on it. Javan Trogon and Javan Cochoa hold the middle slopes, Volcano Swiftlet hawks over the summit crater, and Javan Hawk-Eagle, the national bird, soars over the ridge on a clear morning.

  • Look for: Javan Trogon, Javan Cochoa, Javan Hawk-Eagle, Volcano Swiftlet, Javan Tesia, Javan Fulvetta, Pink-headed Fruit Dove, Javan Whistling Thrush
  • Also recorded in the parks here: Aberrant Bush Warbler, Black Paradise Flycatcher
  • National park: Mount Gede-Pangrango National Park
  • Stops:
    • , the botanic garden at the trailhead at 1,400 m, and the start of the climb into the montane forest
    • , the city below the mountains, with its own botanic garden and the roost of a large flying fox colony
  • Also watch for: Javan Gibbon calls from the lower forest at dawn and is heard far more often than seen, and Javan Leaf Monkey feeds along the Cibodas trail. The botanic garden at the trailhead is worth an hour of its own for the older plantings.

Gunung Halimun-Salak

The largest remaining block of lowland and submontane forest in Java, on the western end of the same range

Halimun holds what Gede does not: lowland and submontane forest in a block big enough for the birds that need it. The research station at Cikaniki sits in the middle of it with a canopy walkway, and the road in from Kabandungan is birded slowly. This is the reliable ground for Javan Owlet and Chestnut-bellied Partridge, and the Javan Gibbon population here is the largest anywhere.

  • Look for: Javan Owlet, Chestnut-bellied Partridge, Javan Frogmouth, Javan Green Magpie, Black-banded Barbet, Javan Sunbird, White-bibbed Babbler, Javan Scimitar Babbler
  • Also recorded in the parks here: Dark-backed Imperial Pigeon, Eyebrowed Wren-Babbler
  • National park: Mount Halimun Salak National Park
  • Stops:
    • , the research station and canopy walkway inside the park, with simple rooms and the trails starting at the door
  • Also watch for: The Javan Gibbon population in this park is the largest left, and the family groups around Cikaniki are the ones researchers follow. Javan Leopard is present and essentially never seen, and Javan Leaf Monkey is common along the road.

Ujung Kulon and the western tip

A peninsula at the western tip of Java, reached by boat, and the last place on earth the Javan Rhinoceros survives

Ujung Kulon is a boat trip from the north coast and a different Java from the terraced hills behind it: lowland forest running to the beach, grazing lawns cut by rhino and banteng, and hardly anybody there. Green Peafowl displays on the open ground at Cidaon, Milky Stork works the mangrove edge, and the offshore islands hold breeding terns and the view across to Krakatau.

  • Look for: Green Peafowl, Milky Stork, Green Junglefowl, Javan Kingfisher, Black-collared Starling, Grey-cheeked Green Pigeon, Eurasian Whimbrel, Cerulean Kingfisher
  • Also recorded in the parks here: Aleutian Tern, Black-nest Swiftlet
  • National park: Ujung Kulon National Park
  • Stops:
    • , the port on the west Java coast, and the departure point for the boat to Peucang island
    • , the island off the peninsula with the guesthouse and the crossing to the Cidaon grazing lawn
  • Also watch for: The last Javan Rhinoceros in the world live here, fewer than eighty of them, monitored on camera traps and never shown to visitors. Banteng graze the Cidaon lawn in the open, which is the sight most people come for, and Javan Gibbon calls from the forest behind.

Baluran, Alas Purwo and East Java

Savanna and dry monsoon forest at Java's eastern end, closer in feel to Africa than to the rest of the island

East Java is dry, and Baluran's savanna at Bekol is the one place in Indonesia that looks like an African plain, with a volcano behind it and Banteng and deer on the grass. The birding is open-country and easy: Green Peafowl displays in the early morning and Green Junglefowl crosses the track. Alas Purwo further south adds forest and a wetland, and Ijen's sulphur crater is on the same road.

  • Look for: Green Junglefowl, Javan Banded Pitta, Black-winged Myna, Javan Flameback, Small Minivet, Racket-tailed Treepie, Javan Woodcock, Lesser Adjutant
  • Also recorded in the parks here: Great Slaty Woodpecker, Maroon-breasted Philentoma
  • National park: Alas Purwo National Park
  • Stops:
    • , the savanna park on the northeast coast, with the Bekol grassland and the tower overlooking it
    • , the town at the eastern tip, the ferry port for Bali, and the base for Alas Purwo and Ijen
  • Also watch for: Banteng, the wild ox that domestic Balinese cattle descend from, graze the Bekol savanna in the open, and Java's dhole still hunts the same ground. Long-tailed Macaque is everywhere and the Ijen crater's blue sulphur flames draw a separate crowd overnight.

Bali Barat and the Bali Starling

Dry monsoon forest on Bali's northwest peninsula, and the last wild range of the island's own starling

Bali is a two-hour drive from the airport to the far end of the island, and the reason to make it is Bali Starling. The species fell to a handful of wild birds in the 1990s and survives now through releases into the Bali Barat national park and onto Nusa Penida, watched at feeding stations near Teluk Terima. The dry forest around it holds the eastern Javan lowland list and Menjangan island offshore is the diving.

  • Look for: Bali Starling, Java Sparrow, Javan Kingfisher, Yellow-vented Bulbul, Sunda Pygmy Woodpecker, Bar-winged Prinia, Cerulean Kingfisher, Olive-backed Tailorbird
  • Also recorded in the parks here: Kamchatka Leaf Warbler, Lemon-bellied White-eye
  • National park: West Bali National Park
  • Stops:
    • , the ferry town at Bali's western tip, with the national park headquarters and the starling sites a short drive east
  • Also watch for: Menjangan island takes its name from the Rusa deer that swim to it. The park's dry forest holds Long-tailed Macaque and Javan Leaf Monkey, and the reef off Menjangan is the best wall diving on Bali.

Way Kambas and the Sumatran lowlands

Lowland swamp forest at Sumatra's southern tip, and the most accessible lowland birding on the island

Way Kambas is three hours from Bandar Lampung and it is where a Sumatran trip usually begins. The forest at Way Kanan is flat, wet and hot, and the river is worked by boat before dawn for White-winged Duck, one of the rarest waterbirds in Asia. Storm's Stork perches on dead snags over the swamp, Great Argus calls from the interior all morning, and the night drives produce owls and civets.

  • Look for: White-winged Duck, Storm's Stork, Great Argus, Rhinoceros Hornbill, Wrinkled Hornbill, Blue-eared Kingfisher, Black-and-red Broadbill, Malayan Crested Fireback
  • Stops:
    • , the ranger post inside the park, with simple rooms, the river boat and the trails the park is reached from. Mapped on the park itself, because the bare name Way Kanan returns a regency 140 km away
    • , the airport city at Sumatra's southern end, three hours from the park
  • Also watch for: Sumatran Elephant is managed here in a conservation centre, and the wild population uses the same forest. Sumatran Rhinoceros survives in a breeding sanctuary within the park, one of very few places it exists at all, and Sumatran Tiger is present and unseen.

Kerinci Seblat and the Sumatran highlands

The Barisan range at its highest, on a volcano of 3,800 m, holding most of Sumatra's montane endemics

Kerinci is the hard, essential leg of a Sumatran trip. Kersik Tuo is a tea-estate village at 1,400 m under the volcano, and the birding runs from the tea rows up through the forest to the crater lake at Gunung Tujuh. Salvadori's Pheasant and Schneider's Pitta are both here and both are worked with patience, Sumatran Trogon holds the middle slopes, and the Tapan road down the western side adds the submontane species.

  • Look for: Salvadori's Pheasant, Schneider's Pitta, Graceful Pitta, Sumatran Trogon, Red-billed Partridge, Sumatran Wren-Babbler, Shiny Whistling Thrush, Sumatran Laughingthrush
  • Stops:
    • , the tea-estate village at 1,400 m under the volcano, with homestays and the forest trail starting behind it
    • , the coastal airport city, and a seven to nine hour drive from the mountain
  • Also watch for: Kerinci Seblat is the largest national park in Sumatra and holds the biggest remaining Sumatran Tiger population, monitored by camera trap and never met on a trail. Sumatran Serow works the higher slopes and Siamang calls from the forest below the tea.

Gunung Leuser and Aceh

The rainforest of northern Sumatra, and the only place on earth tigers, rhinos, elephants and orangutans still share a forest

Leuser is the great forest of Sumatra and most visitors come for the orangutans rather than the birds. Both are worth it. Bukit Lawang on the eastern edge is the busy end; Ketambe up the Alas valley in Aceh is a research station with far fewer people and better forest. The hornbill list here is the best in Indonesia, with Helmeted Hornbill still present in a forest where its casque has made it a target.

  • Look for: Helmeted Hornbill, Great Hornbill, Bushy-crested Hornbill, Sumatran Ground Cuckoo, Sumatran Leafbird, Fire-tufted Barbet, Sumatran Treepie, Blue-rumped Parrot
  • Also recorded in the parks here: Bar-bellied Cuckooshrike, Bat Hawk
  • National park: Gunung Leuser National Park
  • Stops:
    • , the village on the Bohorok river at the eastern edge of the park, three hours from Medan, and the busy end of Leuser
    • , the research station up the Alas valley in Aceh, with far fewer visitors and much better forest
    • , the airport city for northern Sumatra, and the road head for both ends of the park
  • Also watch for: Sumatran Orangutan is the reason most people come, and Ketambe is where the long-term studies were done. This is the only forest left where Sumatran Tiger, Sumatran Rhinoceros, Sumatran Elephant and orangutan all still occur together, and Thomas's Leaf Monkey and Siamang are met daily.

Tanjung Puting and southern Kalimantan

Peat swamp and blackwater river in Borneo's south, watched from a liveaboard klotok rather than a road

Tanjung Puting is done on a klotok, a wooden houseboat that motors up the Sekonyer river and ties up at night, and the birding is done from its roof. The forest is peat swamp and heath, which is a distinct list from the Bornean hill forests, and the river gives Storm's Stork and kingfishers at eye level. Camp Leakey at the top of the river is the orangutan station Birute Galdikas founded in 1971.

  • Look for: Storm's Stork, Lesser Adjutant, Stork-billed Kingfisher, Black-and-red Broadbill, Hook-billed Bulbul, Bornean Black Magpie, White-bellied Woodpecker, Wrinkled Hornbill
  • Stops:
    • , the river port where the klotoks tie up, twenty minutes from Pangkalan Bun airport
    • , the orangutan research station at the top of the Sekonyer, founded in 1971 and still working
  • Also watch for: Bornean Orangutan comes to the feeding platforms at Camp Leakey and two other stations up the river, and Proboscis Monkey lines the Sekonyer at dusk in numbers. Long-tailed Macaque, Silvered Leaf Monkey and, on a night boat, Sunda Clouded Leopard tracks on the bank.

Balikpapan, Sungai Wain and East Kalimantan

Lowland dipterocarp forest behind an oil city, and the accessible end of Indonesian Borneo

East Kalimantan is where Indonesian Borneo is easiest to reach, and Sungai Wain is a protected forest half an hour from Balikpapan airport with a research trail through it. The list is the Bornean lowland one that Sabah is famous for, without the price. Kutai national park, 180 km north up the coast, adds a larger block, and Wehea inland is a community-managed forest with a growing reputation. Both are a separate trip from Sungai Wain rather than a day out of it.

  • Look for: Bornean Bristlehead, Bornean Banded Pitta, Bornean Black Magpie, Blue-headed Pitta, Black-and-yellow Broadbill, Bornean Crested Fireback, White-crowned Hornbill, Bornean Barbet
  • Stops:
    • , the oil city with the airport, and the base for Sungai Wain half an hour away
    • , the provincial capital up the Mahakam, and the road north towards Kutai
  • Also watch for: Sungai Wain holds a reintroduced Bornean Orangutan population and a good density of Bornean Gibbon, and the proboscis monkeys of Balikpapan bay are watched from a boat out of the city. Sun Bear is present and there is a rehabilitation centre at Samboja nearby.

Frequently Asked Questions

When is the best time to go birding in the Greater Sundas?

June to September for Java, Bali and the southern Sumatran lowlands, when Gilimanuk falls to 33 mm and Way Kambas to 67 mm. Northern Sumatra runs on a different calendar and is driest in February, so a Gunung Leuser trip is better in January to March. The three seasons.

Is there anywhere here with a real dry season?

Bali's western tip and East Java, and nowhere else. Bogor in West Java takes 3,619 mm a year and never records under 201 mm in a month, which is what an equatorial climate actually looks like. The further east above, the more seasonal it becomes. When to go, season by season.

What is Wallace's Line and does it matter here?

It is the deep-water channel between Bali and Lombok and between Borneo and Sulawesi. Every region here sits on its Asian side, which is why the birds are hornbills, pittas, trogons and barbets rather than the cockatoos and honeyeaters of the other three Indonesian guides. The birds.

Can I see the Bali Starling in the wild?

Yes, in Bali Barat national park at the island's northwest tip, two hours from the airport. The species fell to a handful of wild birds in the 1990s and survives through releases, watched at feeding stations near Teluk Terima and on Nusa Penida. Bali Barat and the Bali Starling.

How hard is the Kerinci leg?

It is the hardest thing above. Kersik Tuo is a seven to nine hour drive from Padang, the birding is steep forest at 1,400 m and above, and Salvadori's Pheasant and Schneider's Pitta both take patience measured in mornings rather than hours. It is also where most of Sumatra's montane endemics are. Kerinci Seblat and the Sumatran highlands.

Is Kalimantan worth it if I have been to Sabah?

For the birds, the lists overlap heavily and Sabah's infrastructure is better. What Kalimantan adds is peat swamp and heath forest on the Sekonyer, which Sabah does not have, and Tanjung Puting's klotok trip is a different way of birding from a lodge. Tanjung Puting and southern Kalimantan.

Why does this guide carry no certification badges?

Because the certified properties in Indonesia are almost all Balinese resorts, and this guide reaches only the western tip of Bali. Every chip on a BeakBrain guide renders from a scheme's own register rather than from a property's claim. Certification.

How many bird species does Indonesia have?

More than any country except Colombia and Peru, and about a fifth of them live nowhere else. The card wall carries every Indonesian species with a BeakBrain card, which is coded at country level, so the same wall appears on all four Indonesian guides. The birds.
