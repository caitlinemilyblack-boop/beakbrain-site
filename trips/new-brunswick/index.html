Birding in New Brunswick

Semipalmated Sandpipers gather on the upper Fundy mudflats in numbers that Burrows put at half a million birds on one beach, and most of the world's population passes through in a few weeks of August.

Birds that decide the trip

Semipalmated Sandpiper on the Fundy flats

Almost the whole world population stops on these mudflats to double its weight, and at high tide the flock is pushed onto a handful of beaches.

When Late July to early September, around high tide.

Where Shepody Bay and the upper Fundy

Atlantic Puffin from the Machias Seal blinds

The blinds sit inside the colony, so the birds land a metre or two away and carry on. Landings are capped and permitted, and the fog decides.

When June and July, by permitted landing.

Where Grand Manan and the Fundy islands

Bicknell's Thrush on Mount Carleton

It breeds only in stunted summit spruce in the north-east, and Mount Carleton is the highest ground in the Maritimes and the province's reliable site.

When The first two weeks of June, at dawn or dusk.

Where Fundy National Park and the highlands

Piping Plover on the Acadian sand

The barrier beaches of the Northumberland Strait hold nesting pairs behind rope every summer, and the marked path is where you stand rather than the sand.

When May to August, on closed beach sections.

Where Kouchibouguac and the Acadian coast

Razorbill on the island ledges

It shares the Machias Seal ledges with the puffins and murres, and the blinds put all three within a couple of metres of the camera.

When June and July, from the colony blinds.

Where Grand Manan and the Fundy islands

Nelson's Sparrow in the salt marsh

A hissing song from thick salt marsh at dusk, and both the Tantramar and the strait lagoons hold breeding birds.

When June and July, in salt marsh at dusk.

Where The Tantramar and the Chignecto isthmus, Kouchibouguac and the Acadian coast

Leach's Storm Petrel off the Fundy islands

It comes to its burrows only after dark, so daylight means a boat over the tidal upwelling off the island banks.

When June to August, from a boat offshore.

Where Grand Manan and the Fundy islands

Snowy Owl on the Tantramar

The diked marsh holds wintering owls most years. They sit on dike tops and fence posts in open country, so a slow drive along the dike roads finds them.

When December to February, on the dikes.

Where The Tantramar and the Chignecto isthmus

When to Go

New Brunswick is a shorebird province before it is anything else. The upper Bay of Fundy holds the whole Semipalmated Sandpiper passage for a few weeks each August, and the island seabird colonies run through June and July on either side of it.

The warbler arrival

Late May and early June

Acadian forest fills with breeding warblers and the highlands open.

Go for Fundy National Park and Mount Carleton.

Trade off The shorebirds have not arrived and the island boats are only starting.

Typical daily average

The seabird season

June and July

Machias Seal Island holds puffins, razorbills and terns, and the blinds stand inside the colony.

Go for Grand Manan, with a landing permit booked well ahead.

Trade off Fog closes the crossing often, and the island takes a fixed number of visitors a day.

Typical daily average

The Fundy shorebird staging

Late July to early September

Semipalmated Sandpipers pack the upper bay mudflats and roost on the beaches at high tide.

Go for Mary's Point and Johnson's Mills, on the two hours either side of high tide.

Trade off The birds leave on one favourable wind, so a fixed date can miss the peak.

Typical daily average

The winter marsh

December to February

Snowy Owls and Rough-legged Buzzards hunt the Tantramar, which Burrows counted through the 1970s.

Go for Sackville, the Tantramar and Cape Jourimain.

Trade off Short days, hard cold and most of the coast frozen.

Shepody Bay and the upper Fundy

Mudflats that feed most of the world's Semipalmated Sandpipers in a few weeks each August

The upper Bay of Fundy has the largest tides on earth and mudflats rich in one small amphipod, and almost the entire Semipalmated Sandpiper population stops here to double its weight before flying to South America non-stop. Burrows recorded concentrations at Mary's Point of half a million birds.

  • Habitat: Tidal mudflat under the largest tides in the world, salt marsh, cobble roost beach, and mixed woodland on the shore behind.
  • When: Late July to early September, and only within two hours either side of high tide, when the birds are pushed off the flats onto the beaches.
  • Getting there: Moncton is the airport, an hour north. The interpretive centre at Mary's Point is staffed in summer, the beaches are roped at the roost, and walking onto them at high tide flushes the whole flock.
  • Base yourself in: Riverside-Albert
  • Look for: Semipalmated Sandpiper, Semipalmated Plover, Short-billed Dowitcher, Grey Plover, Red Knot, Hudsonian Whimbrel, Least Sandpiper, White-rumped Sandpiper, Peregrine Falcon, Bald Eagle, Nelson's Sparrow, Willet
  • Also covers: Hopewell Rocks, Johnson's Mills, Mary's Point, Riverside-Albert
  • Also watch for: The mudflats carry an amphipod called Corophium in densities of tens of thousands per square metre, which is the whole reason the sandpipers stop here.

Grand Manan and the Fundy islands

A seabird island where the viewing blinds stand inside the colony itself, and a permit gets you in

Machias Seal Island lies out in the Gulf of Maine and holds puffins, razorbills, murres and Arctic Terns, and the hides put a watcher a metre or two from the birds, which carry on regardless. Landings are limited to a fixed number of visitors a day and depend entirely on the sea. Grand Manan itself is the base.

  • Habitat: Bare rock island with turf and boulder, spruce forest and cliff on Grand Manan, weir-fished shallows, and deep tidal water offshore.
  • When: June and July, when the auks and terns are ashore. The blinds are closed outside the breeding season.
  • Getting there: A ferry runs to Grand Manan from Blacks Harbour, about ninety minutes. Machias Seal Island landings go from Seal Cove by licensed boat with a permit, book months ahead, and fog cancels sailings often.
  • Base yourself in: Grand Manan
  • Look for: Atlantic Puffin, Razorbill, Common Murre, Arctic Tern, Common Tern, Leach's Storm Petrel, Wilson's Storm Petrel, Great Shearwater, Sooty Shearwater, Red-necked Phalarope, Common Eider, Black Guillemot
  • Also covers: Campobello Island, Grand Manan, Machias Seal Island, White Head Island
  • Also watch for: The Fundy tides drive an upwelling off the island banks that draws right whales, finbacks and humpbacks into the same water as the shearwaters.

The Tantramar and the Chignecto isthmus

Diked marshland on the neck of land joining the province to Nova Scotia

The Tantramar is a wide diked marsh across the isthmus, drained by Acadian settlers and now a mix of impoundment, hayfield and salt marsh. It holds breeding waterfowl and rails in summer and a raptor concentration in winter, which the Christmas counts Burrows collated recorded through the 1970s.

  • Habitat: Diked freshwater impoundment and hayfield, salt marsh outside the dikes, cattail and bulrush, and sand shore at the strait.
  • When: May to July for the breeding marsh birds, and December to February for the wintering owls and raptors.
  • Getting there: Moncton is the airport, forty minutes west. The Sackville Waterfowl Park has boardwalks in the town itself, and Cape Jourimain has a centre at the bridge approach.
  • Base yourself in: Sackville
  • Look for: American Bittern, Least Bittern, Virginia Rail, Sora, Pied-billed Grebe, Northern Harrier, Snowy Owl, Rough-legged Buzzard, Short-eared Owl, Nelson's Sparrow, Bobolink, Wood Duck
  • Also covers: Cape Jourimain, Cape Tormentine, Sackville, Tintamarre National Wildlife Area
  • Also watch for: The marsh was diked and drained by Acadian settlers in the seventeenth century, and the dike system still sets where the water sits.

Fundy National Park and the highlands

Acadian forest on a coastal plateau, with the province's only high ground behind it

Fundy National Park runs from the shore up onto a forested plateau cut by steep valleys, and holds the warblers and thrushes of the Acadian forest through June. Mount Carleton in the north is the highest ground in the Maritimes, and its stunted summit spruce holds Bicknell's Thrush.

  • Habitat: Acadian mixed forest of spruce, fir, maple and birch, steep river gorge, stunted summit spruce on the highlands, and bog.
  • When: Late May to early July for the singing warblers, with the highland thrush best in the first two weeks of June at dawn or dusk.
  • Getting there: Moncton is the airport for Fundy, ninety minutes east, and Mount Carleton is remote with a gravel access road. Both parks charge a day fee and Fundy has serviced campgrounds.
  • Base yourself in: Alma
  • Look for: Bicknell's Thrush, Blackburnian Warbler, Bay-breasted Warbler, Cape May Warbler, Canada Warbler, Spruce Grouse, Black-backed Woodpecker, Boreal Chickadee, Canada Jay, Barred Owl, Ruffed Grouse, Common Loon
  • Also covers: Alma, Caledonia Gorge, Fundy National Park, Mount Carleton Provincial Park
  • Also watch for: Moose and black bear are common through both parks, and the Fundy fog holds a coastal forest of unusual moss and lichen growth.

Kouchibouguac and the Acadian coast

Barrier islands and lagoons on a warm strait, with terns and plovers nesting on the sand

The Northumberland Strait coast is a low sand shore of barrier islands, dunes and lagoons, warmer than the Fundy side and quite different in its birds. Kouchibouguac holds one of the larger Common Tern colonies in the region and nesting Piping Plovers, and Miscou at the tip of the Acadian Peninsula is a migration point.

  • Habitat: Barrier island and dune, salt lagoon and eelgrass bed, salt marsh, bog and jack pine behind, and warm shallow strait water.
  • When: June and July for the nesting terns and plovers, and late August for the shorebird passage through the lagoons.
  • Getting there: Moncton is the airport, an hour south of the park. Kouchibouguac charges a day fee and runs a boat to the tern colony, and the nesting beaches are closed to walking through the summer.
  • Base yourself in: Kouchibouguac
  • Look for: Piping Plover, Common Tern, Arctic Tern, Roseate Tern, Willet, Nelson's Sparrow, Great Blue Heron, Osprey, Bald Eagle, Black Guillemot, Red-throated Loon, Palm Warbler
  • Also covers: Kouchibouguac National Park, Miscou Island, Neguac, Tabusintac
  • Also watch for: Grey seals haul out on the barrier island sandbars in numbers through the summer, and the lagoons hold the northern edge of several warm-water shellfish.

Frequently Asked Questions

When is the best time to go birding in New Brunswick?

August, if the shorebirds are the point. Most of the world's Semipalmated Sandpipers stop on the upper Fundy mudflats for a few weeks to feed before flying to South America. June and July are the other window, for the island seabird colonies.

How do I see the sandpiper roost properly?

Work the tide, not the clock. The birds feed on the mudflats and are pushed onto a handful of beaches for about two hours either side of high tide, which is when the flock is visible as one mass. Mary's Point and Johnson's Mills are the two places to stand, and the roost beaches are roped.

Can I land on Machias Seal Island?

Yes, with a permit and a licensed boat from Seal Cove on Grand Manan. Numbers are capped each day, bookings open months ahead, and fog cancels the crossing often enough that four days on the island gives a better chance than one.

Where do I look for Bicknell's Thrush?

Mount Carleton, in the stunted spruce near the summit, in the first two weeks of June at dawn or dusk. It is the highest ground in the Maritimes and the one dependable site in the province.

What is there in winter?

The Tantramar marsh. Snowy Owls, Rough-legged Buzzards and Northern Harriers hunt the diked fields, all visible from the road, and the Christmas counts on the isthmus have recorded that raptor concentration since the 1970s.

Can I bird here with limited mobility?

Sites on this page differ from each other more than any rating could carry, so this is the framework the standard British guides use, and the reserve's own page and warden hold the answers.

  • Permission · some reserves open ground to disabled visitors that is closed to everyone else, and it is granted by asking.
  • Priority · parking bays, hides and the nearest paths are often held for disabled visitors. Ask what is reserved and how to claim it.
  • Parking · how close a car reaches the birds shapes the whole day. Ring ahead for the nearest hard standing to the water or the hide.
  • Paths · surfaces, gradients and gate widths vary inside a single reserve. Ask which trail is boarded, surfaced or level.
  • Perches · a hide with a low window, or a bench where the view opens, turns a viewpoint into a morning.

Every reserve above with its own link carries its access detail there, and a warden answers what a page cannot.
