Birding in Tennessee

Tennessee holds the largest spruce-fir forest left in the southern Appalachians and a lake made by an earthquake with drowned cypress still standing in it, four hundred miles apart.

Birds that decide the trip

Black-capped Chickadee on Clingmans Dome

The spruce-fir at the top of the Smokies is an island of northern forest, and it carries this chickadee while Carolina Chickadee holds everything below.

When Late May to July, above five thousand feet.

Where The Great Smoky Mountains

Sandhill Crane on the Duck River

The eastern population barely used this state thirty years ago and now winters here in tens of thousands, with a purpose-built viewing platform looking over the roost.

When December to February, from the viewing platform.

Where The central basin and the barrens

Bald Eagle over Reelfoot

The drowned cypress standing in the lake gives them perches over open water, and the winter concentration here is one of the larger inland gatherings in the country.

When December to February, on the standing cypress.

Where Reelfoot Lake

Cerulean Warbler on the plateau rim

It sings from the tops of tall hardwoods on the gorge sides, and the Cumberland Plateau rim roads bring that canopy level with the road.

When April to July, from rim roads above the gorges.

Where The Cumberland Plateau

Swainson's Warbler in the rhododendron

It keeps to dense rhododendron and cane on wet ground and is heard far more than seen, and the plateau gorges hold it alongside birds that belong much further north.

When May and June, from thick cover at dawn.

Where The Cumberland Plateau

Buff-breasted Sandpiper on the drawdown

It prefers short dry ground rather than mud, so the drying edges of the Tennessee River refuge units are among the better inland places to find one in autumn.

When Late August to September, on dry margins.

Where The Tennessee River refuges

Red Crossbill in the Smokies fir

It breeds whenever the cones are right rather than to a calendar, and the southern Appalachian spruce-fir holds a resident population with its own call type.

When All year, in the high spruce and fir.

Where The Great Smoky Mountains

Prothonotary Warbler in the drowned cypress

It nests in holes over standing water, and a lake full of dead cypress trunks is about as much of that habitat as a bird could ask for.

When April to July, over standing water.

Where Reelfoot Lake, The Tennessee River refuges

When to Go

Tennessee is long and narrow, and its two ends run on different calendars. The mountains in the east are a June proposition, and the west is a winter one, with the river refuges in between working best on the autumn drawdown.

The mountain breeding season

Late May to July

The spruce-fir birds are singing above five thousand feet and the plateau warblers are in.

Go for Clingmans Dome and the high Smokies trails, and the Cumberland gorges.

Trade off The west is hot and quiet and the park roads fill by mid-morning.

Typical daily average

The autumn drawdown

August to October

The Tennessee River refuges expose mudflats and take the southbound waders.

Go for The Tennessee and Cross Creeks refuges on the drawdown.

Trade off The mudflats depend on reservoir management, so check before travelling.

Typical daily average

The crane and eagle winter

December to February

The Duck River unit holds tens of thousands of Sandhill Cranes and Reelfoot holds the eagles.

Go for The Duck River crane platform, and Reelfoot on a guided eagle tour.

Trade off The mountains are shut above the passes and the warblers are gone.

Typical daily average

The spring passage

April and May

Warblers move up the plateau gorges and the swamp breeders arrive at Reelfoot.

Go for The Cumberland gorges and Reelfoot boardwalks.

Trade off The high Smokies are still cold and the cranes have gone north.

8 days

Mountains to the western lakes

Knoxville (TYS), Gatlinburg 2 nights, Crossville 2 nights, Nashville 1 night, Paris 1 night, Tiptonville 2 nights, out of Memphis (MEM)

An open-jaw run west in early June that crosses the whole state. Spruce-fir on Clingmans Dome first, then the sandstone gorges of the plateau, the central basin glades, and a finish in the drowned cypress at Reelfoot. About 550 miles.

  1. 1Great Smoky Mountains National Park
  2. 2Clingmans Dome
  3. 3Cades Cove

5 days

The high Smokies

Knoxville (TYS), Gatlinburg 4 nights, back to Knoxville

Four days in the second half of June among the highest spruce and fir in the southern Appalachians. Be at Clingmans Dome before the car park fills, walk the ridge for Black-capped Chickadee and Red Crossbill, and take Cades Cove at dawn for the valley birds and the bears.

  1. 1Reelfoot Lake
  2. 2Lake Isom National Wildlife Refuge
  3. 3Black Bayou

4 days

Reelfoot in winter

Memphis (MEM), Tiptonville 3 nights, back to Memphis

Three January days on a lake that an earthquake made. The state park runs guided eagle tours, the drowned cypress makes the perches, and the geese come off the refuge fields in the morning. Boat access opens up the rest of the lake.

  1. 1Duck River Unit
  2. 2Radnor Lake State Natural Area
  3. 3Arnold Air Force Base barrens

4 days

The Duck River cranes

Nashville (BNA) 3 nights, back to Nashville

Three days in January for a crane flock that has grown from almost nothing in thirty years to tens of thousands. The viewing platform at the Duck River unit is the place to stand, and Radnor Lake inside the city fills the rest of each day.

The Great Smoky Mountains

Spruce and fir above six thousand feet, and a road that reaches it

The Smokies hold the largest area of spruce-fir forest left in the southern Appalachians, and Clingmans Dome road climbs almost to the top of it. Walking up from the valley passes through the equivalent of a thousand miles of latitude, and the breeding list changes with it.

  • Habitat: Red spruce and Fraser fir above five thousand feet, northern hardwood below, cove forest of tulip poplar and hemlock in the hollows, heath bald on exposed ridges, and open pasture at Cades Cove.
  • When: Late May to July for the high-elevation breeders. The park is busiest in October and the Clingmans Dome road closes from December to March.
  • Getting there: Knoxville is the airport, an hour east, and Gatlinburg the base. The park charges a parking fee, Cades Cove has a one-way loop that fills by mid-morning, and the Clingmans Dome road is closed through winter.
  • Base yourself in: Gatlinburg
  • Look for: Black-capped Chickadee, Red Crossbill, Golden-crowned Kinglet, Winter Wren, Blackburnian Warbler, Canada Warbler, Chestnut-sided Warbler, Black-throated Blue Warbler, Veery, Northern Saw-whet Owl, Ruffed Grouse, Northern Raven
  • Also covers: Cades Cove, Clingmans Dome, Great Smoky Mountains National Park
  • Also watch for: Black bear are at one of the highest densities in the country, elk were returned to Cataloochee, and the park holds more salamander species than anywhere else on earth.

The Cumberland Plateau

Sandstone gorges cut two hundred metres into a flat-topped plateau

The Cumberland Plateau is a sandstone table with rivers cut deep into it, so the gorge walls hold hemlock and cool damp forest while the plateau surface above is dry oak and pine. Cerulean Warbler and Swainson's Warbler both breed on the same plateau in quite different places.

  • Habitat: Sandstone gorge with hemlock and rhododendron, dry oak and pine on the plateau top, cove hardwood in the coves, and old field and reclaimed mine land on the margins.
  • When: Late April to June for the breeding birds. The gorge trails are steep and the rock is slippery when wet.
  • Getting there: Knoxville or Nashville are the airports and Crossville the base. The national forest and the river area are free with gravel access roads, and Savage Gulf has marked rim and gorge trails.
  • Base yourself in: Crossville
  • Look for: Cerulean Warbler, Swainson's Warbler, Worm-eating Warbler, Kentucky Warbler, Hooded Warbler, Louisiana Waterthrush, Acadian Flycatcher, Wood Thrush, Scarlet Tanager, Yellow-breasted Chat, Ruffed Grouse, Barred Owl
  • Also covers: Big South Fork National River and Recreation Area, Cherokee National Forest, Savage Gulf State Natural Area
  • Also watch for: The gorges hold black bear and one of the larger cave systems in the country, and elk were reintroduced on the northern plateau.

Reelfoot Lake

A lake made by an earthquake, with drowned cypress standing in it and eagles above

Reelfoot in Tennessee was formed when the New Madrid earthquakes of 1811 and 1812 dropped the land and the Mississippi ran backwards into the hollow. The drowned cypress is still standing, and the lake holds one of the larger inland wintering Bald Eagle concentrations in the country.

  • Habitat: Shallow earthquake lake with standing drowned cypress, cypress and tupelo swamp margins, bottomland hardwood, and farmed refuge fields.
  • When: December to February for the eagles and geese, and April to June for the swamp breeders.
  • Getting there: Memphis is the airport, two hours south, and Tiptonville the base. The state park runs guided eagle tours in winter, boat access is the only way into much of the lake, and the boardwalks are free.
  • Base yourself in: Tiptonville
  • Look for: Bald Eagle, Snow Goose, Greater White-fronted Goose, American White Pelican, Anhinga, Wood Duck, Prothonotary Warbler, Least Bittern, Purple Gallinule, Mississippi Kite, Barred Owl, Yellow-crowned Night Heron
  • Also covers: Black Bayou, Lake Isom National Wildlife Refuge
  • Also watch for: The lake holds alligator gar and one of the more visible river otter populations in the state.

The Tennessee River refuges

Dammed river running as a chain of lakes, with refuges on the shallow bays

The Tennessee River was turned into a chain of reservoirs, and the refuges on its shallow embayments now take the wintering waterfowl and the autumn shorebirds. Drawdowns on the mudflats produce shorebird numbers a landlocked state should not have.

  • Habitat: Shallow reservoir embayment with mudflat when drawn down, moist-soil impoundment, bottomland hardwood, and farmed refuge fields.
  • When: August to October for the shorebirds on the drawdown, and November to February for the waterfowl and eagles.
  • Getting there: Nashville is the airport, two hours east, and Paris the base. Both refuges have auto tours and free access, and the mudflats depend on the reservoir drawdown, which is worth checking before travelling.
  • Base yourself in: Paris
  • Look for: Snow Goose, Greater White-fronted Goose, Sandhill Crane, American White Pelican, Bald Eagle, Stilt Sandpiper, Pectoral Sandpiper, Buff-breasted Sandpiper, American Golden Plover, Least Tern, Prothonotary Warbler, Wood Duck
  • Also covers: Cross Creeks National Wildlife Refuge, Kentucky Lake, Tennessee National Wildlife Refuge
  • Also watch for: The refuges hold a large white-tailed deer population and the river carries one of the richest freshwater mussel faunas in the country.

The central basin and the barrens

Limestone cedar glades and a crane flock that grew from nothing in thirty years

The central basin is limestone with thin soil, which produces cedar glades that carry plants found nowhere else. The Duck River unit on Old Hickory Lake has become the main eastern Sandhill Crane wintering ground, from almost nothing thirty years ago, and it now holds tens of thousands.

  • Habitat: Limestone cedar glade with thin soil, oak and hickory on the deeper ground, reservoir embayment and mudflat, and open barrens managed with fire on the air base.
  • When: December to February for the cranes, and April to June for the glade and barrens breeders.
  • Getting there: Nashville is the airport and the base. Radnor Lake is free with a walking-only road, the Duck River unit has a crane viewing platform, and the air base barrens need arranged access.
  • Base yourself in: Nashville
  • Look for: Sandhill Crane, Whooping Crane, Bald Eagle, Prairie Warbler, Blue-winged Warbler, Yellow-breasted Chat, Field Sparrow, Northern Bobwhite, Eastern Whip-poor-will, Barred Owl, Pileated Woodpecker, Wood Duck
  • Also covers: Arnold Air Force Base barrens, Duck River Unit, Radnor Lake State Natural Area
  • Also watch for: The cedar glades hold plants that exist on almost no other rock in the world, and Radnor Lake has a well-watched otter and bobcat population.

Frequently Asked Questions

When is the best time to go birding in Tennessee?

The second half of June for the mountains, when the spruce-fir birds are singing on Clingmans Dome and the plateau warblers are in. January is the other answer, for the Duck River cranes and the Reelfoot eagles at the western end of the state.

How do I get to the high spruce-fir?

The Clingmans Dome road, which climbs almost to the summit and closes from December to March. Arrive before the car park fills, then walk the ridge trail: the chickadees, crossbills and Winter Wrens are all within a mile of the top.

What happened at Reelfoot Lake?

The New Madrid earthquakes of 1811 and 1812 dropped the land and the Mississippi ran backwards into the hollow, filling it. The Tennessee cypress that drowned is still standing, which gives the wintering eagles their perches and the Prothonotary Warblers their nest holes.

Where do I see the cranes?

The Duck River unit on Old Hickory Lake, from the viewing platform, December to February. The eastern flock barely used this state thirty years ago and now winters here in tens of thousands, which is one of the faster changes in North American bird distribution.

Will the river mudflats be exposed?

It depends on the reservoir drawdown, which is a management decision. When the levels drop in late summer the Tennessee River refuges produce shorebird numbers a landlocked state has no business having; when they hold, the same units are open water.

Can I bird here with limited mobility?

Sites on this page differ from each other more than any rating could carry, so this is the framework the standard British guides use, and the reserve's own page and warden hold the answers.

  • Permission · some reserves open ground to disabled visitors that is closed to everyone else, and it is granted by asking.
  • Priority · parking bays, hides and the nearest paths are often held for disabled visitors. Ask what is reserved and how to claim it.
  • Parking · how close a car reaches the birds shapes the whole day. Ring ahead for the nearest hard standing to the water or the hide.
  • Paths · surfaces, gradients and gate widths vary inside a single reserve. Ask which trail is boarded, surfaced or level.
  • Perches · a hide with a low window, or a bench where the view opens, turns a viewpoint into a morning.
