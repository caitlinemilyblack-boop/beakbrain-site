Atlas / Illinois

Birding in Illinois

Illinois holds close to a fifth of the world's Canvasbacks on one river, half a million waterfowl on another, and a few acres of city scrub on Lake Michigan that produce a whole spring migration in May.

At a glance

Chicago (ORD or MDW) for the lakefront and the northern prairie, St. Louis for the whole southern half, and Peoria for the Illinois River
Getting around
A hire car, except on the Chicago lakefront where public transport reaches every site. Chicago to Cairo is 380 miles

Birds that decide the trip

Canvasback on the upper Mississippi

The refuge units take more than 135,000 of them on passage, which is close to a fifth of the world population resting on one river at once.

When November to December, on the open pools above the locks.

Where The upper Mississippi and the prairie

Henslow's Sparrow at Goose Lake Prairie

As many as sixty breeding pairs hold this one prairie remnant, which makes it one of the strongest populations anywhere for a bird in long decline. The song is two syllables and easy to walk past.

When May to July, from tall grass on still mornings.

Where The upper Mississippi and the prairie

Prothonotary Warbler in the Cache cypress

It nests in holes over standing water and is the bird the southern swamp is really for, gold against grey bark on the boardwalk at Heron Pond.

When Late April to July, over standing water.

Where Cache River and the southern swamps

Buff-breasted Sandpiper on the drawdown

It prefers short dry ground rather than mud, which makes the drying edges of the Illinois River impoundments one of the better inland places to find one in autumn.

When Late August to September, on dry margins.

Where The Illinois River valley

Connecticut Warbler at Montrose Point

A skulking bird that most people miss everywhere else turns up on the lakefront most springs, because a migrant crossing the lake takes whatever cover it first reaches.

When The middle two weeks of May, in low cover at dawn.

Where The Chicago lakefront

Snow Goose at the confluence

Twenty thousand at Union County alone sit among more than 300,000 Canada Geese, which is the largest goose concentration in Illinois.

When December to February, on the refuge fields.

Where The confluence at Horseshoe Lake

Mississippi Kite over the bottomland

It hunts dragonflies on the wing over the river bottoms in the far south of the state, and takes them in its feet without stopping.

When May to August, over open bottomland on warm afternoons.

Where Cache River and the southern swamps, The confluence at Horseshoe Lake

Bald Eagle below the locks

Hundreds winter along the Mississippi wherever the dams keep the water open, and the observation decks at the locks put them within scope range on the ice edge.

When December to February, at the lock and dam decks.

Where The upper Mississippi and the prairie

When to Go

Illinois is a flyway state, so its calendar is about water levels and passage rather than about breeding. The one exception is the far south, where a southern swamp and an unglaciated range of hills hold birds that breed nowhere else in it.

The spring passage

Late April to early June

The lakefront traps fill with warblers and the southern swamp breeders arrive.

Go for Montrose Point at dawn in May, and the Cache River cypress for Prothonotary and Cerulean.

Trade off The waterfowl have gone north and the shorebird mudflats are under water.

Typical daily average

The shorebird drawdown

Late July to October

Chautauqua drops its impoundments to mud and takes 150,000 shorebirds.

Go for Chautauqua and Banner Marsh, then Rend Lake and the river flats.

Trade off The mudflats depend on the drawdown agreeing with the river, so check before travelling.

Typical daily average

The goose winter

November to February

More than 300,000 geese hold the confluence and hundreds of eagles work the locks.

Go for Horseshoe Lake and Union County, then the Great River Road for eagles.

Trade off The lakefront is down to gulls and the southern warblers are in Central America.

Typical daily average

The prairie breeding season

May to July

Goose Lake Prairie holds up to sixty pairs of Henslow's Sparrow with Sedge Wren and King Rail.

Go for Goose Lake Prairie and the grassland units on the upper river.

Trade off It is hot, humid and buggy, and the water birds have dispersed.

5 days

Geese, eagles and the river

St. Louis (STL), Alton 2 nights, Anna 2 nights, back to St. Louis

A January circuit of the two rivers. Hundreds of Bald Eagles below the locks on the Great River Road, then south to the confluence where more than 300,000 geese winter. Cold, open and easy driving, with a scope needed at every stop.

Cache River and the southern swamps

Bald cypress and tupelo standing in water at the northern edge of a southern swamp

The Cache is an abandoned channel of the Ohio, and the cypress-tupelo swamp along it is the furthest north that habitat reaches. A quarter of a million waterfowl winter in southern Illinois around it, four fifths of them Canada Geese, and the breeding warbler list is a southern one.

  • Habitat: Bald cypress and tupelo swamp, bottomland hardwood, slough and oxbow, with upland oak and hickory on the low ridges.
  • When: April to June for the breeding warblers, and December to February for the wintering geese.
  • Getting there: St. Louis or Nashville in Tennessee are the airports and Vienna the base. Heron Pond has a floating boardwalk out into the cypress, and much of the rest is best seen by canoe.
  • Base yourself in: Vienna
  • Look for: Prothonotary Warbler, Yellow-throated Warbler, Cerulean Warbler, Kentucky Warbler, Northern Parula, Barred Owl, Anhinga, Mississippi Kite, Wood Duck, Canada Goose, Least Bittern, King Rail
  • Parks and reserves: Cache River State Natural Area (Protected area), Shawnee National Forest (Forest or grassland), Heron Pond – Little Black Slough Nature Preserve, Mermet Lake State Fish and Wildlife Area (Protected area), Section Eight Woods Nature Preserve
  • Also covers: Cache River State Natural Area, Cypress Creek National Wildlife Refuge, Grassy Slough Preserve, Heron Pond – Little Black Slough Nature Preserve, Limekiln Springs Preserve, Mermet Lake State Fish and Wildlife Area, Section Eight Woods Nature Preserve, Shawnee National Forest
  • Also watch for: The swamp holds cottonmouth and a cypress at Heron Pond that is well over a thousand years old.

The Shawnee hills and Crab Orchard

Sandstone bluffs and oak forest carrying birds that are rare everywhere else in the state

The Shawnee is the only unglaciated corner of the state, so it has hills, sandstone escarpments and forest that the rest of the prairie belt has none of. Cerulean, Kentucky, Prothonotary and Worm-eating Warblers all breed there, and Crab Orchard beside it takes 240,000 waterfowl.

  • Habitat: Oak and hickory forest on sandstone hills, bluff and rock shelter, bottomland along the creeks, and open water with farmed impoundments at Crab Orchard.
  • When: April to June for the breeding birds, and November to February for the waterfowl and eagles.
  • Getting there: St. Louis is the airport, two hours north-west, and Carbondale the base. Garden of the Gods and the other Shawnee escarpment sites have short trails from small car parks, and part of Crab Orchard is closed as a wilderness.
  • Base yourself in: Carbondale
  • Look for: Cerulean Warbler, Kentucky Warbler, Worm-eating Warbler, Prothonotary Warbler, Chuck-will's-widow, Short-eared Owl, Bald Eagle, Canada Goose, Snow Goose, Greater White-fronted Goose, Wood Duck, Barred Owl
  • Parks and reserves: Shawnee National Forest (Forest or grassland), Crab Orchard National Wildlife Refuge, Trail of Tears State Forest (Forest or grassland), Bald Knob Wilderness (Protected area)
  • Also covers: Bald Knob Wilderness, Crab Orchard National Wildlife Refuge, Shawnee National Forest, Trail of Tears State Forest
  • Also watch for: Bobcat have recovered through the forest, and the sandstone shelters hold a large bat population.

The confluence at Horseshoe Lake

Three hundred thousand geese at the point where the Ohio meets the Mississippi

The bottom tip of Illinois is where the two great rivers meet, and the wintering goose concentration there runs to more than 300,000 birds, mostly Canada Geese, with 20,000 Snow Geese and 50,000 ducks at Union County alone. Up to a thousand American White Pelicans pass through in spring.

  • Habitat: Shallow cypress-ringed oxbow lake, farmed goose fields, river bottomland and sandbar along the Mississippi.
  • When: November to February for the goose concentration, and April and May for the pelicans and the passerine passage.
  • Getting there: St. Louis is the airport and Anna the base. Horseshoe Lake has a causeway across it and a refuge road, and the river refuge units are reached down levee roads that flood.
  • Base yourself in: Anna
  • Look for: Canada Goose, Snow Goose, Greater White-fronted Goose, American White Pelican, Bald Eagle, Mississippi Kite, Prothonotary Warbler, Piping Plover, Pectoral Sandpiper, Stilt Sandpiper, American Golden Plover, Little Gull
  • Parks and reserves: Horseshoe Lake Conservation Area (Protected area), Union County State Fish and Wildlife Area (Protected area), Shawnee National Forest (Forest or grassland), Bald Knob Wilderness (Protected area), Camp Hutchins (Protected area)
  • Also covers: Bald Knob Wilderness, Camp Hutchins, Horseshoe Lake Conservation Area, Middle Mississippi River National Wildlife Refuge, Shawnee National Forest, Union County Conservation Area, Union County State Fish and Wildlife Area
  • Also watch for: The cypress at Horseshoe Lake grows straight out of open water and the lake holds alligator gar.

The Illinois River valley

Half a million waterfowl and 150,000 autumn shorebirds on one river valley

Chautauqua alone supports up to 500,000 waterfowl of 24 species and 150,000 shorebirds on the autumn passage, because the refuge can draw its impoundments down to mud at exactly the right time. Banner Marsh and Marshall add another 220,000 birds up and down the same Illinois River valley.

  • Habitat: Shallow backwater lake and moist-soil impoundment with exposed mudflat, bottomland forest, restored floodplain wetland and farmed levee ground.
  • When: Late July to October for the shorebird passage as the water drops, and November to February for the waterfowl.
  • Getting there: Peoria is the nearest airport and Havana the base. Chautauqua has an observation deck above the lake, and the mudflats only appear when the drawdown and the river level agree, so check before travelling.
  • Base yourself in: Havana
  • Look for: Canvasback, Lesser Scaup, Mallard, Northern Pintail, Blue-winged Teal, American White Pelican, Buff-breasted Sandpiper, Hudsonian Godwit, Stilt Sandpiper, Pectoral Sandpiper, Wilson's Phalarope, Piping Plover
  • Parks and reserves: Chautauqua National Wildlife Refuge, Marshall State Fish and Wildlife Area (Protected area), Chautauqua National Wildlife Refuge - Cameron Unit, Chautauqua National Wildlife Refuge - Billsbach Unit, Sand Ridge State Forest (Forest or grassland)
  • Also covers: Banner Marsh and Rice Lake State Fish and Wildlife Areas, Chautauqua National Wildlife Refuge - Billsbach Unit, Chautauqua National Wildlife Refuge - Cameron Unit, Marshall State Fish and Wildlife Area, Sand Ridge State Forest
  • Also watch for: The valley holds one of the larger river otter populations in the state and thousands of postbreeding herons.

The upper Mississippi and the prairie

Canvasbacks in world-significant numbers on one river, and the last prairie inland from it

The refuge units run along 260 miles of river and take more than 500,000 waterfowl. Over 135,000 of them are Canvasback, close to a fifth of the world population. Goose Lake Prairie inland holds as many as sixty breeding pairs of Henslow's Sparrow, one of the few strong populations anywhere.

  • Habitat: Wide river with backwater and wing dam, bottomland forest and island, limestone bluff, and tallgrass prairie with sedge meadow inland.
  • When: December to February for the eagles and diving ducks, and May to July for the prairie breeders.
  • Getting there: St. Louis is the airport for the southern units and Chicago for the prairie. The Great River Road runs the bluffs with frequent pull-ins, and the lock and dam observation decks are the classic eagle viewpoints.
  • Base yourself in: Alton
  • Look for: Canvasback, Lesser Scaup, Bald Eagle, Snow Goose, American White Pelican, Henslow's Sparrow, Sedge Wren, King Rail, Bobolink, Dickcissel, Upland Sandpiper, Short-eared Owl
  • Parks and reserves: Goose Lake Prairie State Natural Area (Protected area), Two Rivers National Wildlife Refuge, Upper Mississippi River National Wildlife and Fish Refuge (Protected area), Midewin National Tallgrass Prairie (Protected area), Pere Marquette State Park (State or provincial park)
  • Also covers: Goose Lake Prairie State Natural Area, LaSalle Lake State Fish and Wildlife Area, Midewin National Tallgrass Prairie, Mississippi River State Fish and Wildlife Area, Pere Marquette State Park, Two Rivers National Wildlife Refuge, Upper Mississippi National Wildlife Refuge and Army Corps of Engineers Lands, Upper Mississippi River National Wildlife and Fish Refuge
  • Also watch for: Hundreds of Bald Eagles winter below the locks, and the prairie holds badger and Blanding's turtle.

The Chicago lakefront

A few acres of scrub on a lake shore, taking a whole migration into the middle of a city

Migrants crossing Lake Michigan come down on the first cover they meet, and on the Chicago shore that cover is a handful of small parks. Montrose Point is the best known of them: a hedge and a patch of scrub that produce lists in May which would be respectable for a county.

  • Habitat: Small lakeside scrub and hedge plantings, mown park grass, beach and dune at the northern end, and the open water of the lake.
  • When: Early May to early June and late August to October for the passage, and December to February for gulls and Snowy Owl.
  • Getting there: O'Hare or Midway are the airports and the lakefront sites are reachable by public transport. Montrose is a public park with free parking that fills at dawn in May, and Illinois Beach is an hour north.
  • Base yourself in: Chicago
  • Look for: Golden-winged Warbler, Connecticut Warbler, Philadelphia Vireo, Olive-sided Flycatcher, Bay-breasted Warbler, Blackburnian Warbler, Black-throated Green Warbler, Snowy Owl, Long-eared Owl, Northern Saw-whet Owl, Franklin's Gull, Lapland Longspur
  • Parks and reserves: Adeline Jay Geo-Karis Illinois Beach State Park (State or provincial park), Montrose Point Bird Sanctuary (Reserve), Emmett Till and Mamie Till-Mobley National Monument, Sedge Meadow Forest Preserve, Wadsworth Savanna Forest Preserve
  • Also covers: Adeline Jay Geo-Karis Illinois Beach State Park, Emmett Till and Mamie Till-Mobley National Monument, Jackson Park, Montrose Point Bird Sanctuary, Sedge Meadow Forest Preserve, Wadsworth Savanna Forest Preserve
  • Also watch for: Coyote are established across the city parks and the lakefront holds a large winter gull roost.

Frequently Asked Questions

When is the best time to go birding in Illinois?

The second week of May, if one visit has to do the most: the Chicago lakefront is at its peak and the southern swamp warblers are all in. Late August to September for the Illinois River shorebird drawdown, and January for the geese and eagles.

Is Montrose Point really worth a special trip?

In May, yes. It is a few acres of hedge, scrub and mown grass on the lake shore, and migrants coming off the water drop into the first cover they meet. Day lists there in the second week of May run to numbers most counties would be pleased with.

Will the shorebird mudflats be there when I visit?

Only if the drawdown and the river level agree. Chautauqua can expose enormous flats and hold 150,000 shorebirds, or it can be under water in the same week of a different year. Check recent reports before committing to the drive.

Where do I see southern swamp birds this far north?

The Cache River in the far south, where an abandoned Ohio River channel carries bald cypress and tupelo standing in water. Heron Pond has a floating boardwalk into it, and the rest is best seen by canoe.

Do I need a car?

For everything except the Chicago lakefront, yes. Montrose, Jackson Park and even Illinois Beach are reachable on public transport, but the river refuges and the southern swamps are hours apart on rural roads.

Can I bird here with limited mobility?

Sites on this page differ from each other more than any rating could carry, so this is the framework the standard British guides use, and the reserve's own page and warden hold the answers.

  • Permission · some reserves open ground to disabled visitors that is closed to everyone else, and it is granted by asking.
  • Priority · parking bays, hides and the nearest paths are often held for disabled visitors. Ask what is reserved and how to claim it.
  • Parking · how close a car reaches the birds shapes the whole day. Ring ahead for the nearest hard standing to the water or the hide.
  • Paths · surfaces, gradients and gate widths vary inside a single reserve. Ask which trail is boarded, surfaced or level.
  • Perches · a hide with a low window, or a bench where the view opens, turns a viewpoint into a morning.

Every reserve above with its own link carries its access detail there, and a warden answers what a page cannot.
