Birding in New York

New York holds a hundred thousand gulls on one winter river, a tern colony of nine thousand pairs on a single island, and six million acres of boreal forest, all within four hours of the same city.

Birds that decide the trip

Roseate Tern on Great Gull Island

The island holds 1,500 pairs against a world population of about 40,000, alongside 7,700 pairs of Common Tern. It is a research station, so the birds are watched from the water rather than the shore.

When Late May to August, from a boat in the sound.

Where Long Island and Jamaica Bay

Piping Plover on the ocean beaches

The 1999 survey found 247 pairs across 64 Long Island beaches, with 69 of them on Westhampton Island alone. They nest above the tideline on open sand, which is exactly where everyone else wants to be.

When April to August, on fenced beach sections.

Where Long Island and Jamaica Bay

Broad-winged Hawk over Derby Hill

Northbound raptors will not cross the open lake, so they run east along the shore until the land turns, and Derby Hill sits on that corner. The Broad-winged peak in the third week of April can be thousands in a morning.

When The third week of April, best on a south wind.

Where The Lake Ontario shore and the hawk watches

Little Gull on the Niagara

The smallest gull in the world turns up among the Bonaparte's flocks below the falls most winters, and the corridor is the most reliable place in the country to look for one.

When Late November to early January, in the Bonaparte's flocks.

Where The Niagara River corridor

Bicknell's Thrush on Whiteface

It breeds in stunted fir near the summits of a few north-eastern mountains, and Whiteface has a toll road that goes almost to the top, which makes it one of the easier places to hear one at dusk.

When Mid-June to early July, at dusk near the summit.

Where The Adirondacks

Cerulean Warbler at Montezuma

Up to 250 pairs breed in the woodland around the wetlands, which is a large share of the state total for a bird that has been in long decline. It sings from the canopy and is heard far more than seen.

When Mid-May to early July, in mature woodland near the water.

Where Montezuma and the Finger Lakes

Canada Jay in the New York bogs

Bloomingdale Bog is the classic place to meet one, and the birds there have long since worked out that a stopped car sometimes means food. They nest in late winter, before the snow has gone.

When All year, most reliably at the bog pull-ins.

Where The Adirondacks

Snowy Owl at Montauk

The dunes and the airfield grass at the eastern end of Long Island take wintering birds in most years, and in an irruption year they can be numerous. They sit on the open ground in plain sight.

When November to March, on dune and open grassland.

Where Long Island and Jamaica Bay

When to Go

Almost every region here peaks in a different month, which makes New York a state to visit for one thing at a time. April belongs to the lake shore, June to the mountains, and the deep winter to a single stretch of river at the far western end.

Mid-March to mid-May

The lake shore turns the northbound raptor stream and concentrates it into a few miles.

Go for Derby Hill and Braddock Bay, with Montezuma an hour away for the waterfowl.

Trade off The Adirondacks are still under snow and the beaches are empty.

The Niagara gull river

Buffalo (BUF), Niagara Falls 3 nights, back to Buffalo

Three days on one New York river in early December, which is the single densest gull watching in the country. Work the gorge rim and the parkway overlooks methodically, take a scope, and bring a passport so the Canadian bank is open too. Long-tailed Duck and Harlequin come with it.

Long Island and Jamaica Bay

A hundred and twenty miles of barrier beach on the edge of the largest city in the country

Long Island carried 247 pairs of Piping Plover across 64 beaches in the 1999 survey, and Great Gull Island at its eastern end is one of the most important tern colonies in the world: 7,700 pairs of Common Tern and 1,500 pairs of Roseate. Jamaica Bay puts salt marsh and impoundments inside the city limits, under the flight path.

  • Habitat: Barrier beach and dune, salt marsh and tidal flat, brackish impoundments, low island grassland, and maritime scrub behind the dunes.
  • When: May to July for the beach nesters and the tern colonies, and November to March for Snowy Owl, sea ducks and gulls at Montauk.
  • Getting there: New York has three airports and Riverhead is a workable base for the eastern end. Great Gull Island is a research station and closed to visitors, and Jamaica Bay is reachable on the subway and a short walk.
  • Base yourself in: Riverhead
  • Look for: Piping Plover, Least Tern, Roseate Tern, Common Tern, American Oystercatcher, Black Skimmer, Clapper Rail, Saltmarsh Sparrow, Seaside Sparrow, Snowy Owl, Glossy Ibis, Red Knot
  • Parks and reserves: Fire Island National Seashore (National site), Jamaica Bay Wildlife Refuge (National wildlife refuge), Montauk Point State Park (State or provincial park), Gateway National Recreation Area (National site), Jamaica Bay Unit (Protected area)
  • Also covers: Fire Island National Seashore, Gateway National Recreation Area, Great Gull Island, Jamaica Bay Unit, Jamaica Bay Wildlife Refuge, Long Island Piping Plover Nesting Beaches, Montauk Point State Park
  • Also watch for: Grey and harbour seals haul out at Montauk through the winter, and horseshoe crabs spawn on the bay beaches in May.

The Adirondacks

Six million acres of forest and bog, holding a boreal list four hours from Manhattan

The Adirondack Park is larger than several states and its interior forest carries the highest Breeding Bird Survey count anywhere for Black-throated Blue Warbler. The peat bogs inside it hold the boreal specialities, and the top of Whiteface has Bicknell's Thrush at the end of a toll road.

  • Habitat: Mixed and coniferous forest, sphagnum bog with black spruce and tamarack, alpine summit on the high peaks, lakes and slow streams.
  • When: Mid-June to mid-July for the breeding birds. The bogs are best at first light and the blackflies peak in June.
  • Getting there: Albany or Burlington in Vermont are the airports and Saranac Lake the base. The Whiteface Memorial Highway is a toll road to just below the summit, and the bogs are entered from short unmarked pull-ins on Route 30 and Uncas Road.
  • Base yourself in: Saranac Lake
  • Look for: Bicknell's Thrush, Spruce Grouse, Black-backed Woodpecker, Canada Jay, Boreal Chickadee, Rusty Blackbird, Palm Warbler, Yellow-bellied Flycatcher, Olive-sided Flycatcher, Black-throated Blue Warbler, Canada Warbler, Common Loon
  • Parks and reserves: Adirondack Park (Protected area), Whiteface Mountain Ski Center (Protected area), High Peaks Wilderness (Protected area), Taylor Pond Wild Forest (Protected area), Vanderwhacker Mountain Wild Forest (Protected area)
  • Also covers: Adirondack Park, Bloomingdale Bog, Ferd's Bog, High Peaks Wilderness, Taylor Pond Wild Forest, Vanderwhacker Mountain Wild Forest, Whiteface Mountain Ski Center
  • Also watch for: Moose have returned to the park and black bear are common, with beaver responsible for most of the open water.

The Lake Ontario shore and the hawk watches

A lake shore that turns the spring raptor migration through ninety degrees and concentrates it

Northbound raptors will not cross open water, so they follow the south shore of Lake Ontario east until the land turns, and Derby Hill and Braddock Bay sit exactly where that happens. Hamlin Beach on the same shore counts more than 200,000 waterfowl on the autumn passage.

  • Habitat: Lake shore and open water, low bluff and scrub, deciduous woodland, marsh and orchard on the plain behind.
  • When: Mid-March to mid-May for the raptor flight, with the Broad-winged Hawk peak in the third week of April. Late September to November for the waterfowl.
  • Getting there: Syracuse or Rochester are the airports. Derby Hill is an Audubon sanctuary with two viewing fields and no fee, and Braddock Bay has a raised platform beside the road.
  • Base yourself in: Oswego
  • Look for: Broad-winged Hawk, Sharp-shinned Hawk, Red-shouldered Hawk, Rough-legged Buzzard, Golden Eagle, Bald Eagle, American Goshawk, Northern Harrier, Greater Scaup, White-winged Scoter, Red-breasted Merganser, Bonaparte's Gull
  • Parks and reserves: Braddock Bay Wildlife Management Area (National wildlife refuge), Hamlin Beach State Park (State or provincial park), McCarthy Hill State Forest (Forest or grassland), Pinnacle State Park (State or provincial park), Greece Canal Park (Protected area)
  • Also covers: Braddock Bay Wildlife Management Area, Derby Hill, Greece Canal Park, Hamlin Beach State Park, McCarthy Hill State Forest, Pinnacle State Park
  • Also watch for: White-tailed deer work the lake plain orchards and coyote are regular on the ridge above Derby Hill.

Montezuma and the Finger Lakes

A vast waterfowl staging ground, with one of the larger Cerulean Warbler populations behind it

The New York wetlands at the head of Cayuga Lake take over a hundred thousand waterfowl on migration, as many as 25,000 of them American Black Ducks. The surrounding woodland holds up to 250 pairs of Cerulean Warbler, one of the larger populations anywhere.

  • Habitat: Freshwater marsh and impoundment, wet meadow, shrubland, riparian woodland, pasture and cropland on the drumlin ridges.
  • When: Late March to early May for the northbound waterfowl, and May to July for the marsh and woodland breeders.
  • Getting there: Syracuse or Rochester are the airports and Seneca Falls the base. The refuge has a wildlife drive and observation tower off Route 20, and the northern wetlands are a patchwork of state units reached on back roads.
  • Base yourself in: Seneca Falls
  • Look for: Snow Goose, Tundra Swan, Canvasback, Redhead, Northern Shoveler, Sandhill Crane, American Bittern, Least Bittern, Black Tern, Virginia Rail, Cerulean Warbler, Prothonotary Warbler
  • Parks and reserves: Montezuma National Wildlife Refuge, Cayuga Lake State Park (State or provincial park), Northern Montezuma Wildlife Management Area (National wildlife refuge), Finger Lakes National Forest (Forest or grassland), Deer Haven Park (Protected area)
  • Also covers: Cayuga Lake State Park, Deer Haven Park, Finger Lakes National Forest, Montezuma National Wildlife Refuge, Northern Montezuma Wetlands, Northern Montezuma Wildlife Management Area
  • Also watch for: Muskrat and beaver hold the marsh open and white-tailed deer feed on the dykes at dusk.

The Niagara River corridor

A hundred thousand gulls on one river in a single day, and up to a dozen species among them

The New York rapids and the water below the falls and the generating plants keep fish coming to the surface all winter, and that draws as many as a hundred thousand gulls to a short stretch of river in a day. Most are Ring-billed, American Herring and Bonaparte's, and the rare ones are found by working through them.

  • Habitat: Fast river, rapids and gorge, plunge pool below the falls, and deciduous woodland along the parkway on both banks.
  • When: Late November to early January, when the gull numbers peak and the rarities are most likely.
  • Getting there: Buffalo is the airport, half an hour away, and Niagara Falls in New York is the base. The best viewpoints run along the Robert Moses Parkway and the gorge rim, and a passport is needed to cross to the Canadian bank where some of the best watching is.
  • Base yourself in: Niagara Falls
  • Look for: Bonaparte's Gull, Little Gull, Black-headed Gull, Iceland Gull, Glaucous Gull, Great Black-backed Gull, American Herring Gull, Ring-billed Gull, Common Goldeneye, Long-tailed Duck, Harlequin Duck, Purple Sandpiper
  • Parks and reserves: Niagara Falls State Park (State or provincial park), Buckhorn Island State Park (State or provincial park), Beaver Island State Park (State or provincial park), Artpark State Park (State or provincial park), Whirlpool State Park (State or provincial park)
  • Also covers: Artpark State Park, Beaver Island State Park, Buckhorn Island State Park, Niagara Falls State Park, Niagara River Corridor, Whirlpool State Park
  • Also watch for: The gorge below the falls holds wintering peregrines and the river carries large numbers of migrating fish.

Frequently Asked Questions

When is the best time to go birding in New York?

It depends entirely which region. The third week of April for the Derby Hill raptor flight, the second half of June for the Adirondack bogs and the Long Island tern colonies, and early December for the Niagara gulls. There is no single month that serves all of them.

Why do so many raptors pass Derby Hill?

Northbound birds will not cross open water, so they follow the south shore of Lake Ontario east until the shoreline turns north. Derby Hill sits on that corner, which funnels a whole spring flight over two fields. Braddock Bay works the same way further west.

Can I visit Great Gull Island?

No. It is a research station and closed to visitors, which is part of why nine thousand pairs of terns nest there. The colony is watched from a boat in Long Island Sound.

What makes the Niagara River so good for gulls?

The rapids and the water below the falls and the generating plants keep stunned fish at the surface all winter, so up to a hundred thousand gulls of a dozen species work a short New York stretch of river in a single day. Most are Ring-billed and American Herring, and the rare ones are found by grinding through them.

Do I need a passport for the Niagara corridor?

For the best of it, yes. Some of the strongest viewpoints face the New York bank from the Canadian side, and crossing the bridge is a border crossing. The American side alone is still worth the trip.

Can I bird here with limited mobility?

Sites on this page differ from each other more than any rating could carry, so this is the framework the standard British guides use, and the reserve's own page and warden hold the answers.

  • Permission · some reserves open ground to disabled visitors that is closed to everyone else, and it is granted by asking.
  • Priority · parking bays, hides and the nearest paths are often held for disabled visitors. Ask what is reserved and how to claim it.
  • Parking · how close a car reaches the birds shapes the whole day. Ring ahead for the nearest hard standing to the water or the hide.
  • Paths · surfaces, gradients and gate widths vary inside a single reserve. Ask which trail is boarded, surfaced or level.
  • Perches · a hide with a low window, or a bench where the view opens, turns a viewpoint into a morning.

Every reserve above with its own link carries its access detail there, and a warden answers what a page cannot.
