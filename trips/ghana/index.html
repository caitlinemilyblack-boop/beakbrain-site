At a glance

Best months
  • November to March for firm roads, concentrated game at Mole and the migrants from Europe on the lagoons
  • August and September for a second dry window in the southern forest

Getting in
Accra is the international gateway. Tamale and Kumasi take domestic flights, and both save a long drive.
Getting around
Main roads are tarred and the bus network is sound, and reaching the reserves wants a high clearance vehicle.

When to Go

Southern Ghana has two rainy seasons, one from March to July and a second in October and November, with a short dry season between them in August and September. The north has a single wet season from April to October. The dry months from November to March are when the roads work everywhere at once, and they are also when the migrants from Europe are on the coast.

The dry season

November to March

The Harmattan blows off the Sahara and the country dries out from the north. This is when almost every bird tour runs.

Go for Forest tracks and savanna roads both work, the waterholes at Mole concentrate birds and game, and the migrants from Europe are on the coastal lagoons.

Trade off Dust hazes the northern light, the north reaches 38 C by March, and this is the busiest and priciest window.

Typical daily average
  • Accra: 23 to 33 °C / 73 to 91 °F
  • Kumasi: 21 to 33 °C / 70 to 91 °F
  • Tamale: 19 to 38 °C / 66 to 100 °F

The main rains

April to July

Rain moves north through the country and the southern forest takes most of it, with the peak in May and June.

Go for Resident birds breed and sing, the forest is at its greenest, and very few other visitors are about.

Trade off The western forest roads become heavy going, the migrants from Europe have left, and afternoon storms cut the birding day short.

Typical daily average
  • Accra: 22 to 33 °C / 72 to 91 °F
  • Kumasi: 22 to 32 °C / 72 to 90 °F
  • Tamale: 22 to 36 °C / 72 to 97 °F

The short dry season

August to October

The south dries out in August and September while the north is still in its single wet season, and rain returns to the coast in October.

Go for The southern forest is open and quiet, and the first waders and terns reach the lagoons in September.

Trade off The north is at its wettest in August and September, the grass at Mole stands head high, and northern tracks are at their worst.

Typical daily average
  • Accra: 22 to 31 °C / 72 to 88 °F
  • Kumasi: 21 to 30 °C / 70 to 86 °F
  • Tamale: 22 to 33 °C / 72 to 91 °F

Accra, the Shai Hills and the coastal lagoons

Dry coastal scrub, two coastal wetlands and a granite hill, all within an hour of the airport

The south-eastern corner of Ghana is the driest part of the country, because savanna reaches the sea here and breaks the West African forest belt in two. Bare granite hills rise straight out of the Accra plain at Shai Hills, and Stone Partridge and Violet Turaco keep to the thicket around their base. Sakumo Lagoon and the Densu Delta lie either side of the capital, and waders and terns from Europe use both of them through the northern winter.

  • Habitat: Scrub, grasses and scattered trees cover a plain lying mostly below 75 m. Brackish creeks, salt flats and lagoons sit behind a low sandy shore, and granite inselbergs (isolated rock hills standing above a plain) carry dry thicket and tall grass at Shai Hills.
  • When: September to April holds the waders and terns that breed in Europe. Accra takes about 880 mm of rain a year, the least of any station in the south, and most of it falls in May and June.
  • Getting there: Shai Hills stands near Tema and a morning from Accra covers it. Tracks run level between the hills, and a short rocky scramble reaches the tomb bat cave.
  • Base yourself in: Accra
  • All year: Stone Partridge, Violet Turaco, Double-spurred Spurfowl, Blue-bellied Roller, Yellow-crowned Gonolek, Senegal Parrot, Mocking Cliff Chat, Blackcap Babbler, Vieillot's Barbet, Senegal Batis, Lavender Waxbill, Bar-breasted Firefinch, African Wattled Lapwing, Spur-winged Lapwing, Lesser Blue-eared Starling, Long-tailed Glossy Starling
  • In winter: Common Tern, Black Tern, Curlew Sandpiper, Little Stint, Grey Plover, Bar-tailed Godwit, Eurasian Whimbrel
  • National park:
    • Shai Hills Resource Reserve, Granite hills and dry thicket standing above the Accra plain on the road towards Akosombo. The tracks between the hills give the easiest savanna birding within reach of the capital.
  • Stops:
    • : A brackish lagoon between Accra and Tema that Ghana lists as a Wetland of International Importance. Terns rest on the bars and waders feed on the open mud.
    • : The mouth of the Densu river west of Accra, with saltpans behind the shore. Ghana lists it as a Wetland of International Importance for the waders that winter on it.
  • Also covers: Accra, Densu Delta, Dodowa, Kpong, Sakumo Lagoon, Shai Hills Resource Reserve, Tema, Weija
  • Also watch for: Olive baboon and kob live in the Shai Hills reserve, and Egyptian tomb bats roost in a cave among the hills.

Kakum, Cape Coast and the Muni lagoon

Moist evergreen rainforest with a canopy walkway, an hour inland from the coast forts

Kakum protects moist evergreen rainforest on the headwaters of the Kakum and Pra rivers, and a rope walkway strung between the tallest trees carries visitors through the canopy rather than under it. Hornbills cross above it at first light, and Blue Cuckooshrike, Sharpe's Apalis and Copper-tailed Starling travel with the mixed feeding flocks. The Muni lagoon at Winneba lies along the coast road east and adds terns and waders to the same trip.

  • Habitat: Moist evergreen rainforest covers the reserve, and most of it has been modified by past logging rather than left primary. Farmbush (a mosaic of cultivation, regrowth and scattered trees) surrounds the block, and a brackish lagoon sits behind the sandy shore at Winneba.
  • When: November to March gives the firmest tracks and the clearest mornings on the walkway. August and September form a short dry season between the two rainy seasons and open the forest again. Rain peaks in May and June.
  • Getting there: Kakum is about four hours by road from Accra, and public transport connects through Cape Coast. An uneven stepped track climbs 750 yards from the visitor centre to the walkway.
  • Base yourself in: Cape Coast
  • All year: Yellow-casqued Hornbill, Brown-cheeked Hornbill, Black-casqued Hornbill, Western Long-tailed Hornbill, Blue Cuckooshrike, Sharpe's Apalis, Red-cheeked Wattle-eye, Copper-tailed Starling, Green-tailed Bristlebill, Yellow-bearded Greenbul, Kemp's Longbill, Rufous-sided Broadbill, Black-collared Lovebird, Finsch's Rufous Thrush, Green-backed Woodpecker, Hartlaub's Duck, White-breasted Guineafowl, Yellow-billed Turaco
  • In winter: Rosy Bee-eater, Common Tern, Curlew Sandpiper, Little Stint
  • National park:
    • Kakum National Park, The best known block of rainforest in Ghana and the one every operator itinerary visits, on the road north from Cape Coast. The canopy walkway leaves from the visitor centre, and the Assin Attandaso Resource Reserve continues the same forest to the north.
  • Stops:
    • : A coastal lagoon and its surrounding grassland at Winneba, which Ghana lists as a Wetland of International Importance.
    • : The village at the southern gate of Kakum on the Cape Coast road, and the closest settlement to the walkway.
  • Also covers: Abrafo, Assin Fosu, Cape Coast, Elmina, Kakum National Park, Muni Lagoon, Winneba
  • Also watch for: Forest elephant, leopard, bongo, bushbuck and several primates live in the park, though they are hard to see, and more than 600 butterflies have been recorded.

Ankasa and the south-west

Wet evergreen rainforest, a coastal peat swamp and the wettest corner of the country

Ankasa and Nini Suhien together hold the largest continuous block of wet evergreen rainforest left in Ghana, in the corner where the country meets Cote d'Ivoire. Rainfall here runs to nearly 1,900 mm a year, which is more than twice what the Accra plain receives, and the forest keeps its birds through the dry months when drier reserves fall quiet. The Amansuri wetland at Beyin, a coastal swamp forest, and the peninsula at Cape Three Points complete the corner.

  • Habitat: Wet evergreen rainforest covers the reserve on ground rising to a low interior ridge. Swamp forest on peat lies behind the coast at Amansuri, and mangrove, brackish creek and sandy shore run along the edge of the sea.
  • When: December to February is the driest stretch and the easiest for the forest roads. Rain is heaviest in May and June, and a shorter dry spell in August and September opens the tracks again.
  • Getting there: Ankasa sits near Elubo, an hour and three quarters west of Takoradi, and groups use 4x4 vehicles inside the reserve. A dugout canoe reaches the Amansuri swamp from Beyin in an hour.
  • Base yourself in: Elubo
  • Look for: Hartlaub's Duck, Congo Serpent Eagle, Long-tailed Hawk, Nkulengu Rail, White-breasted Guineafowl, Chocolate-backed Kingfisher, African Dwarf Kingfisher, Blue-moustached Bee-eater, Great Blue Turaco, Yellow-billed Turaco, Western Dwarf Hornbill, Red-billed Dwarf Hornbill, Red-billed Helmetshrike, Rufous-winged Illadopsis, Red-vented Malimbe, Blue-billed Malimbe, Buff-throated Sunbird, Johanna's Sunbird, Akun Eagle-Owl, Sandy Scops Owl
  • National parks:
    • Nini Suhien National Park, The northern half of the same forest block, across the Nini river from Ankasa and far less visited.
    • Ankasa Resource Reserve, The southern half of the conservation area, entered from the Elubo road near the border with Cote d'Ivoire. A single track runs into the forest from the gate, and Shelley's Eagle Owl has been claimed here, at one of only two Ghanaian sites for it.
  • Stops:
    • Amansuri wetland : A coastal swamp forest that grows on peat behind the beach at Beyin. A canoe takes visitors in from the village.
    • Cape Three Points : The southernmost point of Ghana, with a forest reserve running down to the sea behind the headland.
  • Also covers: Amansuri wetland, Ankasa Resource Reserve, Axim, Beyin, Cape Three Points, Elubo, Half Assini, Nini Suhien National Park
  • Also watch for: The reserve holds 43 mammals, among them forest elephant, bongo and ten primates including Diana monkey and West African chimpanzee.

Bia and the western forest reserves

Moist evergreen and semi-deciduous forest reserves along the Cote d'Ivoire border

A chain of forest reserves runs north from Ankasa along the western border, and Bia stands at the centre of it as a national park with a resource reserve attached. The forest here shifts from moist evergreen in the south to moist semi-deciduous further north, so the bird list changes as the trees do. Krokosua Hills, Boin River and Tano-Nimiri were surveyed together in a rapid biological assessment, which is why they appear in the reference list of the national field guide.

  • Habitat: Moist evergreen rainforest grades northwards into moist semi-deciduous forest, with cocoa farms and farmbush between the reserves. The ground is gently undulating and the Tano river drains the western edge.
  • When: December to February is driest and easiest for the forest roads. Rain peaks in May and June, and August and September give a shorter dry spell.
  • Getting there: Bia is reached from Kumasi through Bibiani, or on the Wiawso road from Tarkwa. The park has a guest house and camping sites, and Essam Debiso has rooms as well.
  • Base yourself in: Sefwi Wiawso
  • Look for: Yellow-casqued Hornbill, Brown-cheeked Hornbill, Western Long-tailed Hornbill, White-breasted Guineafowl, Long-tailed Hawk, Congo Serpent Eagle, Yellow-billed Turaco, Great Blue Turaco, Blue Cuckooshrike, Copper-tailed Starling, Narrow-tailed Starling, Chestnut-winged Starling, Green-tailed Bristlebill, Grey-headed Bristlebill, Red-tailed Bristlebill, Western Nicator, Rufous-winged Illadopsis, Blue-headed Crested Flycatcher, Superb Sunbird
  • National park:
    • Bia National Park, A national park and resource reserve on the western border north of Enchi, and the largest protected forest in this chain.
  • Stops:
    • : A hill forest reserve east of Bia, surveyed with Draw River and Tano-Nimiri in a rapid biological assessment of the south-western forests.
    • : A town on the western border below Bia, with cocoa farms and remnant forest between it and the park.
    • : The district town south of Bia on the road towards Elubo, and the junction for the Boin River and Boin Tano reserves.

The Atewa Range

Upland evergreen forest above 500 m, the only Ghanaian ground for several forest birds

Atewa is a flat-topped ridge of upland evergreen forest, a forest type that occurs in only two places in Ghana and reaches its best development here. The range rises above 500 m and gives rise to the Densu, Ayensu and Birim rivers. Nimba Flycatcher has been recorded nowhere else in the country, and the range has been surveyed twice in rapid biological assessments because of what it holds.

  • Habitat: Upland evergreen forest covers a plateau above 500 m, with steep flanks falling to farmland and cocoa. Streams rise on the plateau and cut the edges of the ridge, and bauxite lies under the summit soils.
  • When: November to March gives the driest walking on the ridge tracks. Rain falls in most months here, and it peaks in May and June.
  • Getting there: Atewa is about two hours by road from Accra. A track climbs from the foothills onto the plateau, and reaching the upper forest is a long, steady, warm walk.
  • Base yourself in: Kibi
  • Look for: Nimba Flycatcher, Yellow-bearded Greenbul, Green-tailed Bristlebill, Sharpe's Apalis, Kemp's Longbill, Copper-tailed Starling, Blue Cuckooshrike, Yellow-billed Turaco, Rufous-sided Broadbill, Blue-headed Crested Flycatcher, Red-cheeked Wattle-eye, Western Yellow-billed Barbet, Yellow-spotted Barbet, Hairy-breasted Barbet, Buff-throated Sunbird, Johanna's Sunbird
  • National park:
    • Atewa Range Forest Reserve, A forest reserve on a plateau above Kibi. A track climbs from the town onto the ridge.
  • Stops:
    • : The town at the foot of the range on the Accra to Kumasi road, and the usual base for the ridge.
    • : A junction town east of Kibi where the road divides for Koforidua and Kumasi, with cocoa and remnant forest around it.
  • Also covers: Asiakwa, Atewa Range Forest Reserve, Bunso, Kibi, Koforidua, Nkawkaw
  • Also watch for: White-bellied pangolin lives on the range, and Atewa holds more than 100 species that are at risk of extinction.

Kumasi and the Ashanti forests

Moist semi-deciduous forest reserves and a crater lake, within an hour of the second city

The forest around Kumasi is moist semi-deciduous, drier and more seasonal than the coastal blocks, and several small reserves sit close enough to the city for a morning each. Bobiri carries a research guesthouse inside the reserve, Owabi surrounds a reservoir, and Lake Bosomtwe fills a meteorite crater south-east of the city. Black-and-white-casqued Hornbill has almost disappeared from Ghana and now holds on at Bomfobiri alone.

  • Habitat: Moist semi-deciduous forest covers the reserves, with taller emergent trees and a more open understorey than the wet forest further south. Farmbush, cocoa and a reservoir break up the ground between them, and Lake Bosomtwe fills a steep crater.
  • When: November to March is driest. A shorter dry spell falls in August and September, and Kumasi takes rain in every other month with peaks in June and October.
  • Getting there: Bobiri lies just off the Accra to Kumasi road, and a dirt road runs 3.5 km from Kubease to the visitor centre. Kumasi is 30 km away.
  • Base yourself in: Kumasi
  • Look for: Black-and-white-casqued Hornbill, West African Pied Hornbill, Piping Hornbill, Guinea Turaco, Yellow-billed Turaco, Blue-breasted Kingfisher, Shining-blue Kingfisher, African Pitta, Western Nicator, White-tailed Alethe, Orange-breasted Forest Robin, Yellow-mantled Weaver, Blue-billed Malimbe, Red-headed Malimbe, Icterine Greenbul, Swamp Palm Bulbul, Ahanta Spurfowl
  • National parks:
    • Owabi Wildlife Sanctuary, A reservoir and its surrounding forest north-west of Kumasi, which supplies the city with water.
    • Bomfobiri Wildlife Sanctuary, A sanctuary of forest and waterfalls north-east of Kumasi near Kumawu, and the last Ghanaian site for Black-and-white-casqued Hornbill.
  • Stops:
    • Bobiri Forest Reserve : A semi-deciduous forest reserve east of Kumasi run by the Forestry Research Institute of Ghana, with a butterfly sanctuary and a guesthouse inside it.
    • Lake Bosomtwe : A lake filling a meteorite crater south-east of Kumasi, ringed by a forest reserve on the crater rim.
  • Also covers: Bobiri Forest Reserve, Bomfobiri Wildlife Sanctuary, Ejisu, Ejura, Kubease, Kumasi, Kuntanase, Lake Bosomtwe, Owabi Wildlife Sanctuary
  • Also watch for: Mona and colobus monkeys move through the canopy at Bobiri, and African buffalo, black duiker and African civet live in the same reserve. A butterfly sanctuary sits inside the forest.

The Volta estuary and the eastern lagoons

Sandy shore, salt flats and the two largest lagoons in the country, east of the Volta mouth

The Volta reaches the sea at Ada, and the lagoons behind the coast east of the river mouth are the largest in Ghana. Songor is a salt flat and lagoon complex on the western side and Keta is a long shallow lagoon on the eastern side, and both are listed as Wetlands of International Importance. Terns roost on the sandbars in thousands through the northern winter, and waders feed on the flats behind them.

  • Habitat: A narrow sandy shore separates the sea from brackish creeks, salt flats and shallow lagoons. Mangrove grows in patches along the creeks, and coastal scrub, grass and scattered trees cover the ground behind.
  • When: September to April holds the terns and the waders, and numbers are highest from November to February. Rain on this coast is light and it falls mainly in May and June.
  • Getting there: Ada Foah is about an hour and a half by road from Accra, and boats work the estuary and the lake islands from the town.
  • Base yourself in: Ada Foah
  • All year: Common Tern, Black Tern, Little Tern, Curlew Sandpiper, Little Stint, Grey Plover, Bar-tailed Godwit, Eurasian Whimbrel, Spur-winged Lapwing, African Wattled Lapwing, Senegal Thick-knee, Pied Kingfisher, Malachite Kingfisher, Blue-bellied Roller, Northern Red Bishop, Orange Weaver
  • In winter: Common Tern, Black Tern, Little Tern, Curlew Sandpiper, Little Stint, Grey Plover, Bar-tailed Godwit
  • National parks:
    • Songor Ramsar Site, A lagoon and salt flat complex west of the Volta mouth at Ada, which Ghana lists as a Wetland of International Importance.
    • Keta Lagoon, The largest lagoon in Ghana lies behind a narrow sand bar between Anloga and Denu. It is shallow and brackish.
  • Stops:
    • : A town on the sand bar between the sea and Keta Lagoon, with market gardens on the sand and open flats either side.
  • Also covers: Ada Foah, Anloga, Denu, Keta Lagoon, Sogakope, Songor Ramsar Site
  • Also watch for: Marine turtles come ashore to breed on the beach at the Volta mouth.

Kalakpa, Afadjato and the Akwapim-Togo Range

Riparian forest, thicket savanna and the highest hills in Ghana, along the Togo border

A line of hills runs along the eastern border and carries the highest ground in Ghana, with Mount Afadjato reaching 885 m. Forest survives in the wetter gullies and on the range around Agumatsa, and Kyabobo covers the northern end where the hills meet the savanna. Kalakpa lies west of these hills on the plain near Ho, and its gallery forest is where operators take groups for Puvel's Illadopsis and Capuchin Babbler.

  • Habitat: Upland evergreen forest holds on in the gullies and on the higher slopes, and dry semi-deciduous forest and thicket cover the lower ground. Gallery forest lines the watercourses at Kalakpa, and tall grass savanna with scattered trees fills the space between.
  • When: November to March gives the driest walking on the hill paths. Rain falls in two seasons here as it does elsewhere in the south, and it is heaviest in May and June.
  • Getting there: Kyabobo is 400 km from Accra, and its headquarters lie 5 km off the Ho to Tamale road at Nkwanta. Guides climb Mount Afadja from Liati Wote, and the Wli visitor centre sits east of Hohoe.
  • Base yourself in: Ho
  • Look for: Capuchin Babbler, Puvel's Illadopsis, Rufous-winged Illadopsis, Guinea Turaco, Violet Turaco, Great Blue Turaco, African Emerald Cuckoo, Yellow-crowned Gonolek, Red-bellied Paradise Flycatcher, Western Bluebill, Grey-headed Oliveback, Oriole Warbler, Splendid Sunbird, Superb Sunbird, Blue-breasted Kingfisher, Piping Hornbill
  • National parks:
    • Kyabobo National Park, A national park at the northern end of the border hills near Nkwanta, where forest on the slopes meets savanna on the plain.
    • Kalakpa Resource Reserve, A reserve of thicket savanna and gallery forest west of Ho, and the ground operators use for Puvel's Illadopsis and Capuchin Babbler.
  • Stops:
    • Mount Afadjato : The highest peak in Ghana at 885 m, on the border with Togo above the village of Liati Wote.
    • Agumatsa Wildlife Sanctuary : A sanctuary of forest and waterfall on the Agumatsa range at Wli. The path in starts at the village.
  • Also covers: Agumatsa Wildlife Sanctuary, Hohoe, Kalakpa Resource Reserve, Kpando, Kyabobo National Park, Mount Afadjato, Nkwanta, Wli
  • Also watch for: Kyabobo holds buffalo, kob, warthog, aardvark, bushbuck, duiker and baboon, and a park survey lists at least 500 butterflies.

Mole and the Damongo savanna

Guinea savanna woodland and a waterhole below an escarpment, the largest park in Ghana

Mole is the biggest protected area in Ghana and the one savanna site every operator itinerary reaches. The camp stands on an escarpment above two waterholes, and elephants and antelope come to drink below it in the dry season. Four vultures that have collapsed elsewhere in West Africa survive here in numbers, and Hooded Vulture is absent from every Ghanaian reserve except Mole and Shai Hills.

  • Habitat: Guinea savanna covers the park, with wooded grassland, tall perennial grasses between the trees and gallery forest along the watercourses. Fires burn through the grass in the dry season, and bare granite outcrops break the surface in places.
  • When: November to April is the dry season and the birds and mammals concentrate at the waterholes as it goes on. The single wet season runs from April to October and peaks in September, and the grass is head high by the end of it.
  • Getting there: Mole is 24 km from Damongo and 146 km from Tamale on a paved road, and most groups fly Accra to Tamale rather than drive. An armed game guard walks with visitors.
  • Base yourself in: Damongo
  • All year: White-headed Vulture, White-backed Vulture, Hooded Vulture, Saddle-billed Stork, Marabou Stork, Abyssinian Ground Hornbill, Abyssinian Roller, Northern Carmine Bee-eater, Red-throated Bee-eater, Violet Turaco, Stone Partridge, Fox Kestrel, Bateleur, Martial Eagle, Bearded Barbet, Yellow-billed Shrike, White-fronted Black Chat, Sun Lark, Piapiac, White-crowned Lapwing
  • In the breeding season: Standard-winged Nightjar, Northern Carmine Bee-eater, Red-throated Bee-eater
  • National park:
    • Mole National Park, The largest protected area in Ghana, on the escarpment west of Damongo. Guided walks leave from the camp and drop to the waterholes below it.
  • Stops:
    • Larabanga : The village at the park entrance on the Damongo to Sawla road, with an old mud and stick mosque at its centre.
    • : The district town east of the park and the last place on the way in with fuel and supplies.
  • Also covers: Bole, Damongo, Larabanga, Mole National Park, Sawla
  • Also watch for: Elephant, buffalo, roan antelope, kob and warthog use the same waterholes as the birds, and the park now holds between 600 and 800 elephants. Baboon and patas monkey are common along the tracks.

Bui, Digya and the Volta basin

Riparian forest on the Black Volta and the wooded shore of the largest artificial lake in Africa

The Volta basin fills the middle of Ghana and Lake Volta fills the basin, and two national parks work its edges. Bui sits on the Black Volta where the river cuts a gorge through hills on the western border, and its riparian forest carries Black-throated Coucal. Digya covers a peninsula on the western shore of the lake, where forest in the transition zone between savanna and rainforest holds Crowned Eagle and Black-headed Bee-eater.

  • Habitat: Guinea savanna covers most of the ground, with wooded grassland and tall grasses between the trees. Gallery forest lines the Black Volta at Bui and the streams at Digya, and the lake shore itself carries drowned timber and seasonal mudflats.
  • When: November to April is the dry season, when the tracks work and the river level falls to expose rocks and sandbars. The single wet season runs from April to October.
  • Getting there: Digya is reached from Atebubu in the north, through Kwame Danso and Donkorkrom.
  • Base yourself in: Wenchi
  • Look for: Black-throated Coucal, Crowned Eagle, Black-headed Bee-eater, African Woolly-necked Stork, African Finfoot, Rock Pratincole, African Skimmer, White-crowned Lapwing, Senegal Thick-knee, Giant Kingfisher, Blue-bellied Roller, Violet Turaco, Guinea Turaco, Grey-headed Oliveback, Red-headed Quelea, African Pygmy Goose
  • National parks:
    • Bui National Park, A national park on the Black Volta on the western border, where the river cuts a gorge between hills. A dam now stands at the gorge.
    • Digya National Park, A large park on a peninsula in the western shore of Lake Volta, in the transition zone where savanna meets forest.
  • Stops:
    • : A lake port on the eastern side of the Digya peninsula where the ferry crosses Lake Volta.
  • Also covers: Atebubu, Bamboi, Bui National Park, Digya National Park, Kwame Danso, Wenchi, Yeji
  • Also watch for: Digya holds elephant, buffalo, waterbuck and six primates, and West African manatee and crocodile live in the lake water.

Gbele, Tono and the far north

Sudan savanna, an irrigation reservoir and the rivers where Egyptian Plover feeds

The far north is the driest part of Ghana and the only part that reaches Sudan savanna, where the trees stand further apart and the grasses are shorter. Gbele Resource Reserve protects a block of it in the Upper West, the Tono reservoir near Navrongo draws birds that occur nowhere else in the country, and the White Volta and the Black Volta carry Egyptian Plover onto their sandbars as the water drops. Little Green Bee-eater has been recorded at Tono and at no other Ghanaian site.

  • Habitat: Sudan savanna occupies the extreme north-east, with short grasses, widely scattered trees and bare granite outcrops. Guinea savanna covers the rest, gallery forest lines the rivers, and an irrigation reservoir and its shallow margins lie at Tono.
  • When: November to March is dry, and the sandbars on the rivers are widest in February and March. The single wet season runs from April to October and peaks in August, when Bolgatanga takes more rain in the month than Accra takes in three.
  • Getting there: The Gbele reserve begins 17 km south of Tumu, and dugout canoes run river safaris along the Black Volta at Wechiau.
  • Base yourself in: Bolgatanga
  • All year: Egyptian Plover, African Green Bee-eater, Fox Kestrel, Standard-winged Nightjar, Abyssinian Roller, Bearded Barbet, Northern Red-billed Hornbill, African Grey Hornbill, Beaudouin's Snake Eagle, Grasshopper Buzzard, Bruce's Green Pigeon, Speckle-fronted Weaver, Chestnut-crowned Sparrow-Weaver, Sun Lark, Green-winged Pytilia, Golden-breasted Bunting, Brown-rumped Bunting, White-crowned Lapwing
  • In the breeding season: Standard-winged Nightjar, Northern Carmine Bee-eater
  • National parks:
    • Gbele Resource Reserve, A reserve of Guinea savanna and gallery forest between Tumu and Wa, and the largest protected area in the Upper West.
    • Wechiau Community Hippo Sanctuary
  • Stops:
    • : An irrigation reservoir west of Navrongo with shallow margins and degraded scrub around it. It is the only Ghanaian site for African Green Bee-eater.
    • Wechiau : A stretch of the Black Volta south-west of Wa managed as a community hippo sanctuary, with riverine forest along the bank.
    • : The town below the Tono reservoir and the base for the White Volta and the thicket clump savanna around Sapeliga.
  • Also covers: Bolgatanga, Gbele Resource Reserve, Navrongo, Tono Dam, Tumu, Wechiau Community Hippo Sanctuary
  • Also watch for: Hippos live along 40 km of the Black Volta at Wechiau, and early morning and evening are when they surface. Vervet monkeys feed in the trees along the bank.

Frequently Asked Questions

When is the best time to go birding in Ghana?

November to March. The Harmattan dries the country out from the north, forest tracks and savanna roads both work, the waterholes at Mole concentrate birds and game, and the waders and terns that breed in Europe are on the coastal lagoons. August and September give a second, shorter dry window in the southern forest. The three seasons.

Where do you see White-necked Rockfowl in Ghana?

At the colony at Bonkro, which every Ghanaian bird tour visits, and at the community sanctuary at Asumura. The bird builds mud nests under rock overhangs on the rainforest floor, birds come in during the late afternoon, and the Ghanaian nesting areas were described in the journal of the West African Ornithological Society in 2008.

How many bird species live in Ghana?

No bird occurs in Ghana and nowhere else, and the draw here is something different. Ghana holds the eastern end of the Upper Guinea forest, a block running from Guinea to Ghana whose birds live in no other forest on earth, and it carries a full northern savanna list in the same country. Every Ghanaian species.

Which parts of Ghana are worth a route?

Eleven regions, from the wet evergreen forest at Ankasa in the south-west corner to the Sudan savanna on the Burkina Faso border. They follow the Important Bird Areas listed in the national field guide, and the standard trip strings the forest at Kakum and Ankasa together with the savanna at Mole. Where to bird.

How long does a Ghana birding trip take?

Ten days covers the forest at Kakum, Ankasa and Bia. Three weeks adds the Atewa ridge, the Ashanti reserves and the drive north to Mole and the Sudan savanna, which is what the longer operator itineraries run. The five routes.

Can you drive yourself around Ghana?

The main roads are tarred and the bus network is sound, so moving between towns is straightforward. Reaching the forest reserves and the northern parks wants a high clearance vehicle, and the national parks are walked with a guide arranged at the gate.

What is the Upper Guinea forest?

The block of lowland rainforest that once ran unbroken from Guinea to Ghana, west of the Dahomey Gap where savanna reaches the sea in Togo and Benin. Ghana holds its eastern end, most of it now inside forest reserves, and its birds are found in no other forest. Ten of the sixteen birds restricted to it have been recorded in Ghana.

Which birds can you only see in West Africa?

White-necked Rockfowl, White-breasted Guineafowl, Rufous Fishing Owl, Green-backed Woodpecker, Copper-tailed Starling, Yellow-bearded Greenbul, Sharpe's Apalis and Rufous-winged Illadopsis all live in the Upper Guinea forest and nowhere else. Brown-cheeked Hornbill, Red-billed Helmetshrike and Finsch's Rufous Thrush reach Nigeria and stop there.

Can I bird here with limited mobility?

Sites on this page differ from each other more than any rating could carry, so this is the framework the standard British guides use, and the reserve's own page and warden hold the answers.

  • Permission · some reserves open ground to disabled visitors that is closed to everyone else, and it is granted by asking.
  • Priority · parking bays, hides and the nearest paths are often held for disabled visitors. Ask what is reserved and how to claim it.
  • Parking · how close a car reaches the birds shapes the whole day. Ring ahead for the nearest hard standing to the water or the hide.
  • Paths · surfaces, gradients and gate widths vary inside a single reserve. Ask which trail is boarded, surfaced or level.
  • Perches · a hide with a low window, or a bench where the view opens, turns a viewpoint into a morning.

Every reserve above with its own link carries its access detail there, and a warden answers what a page cannot.
