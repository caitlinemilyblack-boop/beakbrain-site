At a glance

Best months
  • August to November for the best birding in the woodland and the forest
  • May to July for Shoebill as the flood water falls
Regions

Routes
The Great North Road: Kasanka, Bangweulu and Mutinondo12 daysThe Luangwa valley7 daysThe Zambezi, Kafue and the flats11 daysThe lower valley and Mwinilunga12 days
Getting in
Lusaka takes the international flights, and Livingstone and Mfuwe are the two airports most birding trips use inside the country.
Getting around
The tarred Great North Road reaches most of the eastern sites. A high-clearance vehicle is the minimum and a 4WD is essential during the rains, when several parks close to vehicles altogether.

When to Go

The Zambian year divides into a hot wet season from November to April, a cool dry season from May to August and a hot dry spell in September and October. August to November gives the best birding in both woodland and forest. The rains bring the widest diversity and the worst roads, and Shoebill is easiest in the early dry season as the flood water falls.

Hot and wet

November to April

The rains bring the widest diversity of the year, and they close several of the best parks while they do it.

Go for African Pitta in the lower valley, the dambo rails and flufftails, and Palearctic migrants in the woodland.

Trade off Much of Kafue, South Luangwa and the north west becomes hard or impossible to reach, and the flood plains are under water.

Cool and dry

May to August

The roads firm up, the weather is pleasant, and the flood water starts to fall off the swamps.

Go for Shoebill by canoe at Bangweulu from May to July, and the miombo bird parties that run through the middle of the year.

Trade off The migrants have gone and forest birds are quiet in the coldest weeks.

Hot and dry

September to October

The strongest all-round window, with high bird activity and every road open.

Go for Southern Carmine Bee-eater colonies on the Luangwa, migrant waders on the Kafue flats, and the north-western forests at their most active.

Trade off It is the hottest part of the year and water is scarce away from the rivers.

South Luangwa and Mfuwe

A broad tropical river with sandbars, oxbows and mopane woodland behind

The Luangwa runs shallow and wide through a valley that falls away below the Muchinga escarpment. When the water drops, the sandbars carry African Skimmer and White-crowned Lapwing, enormous Southern Carmine Bee-eater colonies open in the banks, and Lilian's Lovebird comes to surface water in the mopane.

  • Habitat: A broad tropical river with sandbars, oxbow lakes and seasonal marshes, riparian forest along the banks and extensive mopane woodland beyond it.
  • When: May to November for firm roads and for the water birds that concentrate as the oxbows dry. September and October for the bee-eater colonies. The rains from December to April flood large parts of the valley.
  • Getting there: The park is entered at Mfuwe, 130 km from Chipata on a road that takes about three hours when dry and much longer when wet. There is an airstrip at Mfuwe, and day visitors may drive in with their own vehicle.
  • Base yourself in: Mfuwe
  • All year: African Skimmer, Lilian's Lovebird, Southern Carmine Bee-eater, White-crowned Lapwing, Pel's Fishing Owl, Racket-tailed Roller, Böhm's Spinetail, Southern Ground Hornbill
  • In the breeding season: Southern Carmine Bee-eater, Horus Swift, White-fronted Bee-eater, Yellow-billed Stork
  • On passage: African Pitta, Thrush Nightingale
  • In winter: Lilian's Lovebird, Arnot's Chat, Meves's Starling, Racket-tailed Roller
  • Also recorded in the parks here: Abdim's Stork, Dickinson's Kestrel
  • National park: South Luangwa National Park
  • Stops:
    • : The village at the main gate, with fuel, camp sites and organised game drives. Nine species of swift have been recorded at the bridge here alone.
    • : The sector on the eastern bank. Two sizeable Yellow-billed Stork colonies nest here.
    • : The town on the main road east, and the last fuel and supplies before the valley road.
  • Also covers: Chipata, Mfuwe, Nsefu, South Luangwa National Park
  • Also watch for: Thornicroft's Giraffe and Cookson's Wildebeest live only in this valley, and Leopard are seen here as readily as anywhere on the continent.

The Bangweulu Swamps

Flood plain and papyrus, and the most reliable Shoebill in the region

Bangweulu is a swamp and flood plain covering a large part of the north, and it is where people come for Shoebill. Black Lechwe fill the plain in thousands, and Wattled Crane and Denham's Bustard gather among them in flocks that can run into the hundreds.

  • Habitat: Grassy flood plain and papyrus swamp with termitaria, and miombo woodland and pockets of riparian forest behind them.
  • When: Water is highest in March and April. Shoebill is easiest as the water falls from May to July, when it is reached by canoe. From August to November the bird retreats into permanent swamp and is searched for on foot or by vehicle.
  • Getting there: The drive to Chikuni village is about 120 km of rough road from either approach and takes at least five hours, and a 4WD is needed. From the village the swamp is reached by canoe, and a light aircraft is the quickest way in.
  • Base yourself in: Chikuni
  • All year: Shoebill, Wattled Crane, Denham's Bustard, Swamp Flycatcher, Fülleborn's Longclaw, Great Snipe, Lesser Jacana, Blue-breasted Bee-eater
  • In the breeding season: Katanga Masked Weaver, Cuckoo-finch
  • On passage: Montagu's Harrier, Pallid Harrier, Great Snipe
  • In winter: Shoebill, Wattled Crane, Denham's Bustard, Sooty Chat
  • Also recorded in the parks here: African Grass Owl, African Yellow Warbler
  • National park: Lavushi Manda National Park
  • Stops:
    • Chikuni: The southern village at the swamp edge, and the last point a vehicle reaches in most months.
    • : A village on the eastern approach, with productive miombo woodland along the road to it.
    • : A lake on the western approach. The closed-canopy miombo around it is the best on that road.
  • Also covers: Chikuni, Chiundaponde, Lake Wakawaka, Lavushi Manda National Park
  • Also watch for: Black Lechwe occur here and almost nowhere else, and Tsessebe, Oribi and Sitatunga share the plain with them.

Kasanka National Park

Miombo, swamp forest and a hide eighteen metres up a mahogany

Kasanka is small and holds a great deal. Miombo woodland, papyrus swamp and mushitu (moist evergreen forest that grows inside a wet dambo) all sit within a short drive of each other, and the Fibwe hide looks out over the Kapabi swamp from high in a mahogany.

  • Habitat: Miombo woodland, flood plain and pans, papyrus swamp, and mushitu and riparian forest along the rivers.
  • When: Good all year, with harder going from January to March at the height of the rains. Straw-coloured Fruit Bats fill the forest around the hide in November and December.
  • Getting there: The entrance is 55 km north of the Great North Road on a tarred road. The turn north comes 36 km beyond Serenje, which is the last fuel. The park headquarters lies 11 km inside the gate on good gravel.
  • Base yourself in: Serenje
  • All year: Ross's Turaco, Böhm's Bee-eater, Pel's Fishing Owl, Anchieta's Barbet, Bocage's Akalat, Wattled Crane, Black-necked Eremomela, African Broadbill
  • In the breeding season: Wattled Crane, Fülleborn's Longclaw, Marsh Widowbird
  • On passage: Blue Quail, Streaky-breasted Flufftail, Black Coucal
  • In winter: Ross's Turaco, Pel's Fishing Owl, African Spotted Creeper, Racket-tailed Roller
  • Also recorded in the parks here: Black-eared Seedeater, Blue-mantled Crested Flycatcher
  • National park:
    • Kasanka National Park, A small park on the southern edge of the Bangweulu basin, with miombo, swamp and mushitu inside one boundary.
  • Stops:
    • : The town on the Great North Road and the last fuel before the park turn-off 36 km further north.
    • : A viewing platform 18 m up a mahogany over the Kapabi papyrus swamp, with a footpath through mushitu forest beside it.
  • Also covers: Fibwe, Serenje
  • Also watch for: Sitatunga are watched from the hide in numbers, often more than twenty at once, and Puku feed on the flood plain.

Mutinondo and the Muchinga miombo

Tall woodland, dambos and granite hills above an escarpment

Mutinondo is a private reserve of 10,000 hectares perched above the Muchinga escarpment, and it gives the strongest miombo (open woodland of brachystegia trees on poor sandy soil) birding in the country. Bar-winged Weaver creeps along lichen-hung branches here more reliably than anywhere, and Chestnut-headed Flufftail calls from the drying margins of the dambos.

  • Habitat: Tall miombo woodland, dambos, riparian forest, small rivers and granite hills.
  • When: Reachable all year. Mixed foraging flocks in the woodland run from January to July, and the dambo birds are easiest once the rains have set in.
  • Getting there: The turn-off leaves the Great North Road 72 km south of Mpika and 164 km north of Serenje, and a hard sand track runs 25 km through woodland to the camp. The signpost at the railway siding is visible only from the south.
  • Base yourself in: Mpika
  • All year: Bar-winged Weaver, Anchieta's Barbet, Souza's Shrike, Anchieta's Sunbird, Chestnut-headed Flufftail, African Spotted Creeper, Böhm's Flycatcher, Racket-tailed Roller
  • In the breeding season: Fülleborn's Longclaw, Marsh Widowbird, Locust Finch
  • On passage: Blue Quail, Swamp Nightjar
  • In winter: Bar-winged Weaver, African Spotted Creeper, Miombo Rock Thrush, Rufous-bellied Tit
  • Stops:
    • : A private reserve above the escarpment, with tracks from the camp into tall woodland, dambos and granite hills.
    • : The town on the Great North Road north of the turn-off, and the nearest fuel and supplies.
  • Also covers: Kalonje, Mpika, Mutinondo Wilderness

Livingstone and the Zambezi

Spray-zone forest, a basalt gorge and mopane country to the west

The Zambezi above Mosi-oa-Tunya is reached from many points along the bank, and the forest in the permanent spray holds Schalow's Turaco and Trumpeter Hornbill. A viewpoint over the Batoka Gorge below gives a long watch over swifts, martins and raptors. West of Livingstone the road runs into mopane, where Black-cheeked Lovebird comes to waterholes soon after dawn.

  • Habitat: Riverine forest in the permanent spray of the falls, open river with exposed rocks and sandbars, a deep basalt gorge below, and mopane woodland and thorny thicket to the west.
  • When: August to January for Rock Pratincole, while the rocks in the river are exposed. The lovebird country west of town is best in the early morning and late afternoon at waterholes.
  • Getting there: Livingstone has an airport and sits about 10 km from the falls, and the national park entrance is signposted from town. The tarred road west reaches the lovebird country, and the sandy tracks off it towards the Zambezi flood plain need a 4WD.
  • Base yourself in: Livingstone
  • All year: Schalow's Turaco, Black-cheeked Lovebird, Rock Pratincole, African Finfoot, White-backed Night Heron, Trumpeter Hornbill, Half-collared Kingfisher, Verreaux's Eagle
  • In the breeding season: Rock Pratincole, African Black Swift
  • On passage: Black Stork, Squacco Heron
  • In winter: Black-cheeked Lovebird, Schalow's Turaco, Brown Firefinch
  • National park: Mosi-oa-Tunya National Park
  • Stops:
    • : The town above the falls, with the national park at its edge and sewage ponds that have produced many of the country's rarities.
    • Simungoma: A village on the tarred road west. The mopane country north of it is the place to look for Black-cheeked Lovebird.
    • : A settlement north of the main road. The track to it runs through mopane and thorny thicket, with waterholes worth a long early morning wait.
  • Also covers: Livingstone, Mosi-oa-Tunya National Park, Mulobezi, Simungoma

Lochinvar and the Kafue Flats

A flood plain seventy kilometres wide, and the country's only endemic bird

The Kafue flood plain spreads over a vast area and Lochinvar on its southern bank has more than 400 species recorded. As the water falls in September and October the resident birds are joined by migrant waders in their thousands. Chaplin's Barbet, the one bird found nowhere but this country, sits white and conspicuous in the sycamore figs on the farms to the south.

  • Habitat: Shallow flooded grassland, mudflat and open lake on black cotton clay, with dry plains, thicket and fig-studded farmland around the edges.
  • When: September and October at the end of the dry season, when the migrant waders arrive in numbers. Visits after heavy rain need a well equipped vehicle.
  • Getting there: The park is signposted west from the road just north of Monze and lies about 40 km in on dirt. High clearance is essential, the flood plain is pure black cotton clay, and a game scout can be asked for at headquarters to help with navigation.
  • Base yourself in: Monze
  • All year: Chaplin's Barbet, Wattled Crane, Slaty Egret, African Skimmer, Denham's Bustard, Long-toed Lapwing, Yellow-throated Sandgrouse, Black-tailed Godwit
  • In the breeding season: Chaplin's Barbet, Cuckoo-finch, Streaky-breasted Flufftail
  • On passage: Ruff, Caspian Plover, Black-tailed Godwit, Corn Crake
  • In winter: Wattled Crane, Denham's Bustard, Secretarybird, Yellow-throated Sandgrouse
  • Also recorded in the parks here: Buffy Pipit, Blue-billed Teal
  • National parks:
    • Lochinvar National Park, The southern bank of the flats, with Chunga Lake and its flooded grassland at the centre of the birding.
    • Blue Lagoon National Park, The sister park on the northern bank, with a causeway that runs out onto the flood plain.
  • Stops:
    • : The town on the main road, and the turn-off west for the park about 40 km away.
    • : A town with private conservation farms just north of it, and the easiest place to find Chaplin's Barbet.
  • Also covers: Blue Lagoon National Park, Choma, Lochinvar National Park, Monze
  • Also watch for: Kafue Lechwe live only on these flats and run to many thousands, alongside hundreds of Plains Zebra.

Kafue National Park

A park the size of a small country, with plains, miombo and mopane

Kafue is divided by the Great West Road. The forest-lined Kafue River and the Busanga Plains lie to the north, and teak forest, savanna and mopane to the south. Black-cheeked Lovebird lives in the southern mopane, and the riparian forest along the rivers holds Ross's Turaco and Pel's Fishing Owl.

  • Habitat: Mopane and miombo woodland, riparian and teak forest, thicket, savanna, dambos and open flood plain.
  • When: May to November. Most of the park is inaccessible during the rains from November to April, and the northern sector closes to vehicles for much of that time.
  • Getting there: The northern sector leaves the Great West Road just after the Kafue River crossing, and a dirt road runs north to the plains. The southern gate is 69 km from Kalomo and takes at least two and a half hours. Itezhi-Tezhi is signposted from the same main road.
  • Base yourself in: Itezhi-Tezhi
  • All year: Black-cheeked Lovebird, Ross's Turaco, Pel's Fishing Owl, Böhm's Bee-eater, Racket-tailed Roller, African Finfoot, Wattled Crane, Southern Crested Guineafowl
  • In the breeding season: Rock Pratincole, Saddle-billed Stork, Wattled Crane
  • On passage: Rosy-throated Longclaw, Fülleborn's Longclaw
  • In winter: Black-cheeked Lovebird, Arnot's Chat, Meves's Starling, Souza's Shrike
  • Also recorded in the parks here: Dusky Lark, Orange-winged Pytilia
  • National park: Kafue National Park
  • Stops:
    • : The dam and town on the eastern boundary, and the usual way into the middle of the park.
    • : The town on the main tarred road, and the start of the 69 km drive to the southern gate.
    • : The town on the Great West Road nearest the northern sector, and the last supplies before the plains.
  • Also covers: Itezhi-Tezhi, Kalomo, Mumbwa
  • Also watch for: The park holds more antelope species than any other on the continent, with the largest concentrations on the northern plains and in the far south.

Mwinilunga and the north west

Congo basin forest reaching into the far north western corner

The far north west runs up between two international borders, and its birds belong to the Congo forests rather than to the woodland behind them. Patches of mushitu hold Afep Pigeon, Ross's Turaco and Bamboo Warbler, and the broad dambo at Chitunta carries Grimwood's Longclaw and Angola Lark.

  • Habitat: Mushitu forest, tall miombo woodland, bracken and briar scrub, broad dambos and open plains, and rivers with riparian forest and rapids.
  • When: September and October are ideal, when bird activity is high. November and December are interesting and extremely wet, and the forest birds go quiet in the cold dry months of June and July.
  • Getting there: The road is tarred from Lusaka as far as Mwinilunga town, and the drive is close to 1,000 km and better split over two days. Beyond the town the tar ends and a dirt road runs north across the Chitunta Plain towards Ikelenge.
  • Base yourself in: Ikelenge
  • All year: Black-collared Bulbul, Afep Pigeon, Ross's Turaco, Grimwood's Longclaw, Bamboo Warbler, Bannerman's Sunbird, Forbes's Plover, Laura's Woodland Warbler
  • In the breeding season: Bocage's Weaver, Black-and-rufous Swallow
  • On passage: Great Snipe, Blue Quail, Short-tailed Pipit
  • In winter: Black-collared Bulbul, Ross's Turaco, Bar-winged Weaver, Anchieta's Barbet
  • Stops:
    • : The town where the tar ends. The forest reserve between it and the Chitunta Plain holds rich miombo woodland.
    • : The settlement at the northern end of the road, with a private farm nearby that most visitors use as a base.
  • Also covers: Ikelenge, Mwinilunga
  • Also watch for: A private wildlife reserve on the farm at Ikelenge carries plains antelope across its grassland.

The Lower Zambezi

Riparian thicket in a hot valley, and the pitta that arrives with the rains

The valley below Lake Kariba is hot and thickly wooded, and it is the reason many birders come here in the middle of the rains. African Pitta calls from the riparian thickets along the small rivers on the Siavonga road, and the same thickets hold spinetails, nicators and robin-chats.

  • Habitat: Riparian thicket along seasonal rivers, mopane woodland, dense riverine woodland, oxbow pools and open plains along the Zambezi.
  • When: Mid November to March, which is when African Pitta calls and displays. Access in the rains is limited and much of the national park is hard to reach.
  • Getting there: The thickets on the Siavonga road lie about 25 km and 35 km from the Chirundu turn-off and can be birded on a day trip from Lusaka. The national park is entered by crossing the Kafue on a pontoon near Chirundu and following about 50 km of dirt, and some camps transfer visitors by boat.
  • Base yourself in: Siavonga
  • All year: African Pitta, Böhm's Spinetail, Mottled Spinetail, Eastern Nicator, Red-capped Robin-Chat, River Warbler, Livingstone's Flycatcher, Southern Crested Guineafowl
  • In the breeding season: African Pitta, Böhm's Spinetail
  • On passage: Thrush Nightingale, River Warbler, African Emerald Cuckoo
  • In winter: Southern Crested Guineafowl, Eastern Nicator
  • Also recorded in the parks here: Bearded Woodpecker, Cut-throat Finch
  • National park: Lower Zambezi National Park
  • Stops:
    • : The town on the lake shore, and the base for the pitta thickets along the road down to the valley.
    • : The border town on the Zambezi, and the turn-off for both the Siavonga road and the national park.
  • Also covers: Chirundu, Lower Zambezi National Park, Siavonga
  • Also watch for: Elephant, Buffalo and Hippopotamus use the valley floor, so exploring the thickets on foot carries real risk.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I bird here with limited mobility?

Often yes, and it varies by site. A good deal of the birding here happens from roadsides, hides and lodge grounds rather than on long walks, and some reserves keep flat, surfaced trails. Ask a lodge or reserve directly about step free access, surfaces and seating before you book. The Birdability Map records those details for birding sites, and their Access Considerations guide lists the questions worth asking. Coverage is thin outside the United States, so read a blank area as unrecorded rather than unsuitable.
