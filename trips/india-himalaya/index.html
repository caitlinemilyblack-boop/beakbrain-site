At a glance

Best months
November to April for the foothills and hill stations, June to September for Ladakh, February for the snow leopards
Regions
Corbett and the Kumaon foothillsSattal, Pangot and the Nainital hillsThe Great Himalayan National Park and HimachalLadakh: Hemis, the Indus and the high lakesSikkim and the Darjeeling hills
Getting in
Delhi (DEL) for Corbett and the Kumaon hills, Bhuntar (KUU) for Himachal, Leh (IXL) for Ladakh, and Bagdogra (IXB) for Darjeeling and Sikkim
Getting around
A car and driver in the foothills and hills, a flight into Leh and two nights at 3,500 m before going any higher

When to Go

The Himalaya has one monsoon and two answers to it. The foothills and the eastern hills are unbirdable from late June to September, when Darjeeling takes 900 mm in a month. Ladakh, behind the range, is dry all year and its season is the opposite: summer for the high lakes, and February for the snow leopard watching that has built a whole trade around it.

Peak

November to April

The dry, clear half of the year in the foothills and the hills.

Go for Ramnagar records 7 mm in November, Ibisbill winters on the Corbett shingle, the hill-station roads are open and the Himalayan views are clearest.

Trade off Corbett is closed from mid-June to mid-November for the monsoon and the highest ground is under snow.

Typical daily average Ramnagar 8 to 22 °C / 46 to 72 °F, Nainital 4 to 14 °C / 39 to 57 °F, Darjeeling 10 to 20 °C / 50 to 68 °F

Ladakh

June to September

The high-altitude summer, which is the only time the Ladakh lakes are reachable.

Go for Black-necked Cranes breed at Tso Kar and Tso Moriri at 4,500 m, the passes are open, and Leh records 15 mm in its wettest month.

Trade off The rest of the Himalaya is under the monsoon, so a Ladakh trip stands on its own.

Typical daily average Leh 10 to 25 °C / 50 to 77 °F, Kullu 16 to 29 °C / 61 to 84 °F, Nainital 15 to 22 °C / 59 to 72 °F

Snow leopard

February and March

The Hemis winter season, which exists for one animal.

Go for Snow Leopard comes low into the Rumbak valley in the cold and is watched from ridge lines with local spotters. It is the most reliable snow leopard watching anywhere.

Trade off It is minus fourteen at night in Leh, the homestays are unheated, and the birding is a side effect rather than the point.

Typical daily average Leh -11 to 2 °C / 12 to 36 °F, Ramnagar 10 to 25 °C / 50 to 77 °F, Nainital 4 to 14 °C / 39 to 57 °F

Corbett and the Kumaon foothills

Sal forest and a wide river bed in the Kumaon foothills, in India's oldest national park

Corbett was declared in 1936 and its Ramganga river bed is one of the great birding landscapes in Asia: shingle, water and sal forest with the hills behind. Ibisbill winters on the shingle, Great Hornbill crosses the valley at eye level from the Dhikala grasslands, and Wallcreeper works the river cliffs. The Kosi river outside the park is birded from the road and is easier to reach.

  • Look for: Ibisbill, Great Hornbill, Wallcreeper, Pallas's Fish Eagle, Crested Kingfisher, Tawny Fish Owl, Great Slaty Woodpecker, Long-billed Thrush
  • Stops:
    • , the town at the main gate, with the Kosi river running past it and the lodges along it
  • Also watch for: Corbett holds one of the densest tiger populations anywhere and Asian Elephant in herds on the Dhikala grasslands. Gharial and Mugger both use the Ramganga, and Otter is regular from the river track.

Sattal, Pangot and the Nainital hills

Oak and pine forest at 1,500 to 2,400 m an hour above Corbett, birded from the road

The Nainital hills are the accessible Himalayan foothills and the birding is done from a single road that climbs through oak, rhododendron and pine. Cheer Pheasant and Koklass Pheasant are on the ridges above Pangot, the Sattal lakes below hold the thrushes and forktails, and the whole set is reachable on a four-day extension to Corbett.

  • Look for: Cheer Pheasant, Koklass Pheasant, Spotted Forktail, Rufous-bellied Woodpecker, Chestnut-crowned Laughingthrush, Red-billed Blue Magpie, Himalayan Rubythroat, Slaty-headed Parakeet
  • Stops:
    • , the lake town at 2,000 m, with the road up to Pangot and Kilbury beyond it
    • , the seven lakes below the town at 1,370 m, with the oak forest and the bird hides around them
  • Also watch for: Leopard uses the ridge above Pangot and is met on the road at night more often than anywhere else in these hills. Yellow-throated Marten, Goral and Barking Deer are all regular by day.

The Great Himalayan National Park and Himachal

Steep temperate forest to alpine meadow in a World Heritage park with no road through it

The Great Himalayan National Park is walked into from the Tirthan valley and there is no road inside it, which is why it holds what it does. Western Tragopan lives here in the largest remaining population, Himalayan Monal on the meadows above, and Cheer Pheasant on the grassy slopes lower down. It is trekking rather than birding from a vehicle.

  • Look for: Western Tragopan, Himalayan Monal, Snow Partridge, Himalayan Snowcock, Bearded Vulture, Rufous-bellied Niltava, Yellow-billed Blue Magpie, Wallcreeper
  • Stops:
    • , the valley town with the airstrip, and the road up the Tirthan valley to the park boundary
  • Also watch for: Himalayan Brown Bear and Himalayan Tahr both use the high park, and Snow Leopard is present in the highest reaches and effectively never seen. Musk Deer holds the birch forest at the treeline.

Ladakh: Hemis, the Indus and the high lakes

Cold desert above 3,000 m in the rain shadow of the main range, with the Tibetan plateau's birds

Ladakh sits behind the main Himalayan range and is Tibetan in everything but administration. The Indus valley around Leh holds Ibisbill on the shingle, the high lakes at Tso Kar and Tso Moriri carry Black-necked Crane breeding at 4,500 m, and Hanle further east is the accessible Tibetan steppe. The altitude is the hazard and acclimatising at Leh is not optional.

  • Look for: Black-necked Crane, Tibetan Sandgrouse, Tibetan Snowcock, Ibisbill, Brown-headed Gull, Horned Lark, Brandt's Mountain Finch, Red-fronted Rosefinch
  • Stops:
    • , the town at 3,500 m with the airport, and where everybody spends two nights before going higher
    • , the settlement on the Tibetan steppe at 4,500 m, with the observatory and the open plains around it
  • Also watch for: Hemis is the Snow Leopard park, watched in winter from the Rumbak valley with local spotters, and it is the reason a whole trade exists here in February. Blue Sheep, Ladakh Urial, Tibetan Wolf and Tibetan Wild Ass are all on the same ground.

Sikkim and the Darjeeling hills

The eastern Himalaya at its steepest, from subtropical valley to alpine pass in fifty kilometres

The eastern Himalaya is wetter, steeper and richer than the west, and Darjeeling and Sikkim are where it is reachable by road. Singalila ridge above Darjeeling holds Satyr Tragopan and Blood Pheasant, the Lava and Neora valley forests carry the babbler and laughingthrush flocks, and the drop from 3,600 m to 300 m happens over one afternoon's drive.

  • Look for: Satyr Tragopan, Blood Pheasant, Fire-tailed Myzornis, Rufous-necked Hornbill, Red-faced Liocichla, Fire-tailed Sunbird, Scarlet Finch, Golden-breasted Fulvetta
  • Stops:
    • , the hill town at 2,000 m, with the Singalila ridge road running north from it
    • , the Sikkim capital, and the base for the state's valleys and passes
  • Also watch for: Red Panda is watched at Singalila and in the Neora valley, which is the most reliable ground for it in India. Himalayan Black Bear and Yellow-throated Marten are regular, and the ridge gives the Kanchenjunga view on a clear morning.

Frequently Asked Questions

When is the best time to bird the Indian Himalaya?

November to April for the foothills and the hill stations, when Ramnagar records 7 mm in November and Ibisbill is on the Corbett shingle. June to September for Ladakh, which sits in the rain shadow and is the only part of the range open then. February for the snow leopards. The three seasons.

How dry is Ladakh really?

Leh computes 103 mm of rain a year, less than a quarter of what Bhuj in the Thar desert takes, and its wettest month records 15 mm. It is behind the main Himalayan range, which takes the monsoon out of the air before it arrives. Darjeeling at the eastern end of the range takes 3,432 mm. The month by month table.

Is the snow leopard watching real?

Yes, and it is the most reliable in the world. Hemis in February puts trackers on ridge lines above the Rumbak valley, where the leopards come low after the blue sheep in the cold, and the sightings are through a scope at distance rather than close. The homestays that host it are paid under a conservancy scheme that compensates villages for livestock losses. Ladakh.

How high is too high?

Leh is 3,500 m and the standard advice, which operators enforce, is two nights there before going higher. Tso Moriri and Hanle are at 4,500 m and the passes cross 5,300 m. Altitude sickness is the real hazard above and it is not negotiable by fitness. Ladakh.

Corbett or the Kumaon hills?

Both, and they pair naturally because Pangot is ninety minutes above Ramnagar. Corbett is the river bed, the grasslands and the big raptors; the hills above are the oak and pine forest with the pheasants and the laughingthrushes. Together they are the standard first Himalayan trip. Corbett.

What does the eastern Himalaya add?

Rain, steepness and species. Darjeeling and Sikkim are wetter and richer than the west, with Satyr Tragopan, Fire-tailed Myzornis and the Red Panda on the Singalila ridge. The drop from 3,600 m to 300 m happens in an afternoon's drive, which is a compression the western hills do not offer. Sikkim and the Darjeeling hills.

Why does this guide carry no certification badges?

Because India has no national scheme. The Ladakh homestays run under a snow leopard conservancy arrangement, which is a real thing and not a certification, and it is named for what it is rather than dressed as a chip. Certification.

How many bird species does India have?

Around 1,350 recorded, and about eighty live nowhere else. India is four guides here because the Himalaya, the plains, the northeast and the Western Ghats share a country rather than an avifauna. The birds.
