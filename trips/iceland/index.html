At a glance

Best months
Late May to July for the seabird cliffs and the Mývatn ducks, July and August for the highlands, September and October for Nearctic vagrants, and February for Gyrfalcon
Getting around
A hire car does the ring road year round. The highlands need a four wheel drive and are shut until the F-roads open, usually between late May and mid July
The one thing to know
Puffins are ashore from late April to late August and the colonies are largely empty by mid September, so the trip is decided by that window before anything else

When to Go

The Icelandic birding year is decided by two things: when the seabirds are ashore, and when the roads are open. Between them they leave a short, extraordinary summer and a long, quiet winter that is a genuinely different trip.

seabirds

Late April to June

The puffins come ashore from late April and the cliffs fill through May, and by June the light barely leaves.

Go for Every seabird colony occupied, the Mývatn ducks on territory, and birding hours that run past midnight.

Trade off The highlands are still shut, and rooms on the ring road book a long way ahead.

Typical daily average Reykjavík 0 to 11 °C / 32 to 52 °F, Akureyri -2 to 13 °C / 28 to 55 °F

highlands

July and August

The F-roads open through late June and July, which is the only window Þjórsárver and the interior are reachable.

Go for The highlands, the Pink-footed Goose grounds, and the last of the seabirds before they go.

Trade off Puffins thin out through August and the colonies are largely empty by the end of it.

Typical daily average Reykjavík 8 to 13 °C / 46 to 55 °F, Akureyri 7 to 14 °C / 45 to 57 °F

vagrants

September to October

The cliffs empty, the pufflings fledge, and the wind starts bringing birds from the wrong continent.

Go for Nearctic vagrants, the Puffling Patrol in Vestmannaeyjar, and a country with its roads to itself.

Trade off Seabird colonies are finished, and the highland roads shut again from late August.

Typical daily average Reykjavík 2 to 10 °C / 36 to 50 °F, Akureyri 0 to 10 °C / 32 to 50 °F

gyrfalcon

November to March

A different country. Gyrfalcon drop to lower ground, sea ducks pack the fjords, and the dark comes back.

Go for Gyrfalcon at their most findable, Harlequin and Barrow’s Goldeneye on unfrozen water, and aurora over the lake.

Trade off Very short days, weather that closes roads without notice, and boat trips on a thin timetable.

Typical daily average Reykjavík -3 to 4 °C / 27 to 39 °F, Akureyri -5 to 3 °C / 23 to 37 °F

Iceland in a Fortnight

Keflavík (KEF), then the full ring, anticlockwise

The whole country at a pace that lets the weather move you. Every region above, and enough slack to sit out a storm.

Lake Mývatn and the northeast

A shallow lake fed by warm springs, holding more breeding duck species than anywhere else in Europe and protected by its own act of parliament

The single most cited site in every itinerary read. More breeding duck species than anywhere else in Europe, sixteen recorded, and the only European breeding population of Barrow's Goldeneye. Gyrfalcon hunts the area. Protected by its own 1974 act and Ramsar listed.

  • Look for: Barrow's Goldeneye, Harlequin Duck, Gyrfalcon, Horned Grebe, Red-necked Phalarope, Long-tailed Duck, Greater Scaup, Common Scoter
  • Also covers: Aðaldalur, Skútustaðir, Vogar
  • Also watch for: Arctic Fox is the only native land mammal and works the lake shore in both colour morphs. Húsavík on the coast is the whale watching town, with Humpback Whale, Minke Whale and White-beaked Dolphin through the summer.
Where to Stay3 places to stay

Two kinds of place, and the difference is worth knowing. Most carry a current certification from a scheme that publishes its own members, taken from the register rather than from the property. The rest carry none, and are here because birding operators actually use them: the guesthouse at Látrabjarg that is the only building at Europe’s largest bird cliff, the two rooms on Vigur among a hundred thousand puffins, the farm on the Mývatn shore. Iceland is thin ground for certification, with thirty Icelandic properties in the supplier archive against three and a half thousand for Spain, so a list of only certified places would have left four regions with nowhere to sleep. The highlands still have nowhere, because there the answer is a mountain hut rather than a hotel. What each certification actually means

The Westfjords and Látrabjarg

Fourteen kilometres of basalt ledges at the westernmost point of the country, the largest bird cliff in Europe

Látrabjarg is Europe's largest bird cliff, fourteen kilometres of basalt ledges at the westernmost point of the country, holding puffin, Razorbill, Common and Brunnich's Guillemot, Black Guillemot, Fulmar and Kittiwake.

  • Look for: Atlantic Puffin, Razorbill, Common Murre, Thick-billed Murre, Black Guillemot, Northern Fulmar, Black-legged Kittiwake
  • Also covers: Breiðavík, Ísafjarðardjúp, Mjóifjörður, Vigur
  • Also watch for: Hornstrandir is an Arctic Fox reserve where the animals have been protected since 1994 and show no fear of people. Harbour Seal and Grey Seal haul out on the Latrabjarg sands below the cliff.

The highlands

The interior desert and its oases, open only once the F-roads clear, holding the world's largest Pink-footed Goose breeding ground

Þjórsárver is the world's largest breeding ground for Pink-footed Goose, six to ten thousand pairs. Reachable only once the F-roads open.

Frequently Asked Questions

When is the best time to go birding in Iceland?

Late May to July, if you want the seabirds. The puffins come ashore from late April, the cliffs are full through May and June, and the Mývatn ducks are on territory at the same time. February is the other answer, and a completely different trip: Gyrfalcon down on lower ground and dark enough skies for aurora.

When exactly are the puffins there?

First arrivals from late April, the main influx in early May, peak nesting from late May to mid August. Most have gone by late August and the colonies are largely empty by mid September. Any trip built around puffins has to sit inside that window.

Where should I go first?

Lake Mývatn. It holds more breeding duck species than anywhere else in Europe, it is the only place in Europe with a breeding population of Barrow’s Goldeneye, Harlequin Duck are on the Laxá outflow, and Gyrfalcon hunt the same ground. Almost every operator itinerary is built around it.

Is Iceland good for a long species list?

No, and that is the point. Only about 75 species breed regularly, because isolation and the near absence of trees exclude most passerines. What Iceland offers instead is density and closeness: a fifth of the world’s Atlantic Puffins in one archipelago, Europe’s largest bird cliff at Látrabjarg, and the whole European Barrow’s Goldeneye population on one lake.

Can I drive into the highlands?

Only in summer, and only in a four wheel drive. The F-roads open in stages as the snow goes, usually between late May and mid July, and start closing again from late August. Þjórsárver, the world’s largest Pink-footed Goose breeding ground, sits inside that window and nowhere else in the year.

Are there rules about drones near the colonies?

Yes, and they matter. Drones are banned in protected areas through the nesting season, broadly 1 May to 15 September, and Látrabjarg, the Mývatn area and Dyrhólaey are restricted year round. Skaftafell is closed to drones entirely for bird protection.

Why does this guide list so little certified accommodation?

Because Iceland has no certified accommodation register that can be read. Vakinn certifies accommodation but its public directory lists neither category nor accommodation. Green Key has five Icelandic entries and two currently awarded. Rather than present a handful as though they were the field, this guide shows what can be verified against a register and says the rest plainly.

What about the autumn rarities?

September and October. Iceland sits in the middle of the North Atlantic, so birds blown off course from North America make landfall here, and the national list is many times longer than the breeding list of roughly seventy-five. Garður on Reykjanes is the trap people watch.
