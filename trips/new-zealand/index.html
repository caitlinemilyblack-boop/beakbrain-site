When to Go

New Zealand runs a southern calendar, so the breeding season is November to February and the quiet months are the northern summer. The country is long enough that a single sentence about weather fails: Hokitika on the west coast takes 2,362 mm a year and Christchurch, forty kilometres away across the Alps, takes 818.

Peak

November to February

Southern summer, and the one window that works for the forest, the summit ridges and the sea at once.

Go for Land birds are feeding young and singing, the alpine passes are clear so rockwren and kea are reachable, the Alaskan godwits are in on the Miranda shellbanks, and the daylight runs past nine in the evening in the south.

Trade off It is the busiest and dearest stretch of the year, and the New Zealand school holidays from late December to early February fill the small towns and the island ferries.

Typical daily average Auckland 15 to 24 °C / 59 to 75 °F, Christchurch 12 to 23 °C / 54 to 73 °F, Invercargill 10 to 20 °C / 50 to 68 °F

Shoulder

October, March and April

The quiet edges of the season, with most of the birding intact.

Go for October opens the alpine roads and starts the breeding, and March and April hold warm settled weather on both coasts with the seabird boats running well and the accommodation half the price.

Trade off April shortens the days quickly in the south, and the Whataroa heron colony closes at the start of March.

Typical daily average Auckland 13 to 21 °C / 55 to 70 °F, Christchurch 9 to 19 °C / 48 to 66 °F, Hokitika 9 to 18 °C / 48 to 64 °F

Winter

May to September

Cheap, empty, and better than its reputation for the birds that matter most at sea.

Go for Albatross numbers at Kaikoura are at their highest, the Westland Petrel is ashore at Punakaiki from March to December, and the forest birds of the North Island stay put all year.

Trade off Snow closes the alpine passes and the summit ridges, the Milford Road runs on chains, and Stewart Island crossings are cancelled more often.

Typical daily average Auckland 7 to 14 °C / 45 to 57 °F, Christchurch 2 to 12 °C / 36 to 54 °F, Invercargill 1 to 10 °C / 34 to 50 °F

Northland and the kauri forest

Warm northern coast and the last big kauri stands, where the North Island Brown Kiwi is common enough to hear from a road

Northland holds the highest density of North Island Brown Kiwi left on the mainland, and Trounson is the place to stand in the dark and listen for one. The kauri forest at Waipoua is the largest remaining, and New Zealand Pigeon, Tui and New Zealand Bellbird work the canopy through it. On the coast, Waipu and Mangawhai hold most of the world's Fairy Tern, which is down to about forty birds.

  • Look for: North Island Brown Kiwi, Fairy Tern, New Zealand Pigeon, Tui, New Zealand Bellbird, Grey Gerygone, North Island Robin, New Zealand Fernbird, Variable Oystercatcher
  • National park:
    • Waipoua Forest, the largest surviving kauri forest and the easiest place to work a mature northern canopy from a road
  • Stops:
    • , a fenced and trapped kauri remnant west of Dargaville where a guided night walk finds kiwi on the track itself, which is the reason it is on this list rather than the forest next door
    • , a sandspit shared by Fairy Tern and New Zealand Dotterel, roped off through the breeding season and watched from behind the line
    • , a small pest-free island in the Kerikeri inlet, run as a kiwi refuge with a causeway walk at dusk
    • , tidal flats close to town holding godwit, knot and both oystercatchers on a rising tide
  • Also watch for: Kauri trees two thousand years old at Waipoua, and the shoes of every visitor are washed at the gate against kauri dieback. Bottlenose Dolphins work the Bay of Islands year round.

Auckland and the Hauraki Gulf

An open-sanctuary island an hour from a city of a million and a half, and a seabird gulf with more breeding species than anywhere comparable

Tiritiri Matangi is the reason a New Zealand trip can start well. The island was cleared of rats in 1993 and replanted by volunteers, and a day walk finds Stitchbird, North Island Saddleback, South Island Takahe and Whitehead without a hide or a wait. The gulf outside it breeds Cook's Petrel, Black Petrel and Buller's Shearwater, and the New Zealand Storm Petrel was found alive here in 2003 after a century with no record.

  • Look for: Stitchbird, North Island Saddleback, South Island Takahe, Whitehead, New Zealand Kaka, Brown Teal, Cook's Petrel, Black Petrel, New Zealand Storm Petrel, Buller's Shearwater, Australasian Gannet, Little Penguin, Spotted Shag
  • National park: Hauraki Gulf Marine Park
  • Stops:
    • , an open sanctuary reached by ferry from Auckland or Gulf Harbour, holding five species that a visitor is unlikely to see anywhere else in a fortnight
    • , a fenced peninsula on the mainland north of Auckland, where the same species set is free to move and Brown Teal sit on the wetland pools
    • , a mainland Australasian Gannet colony on a cliff with a viewing platform above it, occupied from August to March
    • , the departure point for the day boats that work the gulf's petrel water, which is where the storm petrel is looked for
    • , the Cook's Petrel and stitchbird stronghold, closed to casual landing and watched from the sea on the pelagic run
  • Also watch for: Bryde's Whales are resident in the gulf and Common Dolphins are seen on most crossings. Tuatara live wild on Tiritiri Matangi and are found under the boardwalk on a lucky day.

The Firth of Thames

Tidal flats and shellbanks an hour south of Auckland, and the only place a wader with a bill bent sideways is easy

The shellbanks at Pukorokoro Miranda are the shorebird site of the North Island and one of the best in the southern hemisphere. Wrybill winters here in thousands, and it is the only bird in the world whose bill bends sideways, always to the right. Bar-tailed Godwit arrives in September from Alaska on the longest non-stop flight measured in any bird, and the roost fills on a rising spring tide with Red Knot and both oystercatchers alongside.

  • Look for: Wrybill, Bar-tailed Godwit, Red Knot, South Island Oystercatcher, Variable Oystercatcher, Pied Stilt, Black-billed Gull, White-fronted Tern, Royal Spoonbill, Australasian Bittern, New Zealand Plover
  • Stops:
    • , the coast road north of the centre, watched from the car for spoonbill, stilt and the gull flocks
    • , the high tide roost itself, which fills about two hours before the peak and empties as the flats reappear
  • Visitor centres:
    • Pukorokoro Miranda Shorebird Centre, a shorebird centre on the road with hides on the shellbanks behind it, timed to the tide table it publishes rather than to the hour you arrive
  • Also watch for: The Firth is a Ramsar wetland, and the mangrove edge behind the shellbanks is the southern limit of mangrove in the world.

Pureora, Whirinaki and the mountain rivers

Tall podocarp forest on old ash, holding the kokako and the blue duck of the fast water

Pureora and Whirinaki are the tall podocarp forests the North Island has left, and both hold North Island Kokako, a bird whose organ note carries a kilometre through the canopy at dawn. New Zealand Kaka and Yellow-crowned Parakeet work the same trees. On the mountain rivers, Blue Duck holds territory in the whitewater itself, and the Whakapapa and the Manganui o te Ao are where it is looked for.

  • Look for: North Island Kokako, New Zealand Kaka, Yellow-crowned Parakeet, Blue Duck, Whitehead, Rifleman, North Island Robin, Tomtit, New Zealand Falcon, Long-tailed Koel, Morepork
  • National parks:
    • Pureora Forest Park, the kokako forest, saved from logging by a tree occupation in 1978, watched from the road ends at dawn when the birds are singing
    • Tongariro National Park
  • Stops:
    • , the tallest podocarp stand in the country, quieter than Pureora and holding the same species with kaka more reliable
    • , the mountain village and the rivers running off it, which is Blue Duck water and the easiest access to it
    • , the geothermal lakes, with New Zealand Grebe and scaup on the sheltered arms and a kokako project in the forest above Rotoiti
  • Also watch for: Three active volcanoes stand in Tongariro National Park, which was the fourth national park declared anywhere in the world and the first given by an indigenous people.

Hawke's Bay and the Wairarapa

A gannet colony a car can reach, and a breeding centre that put the kokako back into the forest above it

Cape Kidnappers carries the largest mainland Australasian Gannet colony anywhere, and the birds sit within a few metres of the path along the plateau. Inland, Pukaha holds North Island Kokako and North Island Brown Kiwi in a forest that was almost empty of both forty years ago, and it is the place to hear a kokako without a dawn start. Lake Wairarapa below it is the North Island's largest wetland and holds Australasian Bittern.

  • Look for: Australasian Gannet, North Island Kokako, North Island Brown Kiwi, North Island Saddleback, Brown Teal, Australasian Bittern, New Zealand Falcon, Royal Spoonbill, New Zealand Scaup
  • Stops:
    • , the gannet plateau at the end of the headland, reached along the beach at low tide or by tractor trailer, with the birds on the ground either side of the path
    • , shallow open water and raupo edge, watched from the eastern shore for bittern, crake and the duck flocks
    • , a trapped forest block in the Maungaharuru range, the mainland site for kokako and kiwi in Hawke's Bay
  • Visitor centres:
    • Pukaha National Wildlife Centre, a breeding centre with wild forest behind it, where kokako and kiwi were reintroduced and can be heard from the public track
  • Also covers: Te Awanga
  • Also watch for: The Cape Kidnappers headland is inside a predator-fenced station, and the tuatara and giant weta released there are part of the same programme that keeps the seabirds safe.

Wellington and Cook Strait

A fenced valley inside the capital, an island reserve off the coast, and albatross water an hour from the wharf

Zealandia is a valley inside Wellington behind a predator fence, and it is the only place Little Spotted Kiwi is seen on the mainland on a booked night tour. Stitchbird, New Zealand Kaka and South Island Takahe live in the same valley and the kaka now nest across the suburbs outside it. Kapiti Island offshore carries the same set with North Island Saddleback common, and the Cook Strait boats reach albatross water inside an hour.

  • Look for: Little Spotted Kiwi, Stitchbird, North Island Saddleback, South Island Takahe, New Zealand Kaka, Whitehead, Brown Teal, Spotted Shag, Northern Royal Albatross, Salvin's Albatross, Fairy Prion, Common Diving Petrel
  • Stops:
    • , a nature reserve reached by permit boat from Paraparaumu, with saddleback and stitchbird along the tracks and an overnight option for kiwi
    • , the day boats out of the harbour, which reach shelf water and albatross inside an hour and run best on a southerly
    • , a sheltered tidal arm north of the city with a reserve boardwalk, searched for spoonbill, stilt and the small waders
  • Visitor centres:
    • Zealandia, a fenced valley in the city with a night tour that is the mainland's only reliable Little Spotted Kiwi, booked ahead because the groups are small
  • Also watch for: Tuatara breed inside the Zealandia fence, the first time they have bred on the New Zealand mainland in perhaps two hundred years.

Nelson, Marlborough and Kaikoura

A canyon a kilometre deep within sight of the shore, which puts more albatross species beside one boat than anywhere on earth

The Kaikoura canyon comes within a few kilometres of the beach, and the seabird boats reach it in twenty minutes. A single trip regularly puts Northern Royal Albatross, Salvin's Albatross, Buller's Albatross and Shy Albatross beside the hull at once, with Hutton's Shearwater passing in thousands to a breeding colony that exists only in the mountains behind the town. In the Marlborough Sounds, New Zealand King Shag holds to a handful of rock stacks and lives nowhere else.

  • Look for: Northern Royal Albatross, Salvin's Albatross, Buller's Albatross, Shy Albatross, Hutton's Shearwater, Westland Petrel, Northern Giant Petrel, Pintado Petrel, New Zealand King Shag, South Island Robin, South Island Saddleback, Great Spotted Kiwi, Kea, Bar-tailed Godwit
  • National parks: Nelson Lakes National Park, Abel Tasman National Park
  • Stops:
    • , the seabird boats from the town wharf, reaching canyon water in twenty minutes, which is why a half-day trip here beats a full day almost anywhere else
    • , a thirty kilometre sandspit closed beyond the first few kilometres, with the accessible end holding godwit and knot and a licensed tour running the rest
    • , beech forest around Rotoiti under a long running trapping programme, holding robin, kaka and Great Spotted Kiwi on the summit ridges
    • , the King Shag water, watched from the Picton mail boat or a charter, because the stacks the birds use are the only ones they use
    • , a pest-free island in the outer sound with saddleback and robin on a short loop track, reached on the same boats
  • Also watch for: Sperm Whales are resident off Kaikoura all year and Dusky Dolphins move through in schools of hundreds. New Zealand Fur Seals haul out on the peninsula itself.

The West Coast and Arthur's Pass

Rain forest against the Alps, a kiwi with a population in the hundreds, and the only petrel in the world that breeds on a mainland

Okarito Kiwi lives in one forest and numbers a few hundred birds, and the night tour out of Okarito is the way to hear one. Westland Petrel breeds in the hills behind Punakaiki and comes ashore after dark between March and December, which makes it the only petrel in the world with a mainland colony. Up at Arthur's Pass, Kea works the car park and New Zealand Rockwren holds the scree above the bushline.

  • Look for: Okarito Kiwi, Westland Petrel, Kea, New Zealand Rockwren, Great Spotted Kiwi, Blue Duck, Weka, South Island Robin, Rifleman, Pipipi, Great Egret, Tomtit
  • National parks: Arthur's Pass National Park, Paparoa National Park, Westland Tai Poutini National Park
  • Stops:
    • , a lagoon village with the kiwi forest behind it, where the night tour listens on forestry roads and the day is spent on the lagoon
    • , the Waitangiroto nesting colony, reached only on the licensed jet boat and hide walk, open from November to February
    • , the pancake rocks and the Westland Petrel hills behind them, with the birds coming in over the road after dark in the winter half of the year
    • , the alpine village on the main divide, where kea are on the roofs and the rockwren scree is a walk above it
    • , a lowland loop through kahikatea forest under Aoraki, holding rifleman, tomtit and Great Egret at the outlet
  • Also watch for: The only mainland breeding colony of the Kotuku, the Great Egret, sits on the Waitangiroto near Whataroa and holds fewer than two hundred birds in the whole country.

Canterbury and the Mackenzie

Grey braided rivers off the Alps, holding four birds that breed on shingle and nowhere else

The braided rivers of Canterbury are a habitat with almost no equivalent, and four birds depend on them. Black Stilt is the rarest wading bird in the world with about forty breeding pairs in the wild, all in the Mackenzie, and the hide at Twizel is where it is seen. Wrybill breeds on the same shingle before moving north for winter, alongside Black-fronted Tern and Black-billed Gull. Te Waihora on the coast is the South Island's largest wetland.

  • Look for: Black Stilt, Wrybill, Black-fronted Tern, Black-billed Gull, Double-banded Plover, South Island Oystercatcher, Australasian Bittern, New Zealand Grebe, Kea, New Zealand Falcon, Great Crested Grebe
  • National park: Aoraki Mount Cook National Park
  • Stops:
    • , the Black Stilt release site and its public hide, which is the only place in the world the bird can be counted on
    • , a huge shallow lagoon behind a shingle bar, with bittern in the raupo, the New Zealand bulrush, and the largest wader roosts in the South Island
    • , a braided rivermouth north of Christchurch under a long running river care group, holding wrybill and both terns in the breeding season
    • , a wide shingle mouth with black-billed gull colonies and a lagoon behind it
    • , the alpine end of the basin, where kea come to the car parks and the valley track climbs into rockwren country
    • , a braided river running from the Mackenzie lakes to the sea, holding wrybill and black-fronted tern on its shingle doc.govt.nz
  • Also watch for: The Mackenzie Basin is a dark sky reserve, and the same dry tussock that keeps the sky clear is what the rivers run through.

Otago and the Catlins

The only mainland albatross colony on earth, and a penguin that nests in forest and avoids its own kind

Taiaroa Head at the end of the Otago Peninsula is the only mainland breeding colony of any albatross anywhere, and about forty pairs of Northern Royal Albatross nest within sight of an observatory window. Yellow-eyed Penguin comes ashore on the beaches along the same peninsula and down into the Catlins, nesting in scrub and forest and out of sight of other pairs, which is unusual for a penguin and is why the viewing is done from hides.

  • Look for: Northern Royal Albatross, Yellow-eyed Penguin, Little Penguin, Stewart Island Shag, Spotted Shag, South Island Robin, South Island Saddleback, New Zealand Fernbird, Variable Oystercatcher, Buller's Albatross, Royal Spoonbill
  • Stops:
    • , the albatross colony at the peninsula tip, watched from a booked observatory session, with the birds best on a windy afternoon
    • , the road out to the head, with harbour shags, spoonbill and the penguin beaches along it
    • , a fenced valley north of the city holding saddleback, robin and takahe, and the South Island's answer to Zealandia
    • , a lighthouse headland in the Catlins with a hide below it for Yellow-eyed Penguin and fur seals on the rocks
  • Also watch for: New Zealand Sea Lions breed on the Otago beaches, the only place on the mainland they do, and Hooker's population here has come back from a single female in the 1990s.

Fiordland and the Milford Road

The valley a bird written off for fifty years came back from, and a road with rockwren on the scree above it

South Island Takahe was written off as extinct for fifty years and found alive in the Murchison Mountains above Lake Te Anau in 1948. The mountains themselves stay closed, and the lakeside sanctuary in town holds birds from the same programme. The Milford Road climbs to the Homer tunnel, where Kea work the queue of cars and New Zealand Rockwren holds the scree above the portal, and Blue Duck sits in the Cleddau below.

  • Look for: South Island Takahe, Kea, New Zealand Rockwren, Blue Duck, Rifleman, Pipipi, South Island Robin, Yellowhead, Fiordland Penguin, New Zealand Falcon, Weka
  • National park: Fiordland National Park
  • Stops:
    • , a lakeside aviary holding takahe from the Murchison programme, and the practical way to see the bird without a helicopter
    • , the road over the Homer saddle, with kea at the tunnel, rockwren on the scree above it and Blue Duck on the Cleddau
    • , the quieter fiord, reached by two boats and a bus over Wilmot Pass, with Fiordland Penguin on the outer coast from July to November
  • Also watch for: Bottlenose Dolphins are resident in Doubtful Sound, and the fiord walls carry black coral within ten metres of the surface because the freshwater layer on top keeps the light out.

Southland and Stewart Island

An island where kiwi feed on a beach in daylight, and a forest walk with four hard birds on one loop

Ulva Island is the single best forest walk in the country: South Island Saddleback, Yellowhead, South Island Robin and Weka are all on a short loop and none of them moves away. The Southern Brown Kiwi on Stewart Island feeds in daylight on the beach wrack, which no other kiwi does, and the dusk trips out of Oban are the reason people fly down. Sooty Shearwater breeds in millions on the islands offshore.

  • Look for: Southern Brown Kiwi, South Island Saddleback, Yellowhead, South Island Robin, Weka, Sooty Shearwater, New Zealand Kaka, New Zealand Fernbird, Fiordland Penguin, Stewart Island Shag, Variable Oystercatcher
  • National park: Rakiura National Park
  • Stops:
    • , a pest-free island in Paterson Inlet reached by water taxi from Oban, with four species on one loop track and none of them shy
    • , the island's only town and the base for the beach kiwi trips, which run at dusk and need a spare evening for weather
    • , tidal flats and bog east of Invercargill, holding fernbird in the wire rush and waders on the flats
    • , the ferry port and the headland above it, searched for shearwater and mollymawk passage on an onshore wind
  • Also watch for: Titi, the Sooty Shearwater, is still harvested on the islands off Stewart Island under Rakiura Maori right, which is the only customary seabird harvest left in the country.

Frequently Asked Questions

When is the best time to go birding in New Zealand?

November to February. Land birds are breeding and singing, the alpine passes are open so Kea and New Zealand Rockwren are reachable, and the Bar-tailed Godwit flocks are in on the Firth of Thames. October and March are cheaper and almost as good. When to go, season by season.

How long do I need?

Nine days covers the North Island and twelve the South. Three weeks covers both with the accessible endemics complete, which is why the international operators sell it at three weeks. Four days out of Auckland still delivers Tiritiri Matangi and the Miranda shorebird roost.

Do I have to hire a car?

Yes, for everything outside Auckland and Wellington. The sites are rural road ends and river mouths, the intercity buses reach towns rather than reserves, and the two islands are joined by a vehicle ferry between Wellington and Picton that carries a hire car for a fee.

Where do I actually see a kiwi?

Three places, and each is a different bird. Zealandia in Wellington runs a night tour for Little Spotted Kiwi. Trounson in Northland and Aroha Island near Kerikeri both have guided walks for North Island Brown Kiwi. Stewart Island is the one place a kiwi feeds on a beach in daylight, and that is Southern Brown Kiwi. Every one of them is booked ahead.

What makes Kaikoura worth a whole day?

A submarine canyon a kilometre deep comes within a few kilometres of the beach, so the boats reach deep water in twenty minutes. Four albatross species behind one hull on one trip is ordinary there, and Hutton's Shearwater breeds only in the mountains directly behind the town.

Are the sanctuary islands worth the ferry fare?

Tiritiri Matangi answers that on its own. Stitchbird, North Island Saddleback, South Island Takahe and Whitehead are all findable in one walk, all four are hard or impossible on unfenced mainland, and the island charges a landing fee that funds the trapping keeping them there. Ulva Island in the far south does the same for the South Island species.

Can I visit the subantarctic islands or the Chathams?

The subantarctic islands are reached by expedition cruise from Bluff or Dunedin, usually in November and December, and four of the fifteen operators selling New Zealand run one. Landings on Campbell and the Auckland Islands need a DOC permit that the ship holds. The Chatham Islands are a two hour flight east of Christchurch and are a separate trip with a separate bird list, Black Robin and Shore Plover among them.

Why do the regions above carry no certification badges?

New Zealand runs Qualmark, a tourism grading scheme with a sustainability tier, and its register publishes no machine-readable list a claim can be checked against. Every chip on a BeakBrain page comes from a scheme's own register, so the regions above carry the reserves, the sanctuaries and the guiding instead.
