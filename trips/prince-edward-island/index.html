Birds that decide the trip

Piping Plover on the north shore sand

The park beaches hold one of the higher nesting densities in the country, on open sand a visitor can reach from a car park, which is why the roped sections are so large.

When May to August, on roped beach sections.

Where The national park dunes

Barrow's Goldeneye off the north shore

Burrows recorded 277 of them on a single national park Christmas count, which was the whole Atlantic region's total that year.

When December to March, in harbours and river mouths.

Where The national park dunes, The Hillsborough and Charlottetown

Common Tern on the barrier spits

Colonies sit on the sand spits at the mouths of the bays, and the birds fish the lagoons behind them all summer.

When June and July, on the spits.

Where The national park dunes, Malpeque and the north-west

Great Blue Heron on the oyster bays

Colonies nest in the spruce around Malpeque and Cascumpec, and the birds feed on the eelgrass flats below them.

When April to October, on the bay flats.

Where Malpeque and the north-west, The Hillsborough and Charlottetown

Northern Gannet off East Point

An onshore autumn wind brings the birds within sight of the lighthouse, where they feed over the tide race between the gulf and strait waters.

When October and November, on an onshore wind.

Where East Point and the eastern end, North Cape and the western shore

Purple Sandpiper on the point rock

It winters on wave-washed rock and almost nowhere else, so the reef at North Cape and the cobble at East Point are where to look.

When November to March, on exposed rock.

Where North Cape and the western shore, East Point and the eastern end

Nelson's Sparrow in the dune marsh

The salt marsh behind the barrier beaches holds breeding birds. The song is a thin hiss at dusk that carries no further than a few metres.

When June and July, in salt marsh at dusk.

Where The national park dunes

Hudsonian Whimbrel on the eelgrass flats

It works the exposed eelgrass and mud of the north-western bays on the way south, in small parties rather than the big flocks the smaller waders form.

When August, on a falling tide.

Where Malpeque and the north-west

When to Go

Prince Edward Island is small and low and almost entirely coast, so the birding year on it runs with the water. Terns and plovers nest on the dunes through the summer, the bays fill with waders in August, and sea ducks hold off the points all winter.

The nesting beaches

June and July

Piping Plovers and Common Terns nest on the north shore sand behind rope.

Go for Prince Edward Island National Park, from Cavendish to Greenwich.

Trade off The park beaches are at their busiest and the closed sections are large.

Typical daily average

The bay passage

August and September

Malpeque, Cascumpec and the Hillsborough estuary fill with southbound waders.

Go for The north-western bays and the Hillsborough River.

Trade off Access to the best flats is often across private farmland.

Typical daily average

The autumn sea passage

October and November

The eastern and western points collect scoters, gannets and the odd storm-driven seabird.

Go for East Point and North Cape, on an onshore wind.

Trade off Cold, exposed and often wet, with no shelter at either point.

Typical daily average

The winter ducks

December to March

Barrow's Goldeneye winters here, and Burrows recorded 277 of them on one national park count.

Go for The harbours and river mouths on the north shore.

Trade off Ice closes the inner bays and much of the island's tourist infrastructure is shut.

The national park dunes

Red sandstone dunes and barrier beach, with the island's nesting plovers and terns on them

The north shore is a long run of red sandstone cliff, dune and barrier beach, and the national park protects the best of it in three separate blocks. Piping Plovers nest on the open sand and Common Terns on the spits, and the Greenwich block at the eastern end has a parabolic dune system rare on this coast.

  • Habitat: Red sandstone cliff and barrier dune, marram grassland, salt lagoon and freshwater pond, and spruce woodland behind.
  • When: June and July for the nesting birds, and September for the passage waders on the same beaches.
  • Getting there: Charlottetown is the airport, forty minutes south. The park charges a day fee in summer, the Greenwich dune trail runs on a floating boardwalk, and the plover beaches are roped and wardened.
  • Base yourself in: Cavendish
  • Look for: Piping Plover, Common Tern, Arctic Tern, Willet, Savannah Sparrow, Nelson's Sparrow, Osprey, Bald Eagle, Red-breasted Merganser, Great Blue Heron, Sanderling, Ruddy Turnstone
  • Also covers: Brackley Beach, Cavendish, Greenwich, Prince Edward Island National Park
  • Also watch for: The Greenwich dunes migrate inland over a freshwater pond system and bury the forest behind them, which is a landform found in only a handful of places on this coast.

Malpeque and the north-west

Shallow barrier-built bays that hold the island's waders and its wintering waterfowl

The north-western coast is a chain of shallow bays behind sand barriers, fed by eelgrass beds and mud. Malpeque is the largest, and the flats around it and Cascumpec take the island's southbound wader passage in August. Great Blue Herons nest in the woods around both.

  • Habitat: Shallow barrier-built bay, eelgrass bed and mudflat, salt marsh, sand barrier and spruce woodland.
  • When: August and early September for the wader passage, on a falling tide when the flats open.
  • Getting there: Charlottetown is the airport, an hour east. Most of the shore is farmland and the good vantage points are on side roads, so ask before crossing a field.
  • Base yourself in: Tyne Valley
  • Look for: Semipalmated Sandpiper, Least Sandpiper, Short-billed Dowitcher, Greater Yellowlegs, Lesser Yellowlegs, Grey Plover, Hudsonian Whimbrel, Great Blue Heron, Northern Harrier, American Black Duck, Green-winged Teal, Hooded Merganser
  • Also covers: Bideford, Cascumpec Bay, Malpeque Bay, Tyne Valley
  • Also watch for: Malpeque Bay is a working oyster bay, and the eelgrass beds that feed the waders are the same ones the fishery depends on.

East Point and the eastern end

The island's eastern tip, where an onshore autumn wind pushes seabirds within sight of land

East Point is the far end of the island, and the one place on it where a watcher can sit and let the sea come past. On an onshore wind in October the scoter flocks, gannets and jaegers pass close, and the harbour at Souris behind it holds gulls and sea ducks through the winter.

  • Habitat: Rock and cobble point, sandstone cliff, harbour and river mouth, sand beach, and open gulf water.
  • When: October and November on an onshore wind, and January and February for the sea ducks and white-winged gulls.
  • Getting there: Charlottetown is the airport, ninety minutes west. The lighthouse at East Point has a car park at the tip, and there is no shelter at all, so dress for it.
  • Base yourself in: Souris
  • Look for: Northern Gannet, Black Scoter, Surf Scoter, White-winged Scoter, Long-tailed Duck, Red-throated Loon, Common Loon, Parasitic Jaeger, Iceland Gull, Glaucous Gull, Purple Sandpiper, Harlequin Duck
  • Also covers: Basin Head, East Point, Souris, St Peters Bay
  • Also watch for: The tide races off the point where the gulf and strait waters meet, and the rip is what holds the feeding birds close in.

The Hillsborough and Charlottetown

A long tidal river that reaches the middle of the island and carries its best winter list

The Hillsborough is a tidal river reaching well inland, with mudflat, salt marsh and wooded bank along it. The Christmas counts Burrows collated repeatedly put Hillsborough at the top of the island's totals, with 43 species on one of them including late herons and three sparrow species.

  • Habitat: Tidal river and mudflat, salt marsh, freshwater impoundment, mixed woodland and farmland.
  • When: December and January for the wintering birds, and May and June for the breeding marsh birds.
  • Getting there: Charlottetown airport is fifteen minutes from the river. The Confederation Trail is a level rail trail with parking at every village along it, and it is free.
  • Base yourself in: Charlottetown
  • Look for: Great Blue Heron, Belted Kingfisher, Fox Sparrow, White-throated Sparrow, Savannah Sparrow, Pine Siskin, Wood Duck, Ruddy Duck, Pied-billed Grebe, American Bittern, Sora, Virginia Rail
  • Also covers: Brudenell, Charlottetown, Hillsborough River, Mount Stewart
  • Also watch for: The river was the island's main transport route before the roads, and the Confederation Trail follows the old rail line along much of its length.

North Cape and the western shore

A long reef running out from the island's north-western tip into open water

North Cape is the island's other point, with a rock reef that runs out from it and is exposed at low tide. The reef and the water off it hold seabirds and sea ducks, and the western shore behind is a quiet run of sandstone cliff and fishing harbours.

  • Habitat: Rock reef and cobble point, red sandstone cliff, a working harbour, sand beach, and farmland inland of all of it.
  • When: October and November for the passage, and May for the northbound sea ducks along the same shore.
  • Getting there: Charlottetown is the airport, two hours south-east. There is a car park and an interpretive centre at the cape, and the reef is walkable at low tide with care.
  • Base yourself in: Alberton
  • Look for: Common Eider, Long-tailed Duck, Black Scoter, Red-breasted Merganser, Great Cormorant, Double-crested Cormorant, Northern Gannet, Bald Eagle, Ruddy Turnstone, Sanderling, Purple Sandpiper, Semipalmated Plover
  • Also covers: Alberton, Cape Wolfe, North Cape, West Point
  • Also watch for: The reef off the cape is the longest natural rock reef on this coast and carries a wind farm along the headland above it.

Frequently Asked Questions

When is the best time to go birding in Prince Edward Island?

June and July if the nesting beaches are the point, when the plovers and terns are on the north shore sand. August is better for numbers, with the southbound wader passage through the bays, and October brings the seabird movement past the two points.

Where do I see Piping Plovers without disturbing them?

From the marked path above the beach, in the national park. Nesting sections are roped and wardened through the summer, and the birds nest on open sand rather than in cover, so walking the beach where they are is what does the damage.

Is the island worth a winter visit?

For ducks, yes. Barrow's Goldeneye winters here in numbers unusual for the region, along with scoters, Long-tailed Ducks and white-winged gulls in the harbours. The Hillsborough has topped the island's Christmas counts since the 1970s.

How long do I need?

Four days covers it. The island is 224 km end to end and no corner of it is more than ninety minutes from Charlottetown, so a single base works and the driving is never the constraint.

How do I get there?

Fly to Charlottetown, drive the Confederation Bridge from New Brunswick, or take the seasonal ferry from Nova Scotia. The bridge is 13 km long and is tolled when you leave Prince Edward Island rather than when you arrive.

Can I bird here with limited mobility?

Sites on this page differ from each other more than any rating could carry, so this is the framework the standard British guides use, and the reserve's own page and warden hold the answers.

  • Permission · some reserves open ground to disabled visitors that is closed to everyone else, and it is granted by asking.
  • Priority · parking bays, hides and the nearest paths are often held for disabled visitors. Ask what is reserved and how to claim it.
  • Parking · how close a car reaches the birds shapes the whole day. Ring ahead for the nearest hard standing to the water or the hide.
  • Paths · surfaces, gradients and gate widths vary inside a single reserve. Ask which trail is boarded, surfaced or level.
  • Perches · a hide with a low window, or a bench where the view opens, turns a viewpoint into a morning.

Every reserve above with its own link carries its access detail there, and a warden answers what a page cannot.
