When to Go

The Dutch calendar is set by migration rather than by rain. Spring fills the meadows and reedbeds, autumn moves hundreds of thousands of waders through the Wadden Sea, and winter brings the geese.

Spring

March to June

Meadow birds display, reedbeds fill and every wood is singing before six in the morning.

Go for Come for breeding birds, the shortest nights of the year and the fullest song.

Trade off School holidays and the bulb fields make the coast busy, and accommodation is at its most expensive in April and May.

Autumn

July to October

Wader passage builds through the Wadden Sea and the coast carries anything moving south.

Go for Come for numbers on the tidal flats and for the chance of a scarce migrant on the islands.

Trade off Song has stopped, so identification leans on plumage and call rather than on a bird announcing itself.

Winter

November to February

Geese cover the grassland in six figures and diving duck gather on the IJsselmeer.

Go for Come for the largest concentrations of birds the country holds all year, and the cheapest rooms.

Trade off Eight hours of daylight, frequent grey overcast and a wind off the North Sea with nothing to stop it.

The Full Length of the Country

Amsterdam, the dunes, Zeeland, the Biesbosch, Limburg, the Veluwe, Flevoland, the north

Driving: about 16 hours behind the wheel across the whole route, roughly 1,150 km.

Twelve days takes in the delta and the southern hills as well as the north, which no shorter trip manages.

The Wadden Islands

Dune, saltmarsh and tidal flat, two to three hours from Amsterdam plus a ferry

Five inhabited islands sit along the top of the country, each reached by its own ferry. Texel is the easiest first visit and the others get quieter as you go east. The tide sets the day here, and high water pushes waders onto roosts you can watch from a hide.

Look for: Eurasian Spoonbill, Pied Avocet, Bar-tailed Godwit, Red Knot

Also covers: De Cocksdorp Texel, Midsland, Oosterend, West-Terschelling

Also watch for: Harbour and grey seals haul out on the sandbanks and are visible from the boat trips that run from every island. Harbour seal pups arrive in June and July, grey seal pups from November into January.

The Wadden Coast and Lauwersmeer

Freshwater lake, reedbed and open coast, two hours from Amsterdam

Lauwersmeer was cut off from the sea in 1969 and became a national park of reed, open water and rough grazing. The reedbed singers are on territory from the middle of April, and the surrounding coast is where the mudflat birds go once the tide covers the flats.

Look for: Bluethroat, Montagu's Harrier, Common Crane, Eurasian Spoonbill

Also watch for: Konik horses and Highland cattle graze the reserve and keep the grassland open, and roe deer are regular at the edges. Otter has returned to the waterways and is occasionally seen at first light.

Groningen and Drenthe

Heath, bog and old woodland, two hours from Amsterdam

The two northern provinces hold the country's driest heath and its oldest peat bog at Dwingelderveld. Old pine and oak carry the woodpeckers, the open heath churrs after dusk from late May, and the heather is at its best from late July into September.

Look for: Black Woodpecker, Woodlark, European Nightjar, Red-backed Shrike

Also watch for: Red deer, badger and roe deer live across the heath and old woodland, and adders bask on the sandy tracks at Dwingelderveld from April to September. Sheep flocks still graze the heather by arrangement.

The Frisian Lakes and Meadow-Bird Country

Wet grassland and shallow lakes, ninety minutes from Amsterdam

Friesland holds the largest share of the country's breeding Black-tailed Godwit, a bird the Netherlands carries much of the world population of. The wet meadows are managed for them, and April and May are when the display flights are constant.

Look for: Black-tailed Godwit, Northern Lapwing, Ruff, Eurasian Oystercatcher

Also watch for: Otter has recovered across the Frisian waterways since reintroduction, and brown hares box in the open fields through March and April. Roe deer work the ditches between the wet meadows.

The IJsselmeer and the Afsluitdijk

Freshwater sea and dyke, one hour from Amsterdam

The Zuiderzee became a freshwater lake when the Afsluitdijk closed in 1932, and it is now the country's largest body of fresh water. Winter brings diving duck in numbers, and the dyke itself is a straight thirty two kilometre watchpoint.

Look for: Smew, Great Cormorant, Eurasian Wigeon, Common Tern

Also watch for: The lake carries a large eel population and the fish passage at the Afsluitdijk was built for it. Harbour porpoise turn up on the seaward side of the dyke, most often in winter.

Flevoland and the Oostvaardersplassen

Reclaimed polder, reedbed and new islands, forty minutes from Amsterdam

Oostvaardersplassen grew on land drained in 1968 and became one of the most watched wetlands in Europe. White-tailed Eagle first bred here in 2006. Marker Wadden, the built archipelago a short boat ride north, adds bare sand and shell for terns and waders.

Look for: White-tailed Eagle, Great Egret, Eurasian Bittern, Bearded Reedling

Also watch for: Konik horses, red deer and Heck cattle graze the reserve as large herbivores, which is what keeps the reedbed and grassland in balance. The red deer rut runs through September and October.

The Veluwe

Heath, drift sand and pine and oak forest, one hour from Amsterdam

The largest area of continuous woodland and heath in the country, and the easiest place to work through the woodpeckers. Middle Spotted Woodpecker has spread here since the 1990s. Early mornings from April are when the forest is loudest.

Look for: Middle Spotted Woodpecker, Black Woodpecker, Woodlark, Common Redstart

Also covers: Otterlo

Also watch for: Red deer, wild boar, roe deer and mouflon all live here, and the reserve runs marked wildlife viewing points for them. The red deer rut in September and October is the loudest time of the year.

The North Holland Dunes

Dune, scrub and dune lake, thirty minutes from Amsterdam

A continuous dune belt runs north from Haarlem, close enough to the city for a morning before a flight. Scrub and dune slack hold breeding warblers and Common Redstart, and the coast is a migration line for anything moving along the North Sea.

Look for: Northern Wheatear, Common Redstart, European Stonechat, Eurasian Skylark

Also covers: Noordwijk aan zee

Also watch for: European bison graze the Kraansvlak enclosure at Zuid-Kennemerland, one of the few free-ranging herds in western Europe, alongside fallow deer and Konik horses. Rabbits keep the dune grassland short.

The South Holland Dunes

Dune, wooded valley and estate, forty minutes from Amsterdam

Meijendel and the Wassenaar estates hold older, wetter dune woodland than the north, which brings a different list within reach of The Hague. Terns pass offshore all summer, and the wooded parts are worth walking slowly in May.

Look for: Sandwich Tern, Common Hoopoe, Common Redstart, Icterine Warbler

Also watch for: Fallow deer and roe deer are common through Meijendel and the Wassenaar estates, and foxes hunt the dune slacks in daylight. The dune ponds hold a strong amphibian list in spring.

The Biesbosch and the Great Rivers

Tidal freshwater willow forest and river floodplain, one hour from Amsterdam

The Biesbosch is one of the last tidal freshwater wetlands in Europe, best seen from a boat among the willow. Water levels still rise and fall twice a day. East along the Rhine and Waal, reclaimed floodplain has brought herons and storks back to the river bends.

Look for: White-tailed Eagle, Purple Heron, Common Kingfisher, Great Egret

Also watch for: Beaver was reintroduced here in 1988 and the lodges and gnawed willow are easy to find from a boat. Otter and roe deer both use the same creeks, and dusk is when the beavers move.

The Zeeland Delta

Tidal inlet, saltmarsh and shell bank, two hours from Amsterdam

The delta works left a chain of inlets, some tidal and some closed, each with its own birds. The Oosterschelde still fills and empties twice a day and holds waders through the winter. Bare shell banks carry the scarcest of the breeding plovers.

Look for: Kentish Plover, Pied Avocet, Eurasian Curlew, Dunlin

Also covers: GOES

Also watch for: Harbour and grey seals rest on the banks inside the Oosterschelde and are visible from the shore at low water. Harbour porpoise are regular in the tidal channels through the winter.

Limburg and the Southern Hills

Chalk grassland, hedgerow and beech wood, two hours from Amsterdam

The far south rises into chalk country that belongs more to the Ardennes than to the rest of the Netherlands. Hedged fields and beech wood hold birds found nowhere else in the country, and the walking is the only hill walking there is here.

Look for: Red-backed Shrike, Middle Spotted Woodpecker, Common Redstart, Eurasian Skylark

Also watch for: Badger and roe deer work the hedged fields, and wildcat has returned to the woods along the Belgian and German border. Fire salamanders emerge on wet nights from April to October.

Frequently Asked Questions

When is the best time to go birding in the Netherlands?

April and May for breeding birds and full song, September and October for wader passage through the Wadden Sea, and December to February for geese in six figures. There is no month with nothing in it.

Do I need a car?

It helps, and it is the only way to work a region properly in a morning. The train reaches Amsterdam, Leeuwarden, Groningen, Arnhem and Maastricht, and the island ferries connect to buses, so a car free trip to Texel or Schiermonnikoog works well.

How much does a birding trip here cost?

Accommodation is the largest line. Rate bands on this page run from about $100 a night in the mid range to $500 and above at the top, and winter is consistently the cheapest season.

Do I need to book the island ferries?

For a foot passenger, no. For a car in summer or at a weekend, yes, and well ahead for Texel.

Are the reserves free to enter?

Most are open and free to walk. Some hides and boat trips are ticketed, and a few breeding areas are closed in season, which is marked on the ground.

How does the tide affect a Wadden Sea visit?

It decides the day. Birds feed spread across the flats at low water and gather on roosts as the tide covers them, so check a tide table before choosing a hide.

What should I bring?

A telescope earns its weight here more than in most countries, because so much of the watching is across open water and mudflat. Wind protection matters more than waterproofing.

Is English enough?

Yes. English is widely spoken, and most reserve signage and much of the online reserve information carries an English version.

Why are there no day tours listed?

The marketplace research that fills that section on other country pages has not been run for the Netherlands yet. Listing operators without checking that each sells a priced birding tour would be filling a section rather than answering a question.
