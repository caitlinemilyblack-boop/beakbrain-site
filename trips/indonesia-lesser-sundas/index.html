At a glance

Best months
June to October, with August the driest month recorded anywhere in the chain
Regions
Komodo, Rinca and Labuan BajoWestern Flores: Puarlolo and the Mbeliling hillsCentral Flores: Kelimutu and the Ruteng highlandsSumbaWest Timor: Bipolo, Camplong and KupangGunung Mutis and the Timor highlandsRote and SemauLombok and SumbawaAlor, Wetar and the eastern arc
Getting in
Labuan Bajo (LBJ) for Komodo and western Flores, Ende (ENE) for the eastern half, Waingapu (WGP) for Sumba, Kupang (KOE) for Timor and Rote, and Lombok (LOP) for the western end
Getting around
Short domestic hops between the island airports, a hired car and driver on Flores, Sumba and Timor, and a fast ferry for Rote

When to Go

The Lesser Sundas run on the Australian monsoon rather than the equatorial one, which makes them the most seasonal islands in Indonesia and the easiest to plan. The wet arrives in December and is gone by April; from June to October most of the chain records almost no rain at all. The birding is done in the dry, which is also when the tracks into the forest blocks are passable.

Peak

June to October

The dry season, and there is no argument about it on these islands.

Go for Kupang records 2 mm in August against 392 mm in January, the forest tracks on Sumba and Flores are all passable, and the sea crossings between islands are at their calmest.

Trade off It is also when every operator runs, so guides on Sumba and Flores are booked well ahead, and the Timor lowlands are genuinely hot at 31 C.

Typical daily average Kupang 21 to 31 °C / 70 to 88 °F, Ruteng 15 to 24 °C / 59 to 75 °F, Waingapu 21 to 30 °C / 70 to 86 °F

Shoulder

April, May and November

The edges of the dry, when the islands are green and mostly workable.

Go for The drier islands come good from April and stay reasonable into November, with far less pressure on guides and rooms, and the landscape still has some green in it.

Trade off The Flores highlands hold their rain longest, so Ruteng and Kelimutu are the parts most likely to be washed out at either end.

Typical daily average Kupang 22 to 32 °C / 72 to 90 °F, Ruteng 15 to 25 °C / 59 to 77 °F, Waingapu 22 to 31 °C / 72 to 88 °F

Wet

December to March

The Australian monsoon, and the half of the year the itineraries avoid.

Go for The islands are green, the birds are breeding and singing, and Sumba's Pasola, the ritual horseback contest, falls in February and March.

Trade off Ruteng takes 536 mm in January, the forest tracks close, and the inter-island ferries run to a reduced timetable in rough water.

Typical daily average Kupang 23 to 31 °C / 73 to 88 °F, Ruteng 16 to 24 °C / 61 to 75 °F, Waingapu 23 to 31 °C / 73 to 88 °F

Komodo, Rinca and Labuan Bajo

Dry savanna islands off western Flores, birded from a boat between the dragon walks

Komodo is on almost every Lesser Sundas itinerary and it is genuinely good birding as well as the obvious reason. The islands are dry savanna and monsoon forest rather than rainforest, and the walks the rangers run for the dragons cross exactly the habitat that holds Yellow-crested Cockatoo, a bird the cage trade has taken out of most of its range and which survives here in numbers. Green Junglefowl crosses the trails and Orange-footed Scrubfowl works its mounds under the tamarinds.

  • Look for: Yellow-crested Cockatoo, Green Junglefowl, Orange-footed Scrubfowl, Elegant Pitta, Flame-breasted Sunbird, Wallacean Drongo, Island Monarch, Beach Stone-curlew
  • National park: Komodo National Park
  • Stops:
    • , the port town on western Flores with the airport and every boat, and the base for the whole island group
    • , the larger island, with the ranger station at Loh Liang and the savanna walks behind it
    • , the nearer island, drier and more open, usually the shorter of the two dragon walks
  • Also watch for: The Komodo Dragon is the point for most visitors, and the ranger walks on both Komodo and Rinca are the way it is done. Timor Deer are its prey and are everywhere on the savanna, and the water between the islands holds Reef Manta Ray at Manta Point.

Western Flores: Puarlolo and the Mbeliling hills

Hill forest an hour inland from the ferry port, holding two birds confined to this end of the island

The hills behind Labuan Bajo are where the western Flores endemics live, and the roadside forest at Puarlolo is the site every operator uses. Flores Monarch was known from a handful of records until this forest was worked properly, and it is now found here reliably by call. Flores Hawk-Eagle soars over the ridge, and Wallace's Hanging Parrot feeds in the flowering trees along the road.

  • Look for: Flores Monarch, Flores Hawk-Eagle, Wallace's Hanging Parrot, Flores Crow, Flores Green Pigeon, Flores Jungle Flycatcher, Chestnut-capped Thrush
  • Stops:
    • , the roadside forest on the Trans-Flores highway, birded from the tarmac at first light
    • , the village below the forest, and the nearest mapped settlement to it
  • Also watch for: Flores Giant Rat lives in this forest and is occasionally caught on camera traps, and the island's civets work the road at night. Flores is also where the fossils of Homo floresiensis were found, at Liang Bua an hour east.

Central Flores: Kelimutu and the Ruteng highlands

Montane forest along the central spine of Flores, from the Ruteng ridges to the three coloured crater lakes

The Trans-Flores highway climbs into cloud forest at Ruteng and stays high most of the way east. This is where Bare-throated Whistler sings, which is worth the trip on its own: a small bird with an enormous mimetic song delivered from an exposed perch with its throat inflated. Flores Scops Owl is worked after dark on the same ridges, and Kelimutu's crater lakes at the eastern end are a sunrise walk with the montane birds around the rim.

  • Look for: Bare-throated Whistler, Flores Scops Owl, Flores Shortwing, Little Minivet, Russet-capped Tesia, Flores Sea Cuckoo-Dove, Flores Flowerpecker
  • Also recorded in the parks here: Aberrant Bush Warbler, Ashy-bellied White-eye, Banded Fruit Dove
  • National park: Kelimutu National Park
  • Stops:
    • , the highland town on the Trans-Flores highway, with the forest at Poco Ranaka above it
    • , the village below Kelimutu, and the start of the pre-dawn drive to the crater rim
    • , the airport on the south coast, and the usual arrival or exit point for the eastern half of Flores
  • Also watch for: The Ruteng forest holds Flores Giant Rat and several endemic bats, and the spiderweb rice fields at Cancar below the ridge are a landscape worth an hour. The crater lakes change colour with their water chemistry and are watched from the rim at dawn.

Sumba

A dry island south of Flores, with monsoon forest in the west and grassland across the rest of it

Sumba is the island with the highest endemism per hectare in the Lesser Sundas and the easiest to work: the remaining forest is in a few blocks off one road, and a guide knows all of them. Sumba Hornbill and Citron-crested Cockatoo are both in the Manupeu-Tanadaru forest, Sumba Buttonquail is walked up in the Yumbu grasslands, and the two boobooks are worked after dark from the same track.

  • Look for: Sumba Hornbill, Citron-crested Cockatoo, Sumba Buttonquail, Great Sumba Boobook, Little Sumba Boobook, Sumba Myzomela, Red-naped Fruit Dove, Sumba Green Pigeon
  • Stops:
    • , the airport town on the north coast and the base most groups use
    • , the village on the road west, with the Manupeu-Tanadaru forest blocks either side of it
    • , the western town, close to the forest and to the megalithic villages
  • Also watch for: Sumba has few native mammals and the interest is elsewhere: the island's ikat weaving, its megalithic tombs and the Pasola, a ritual horseback contest held in February and March, are all part of what people come for.

West Timor: Bipolo, Camplong and Kupang

Lowland monsoon forest and rice paddy within an hour of the airport, on the driest ground in the country

Timor is the driest and most Australian-feeling island in Indonesia, and the birding around Kupang is done in eucalypt-like monsoon woodland and irrigated paddy. Bipolo is a small forest patch beside rice fields that produces most of the lowland list in a morning, including Timor Sparrow and Black-banded Flycatcher, and Camplong up the road adds the drier woodland species.

  • Look for: Black-banded Flycatcher, Olive-headed Lorikeet, Timor Boobook, Timor Oriole, Timor Blue Flycatcher, Timor Friarbird, Olive-headed Lorikeet, Timor Green Pigeon
  • Stops:
    • , the provincial capital and airport, and the base for everything in West Timor
    • , a small forest patch beside rice fields an hour from town, and the most productive single morning on the island
    • , a recreation forest on the road east, drier and more open than Bipolo
  • Also watch for: Timor's mammals are almost all introduced, the deer and civets included. What the island has instead is a reptile list with an Australian flavour and, on the coast at Kupang, an evening flight of enormous flying foxes.

Gunung Mutis and the Timor highlands

Eucalypt and Podocarpus forest at 1,800 m on Timor's central mountain, and cold enough for a fire at night

Gunung Mutis is the highland half of a Timor trip and the ground where the island's montane endemics live. The forest is open eucalypt with Podocarpus, unlike anything else in Indonesia and much more like Australia, and the walking is easy. Timor Stubtail, Timor Leaf Warbler and Orange-sided Thrush are all worked here, and the nights at Fatumnasi are genuinely cold.

  • Look for: Timor Stubtail, Timor Leaf Warbler, Orange-sided Thrush, Timor Heleia, Timor Imperial Pigeon, Timor Nightjar, Timor Flowerpecker
  • Stops:
    • , the highland town on the road east from Kupang, and the base for Mutis
    • , the village below the mountain at about 1,500 m, with homestays and the forest trail starting behind
  • Also watch for: The Mutis forest is grazed by cattle and horses belonging to the villages around it, which is part of why it looks the way it does. The interest is botanical as much as anything: this is the largest surviving stand of Podocarpus and eucalypt woodland in Wallacea.

Rote and Semau

Two low islands off Timor's western tip, each with birds split from the Timorese ones

Rote is a ferry from Kupang and a short drive, and it exists on an itinerary because three birds were split from their Timorese counterparts and are found on this island alone. The habitat is degraded scrub and palm savanna rather than forest, which makes the birding quick and the conservation picture uncomfortable.

  • Look for: Rote Boobook, Rote Myzomela, Rote Leaf Warbler, Timor Friarbird, Zebra Finch, Australasian Figbird, Red-cheeked Parrot
  • Stops:
    • , the main town on Rote, reached by fast ferry from Kupang, with the scrub and savanna sites around it
  • Also watch for: Rote's lontar palm savanna is worked for palm sugar and is the landscape that shapes the island. The Rote Island Snake-necked Turtle, endemic to a few lakes here, was traded almost to extinction and is now the subject of a reintroduction programme.

Lombok and Sumbawa

The two islands immediately east of Bali, and the first ground on the far side of Wallace's Line

Lombok is where Wallace's Line is crossed, and the change is immediate: the barbets and trogons of Bali stop and the cockatoos and friarbirds begin. Rinjani's forest above Senaru holds Rinjani Scops Owl, described as recently as 2013 and found on this mountain alone. Sumbawa next door adds a drier list and its own subspecies, and Tambora at its eastern end is the volcano whose 1815 eruption changed the weather worldwide.

  • Look for: Rinjani Scops Owl, Elegant Pitta, Wallacean Drongo, Flame-breasted Sunbird, Yellow-crested Cockatoo, Short-tailed Starling, Garden Sunbird, Wallacean Cuckooshrike
  • National park: Mount Rinjani National Park
  • Stops:
    • , the village on Rinjani's northern slope at about 600 m, with the forest trail and the waterfalls above it
    • , the town on Sumbawa's north coast, with an airstrip and the drier woodland sites inland
  • Also watch for: The Rinjani forest holds Ebony Leaf Monkey and Crab-eating Macaque, both Asian animals at the eastern edge of their range, which is Wallace's Line showing itself in the mammals as well as the birds. Moyo island off Sumbawa is a deer sanctuary.

Alor, Wetar and the eastern arc

The eastern end of the arc, seldom visited, each island holding something described in the last twenty years

East of Flores the arc continues through Lembata, Pantar, Alor and Wetar, and almost nobody birds it. Alor has a boobook and a myzomela of its own, both described in this century, and Wetar further out holds a ground dove, a figbird and an oriole found nowhere else. These are charter legs and the reason they appear on so few itineraries is logistics rather than birds.

  • Look for: Alor Boobook, Alor Myzomela, Wetar Ground Dove, Wetar Figbird, Wetar Oriole, Wetar Myzomela, Wetar Scops Owl
  • Stops:
    • , the town on Alor with an airstrip, and the base for the forest above it and the crossing to Pantar
  • Also watch for: Alor's reefs are among the least visited in Indonesia and the strait between Alor and Pantar carries strong currents and regular cetaceans. Wetar is largely uninhabited and its interior has barely been surveyed.

Frequently Asked Questions

When is the best time to go birding in the Lesser Sundas?

June to October, and unusually for Indonesia the answer is not complicated. These islands run on the Australian monsoon: Kupang records 2 mm of rain in August against 392 mm in January, and Waingapu on Sumba takes under 1,000 mm in a whole year. The forest tracks are passable and the crossings are calm. The three seasons.

How is this different from birding elsewhere in Indonesia?

It is drier, more seasonal and far more Australian in feel. The habitat is monsoon forest, savanna and eucalypt woodland rather than rainforest, the islands are small enough that each has its own endemics, and the birding is done in short days rather than long humid ones. When to go, season by season.

Can I see Komodo Dragons and bird on the same trip?

Yes, and the two happen on the same walk. The ranger trails on Komodo and Rinca cross exactly the savanna and monsoon forest that holds Yellow-crested Cockatoo, Green Junglefowl and Orange-footed Scrubfowl, so the dragon walk is the birding. Komodo, Rinca and Labuan Bajo.

Which island has the most endemics?

Sumba has the highest endemism for its size and the easiest access to it, because the remaining forest sits in a few blocks off one road. Flores has more birds overall but they are spread along 350 km of highway, and Timor's are split between the Kupang lowlands and Gunung Mutis. Sumba.

Is Wallace's Line visible here?

The crossing from Bali to Lombok is the line itself, and the change is immediate: the barbets and trogons of Bali stop and the cockatoos and friarbirds begin. It shows in the mammals too, with Ebony Leaf Monkey and Crab-eating Macaque reaching Lombok and going no further east. Lombok and Sumbawa.

How many flights does a full trip need?

Four or five. There is no ferry network that links Lombok to Timor on any useful schedule, so a chain itinerary hops by air between Lombok, Labuan Bajo, Ende, Waingapu and Kupang, with a road transfer at each end and a fast ferry for Rote. The itineraries.

Why does this guide carry no certification badges?

Because none of the schemes that certify accommodation have reached these islands. Every chip on a BeakBrain guide renders from a scheme's own register rather than from a property's claim, and Indonesia's certified properties are almost all Bali resorts. Certification.

How many bird species does Indonesia have?

More than any country except Colombia and Peru, and about a fifth of them live nowhere else. The card wall carries every Indonesian species with a BeakBrain card, which is coded at country level, so the same wall appears on all four Indonesian guides. The birds.
