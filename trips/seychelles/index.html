Birds that decide the trip

Seychelles Black Parrot in the Vallée de Mai

The parrot breeds on Praslin and nowhere else on earth, and it feeds through the coco de mer palm forest in the middle of the island. The Vallée de Mai is a World Heritage site and the walk through it is an hour on made paths.

Seychelles Scops Owl above 400 metres

The owl lives in the moist forest on the Mahé ridge and on Silhouette. It was thought extinct for most of the twentieth century and is heard far more often than it is seen.

When All year, calling after dark

Where Mahé, Silhouette

Seychelles Magpie-robin on the small islands

The magpie-robin came back from a dozen birds on Frégate and now lives on several of the small granitics. It feeds on open ground and walks up to people.

When All year on the small granitics

Sooty Terns on Bird Island

The colony fills the northern end of Bird Island between May and September and can be heard from the airstrip. The birds nest on the ground in the open.

White-throated Rail on Aldabra

The Aldabra race is the last flightless bird in the Indian Ocean. It survives because the atoll has no cats and no rats, and it comes to the feet of anyone standing still.

When April to October, when the ships run

Where Aldabra

Seychelles Warbler on Cousin

The warbler came off Cousin alone, where about 26 birds survived in 1968, and it now lives on several islands. It works the undergrowth and is easier heard than seen.

Seychelles Paradise Flycatcher on La Digue

The male carries a tail longer than its body and hunts the badamier woodland behind the beach on La Digue. The Veuve Reserve exists for it.

Mahé and the Morne Seychellois

Seychelles Scops Owl in the moist forest above 400 metres, and the only island that has it

Mahé carries the archipelago’s only mountain forest of any size, and the Morne Seychellois National Park holds the ridge above 400 metres where the Seychelles Scops Owl lives. The lower slopes and the coast add the kestrel, the swiftlet and the blue pigeon, so the island answers for most of the granitic endemics without a boat.

  • Habitat: Moist forest above 400 metres on the ridge, dry forest on the slopes between 100 and 400 metres, and lowland and coastal vegetation below that.
  • When: June to September for the seabird colonies offshore and the driest walking. The temperature moves only a few degrees across the year.
  • Getting there: Mahé carries the international airport at Pointe Larue. Victoria is the base and the Morne Seychellois trails leave the Sans Souci road above the town.
  • Base yourself in: Victoria
  • All year: Seychelles Scops Owl, Seychelles Bulbul, Seychelles Kestrel, Seychelles White-eye, Seychelles Blue Pigeon, Seychelles Swiftlet

  • Also recorded in the parks here: Black-winged Pratincole, Crab-plover, Lesser Frigatebird, Lesser Noddy
  • National park:

  • Also covers: Anse Major, Morne Seychellois National Park, Victoria

Silhouette and North

A second mountain forest, and a scops owl population away from Mahé

Silhouette rises steeply out of the sea an hour north-west of Mahé and its national park protects the second block of moist forest above 400 metres in the granitics. North Island lies beyond it and has been replanted since its coconut years.

  • Habitat: Moist forest above 400 metres, dry forest on the lower slopes, and a short coastal fringe.
  • When: June to September, when the crossing is calmest and the forest tracks are dry.
  • Getting there: Boats run from Bel Ombre on Mahé. Both islands are private, so the visit is arranged through the island rather than turned up to.
  • Base yourself in: Bel Ombre, Mahé
  • All year: Seychelles Scops Owl, Seychelles Bulbul, Seychelles White-eye, Seychelles Blue Pigeon

  • On passage:
  • In winter:

  • Also covers: North Island, Silhouette National Park
  • Also watch for: Seychelles sheath-tailed bats roost on Silhouette, and giant tortoises graze the coastal flat on North.

Praslin and the Vallée de Mai

The Seychelles Black Parrot, which breeds nowhere else

The Vallée de Mai is a stand of coco de mer palm in the middle of Praslin and the Seychelles Black Parrot feeds through it. The parrot is the island’s own bird and the surrounding dry forest carries the bulbul, the blue pigeon and the swiftlet with it.

  • Habitat: Dry forest between 100 and 400 metres, with a palm forest of coco de mer at its centre and lowland vegetation on the coast.
  • When: All year for the parrot. August to October is quietest for a walk in the palm forest.
  • Getting there: Praslin has its own airstrip and a ferry from Victoria. The Vallée de Mai is signposted from the Baie Sainte Anne road and charges an entry fee.
  • Base yourself in: Baie Sainte Anne
  • All year: Seychelles Black Parrot, Seychelles Bulbul, Seychelles Blue Pigeon, Seychelles Swiftlet

  • In winter:
  • Parks and reserves: Parc national marin de Curieuse, Vallée de Mai National Reserve
  • Stops:
    • : A World Heritage palm forest in the centre of Praslin, and the reliable place for the Seychelles Black Parrot.
  • Also covers: Baie Sainte Anne, Parc national marin de Curieuse, Vallée de Mai National Reserve
  • Also watch for: The coco de mer palm carries the heaviest seed of any plant, and the Vallée de Mai is where it grows wild.

La Digue and the Veuve Reserve

The Seychelles Paradise Flycatcher, which the book names on La Digue

La Digue is small enough to cross on a bicycle and the Veuve Reserve in the middle of it protects the badamier woodland the Seychelles Paradise Flycatcher nests in. The book names La Digue for the bird where it lists the dry forest species.

  • Habitat: Dry forest and badamier woodland on a low granitic island, with marsh behind the beach and coastal vegetation.
  • When: All year. The flycatcher is resident and the reserve is walked in an hour.
  • Getting there: A ferry runs from Praslin in fifteen minutes and from Victoria in an hour. Bicycles are the transport on the island and the reserve is signposted from La Passe.
  • Base yourself in: La Passe
  • All year: Seychelles Paradise Flycatcher, Seychelles Bulbul, Seychelles Swiftlet, Blue-billed White Tern

  • In winter:
  • Stops:
    • : The woodland reserve in the middle of La Digue, kept for the paradise flycatcher and walked on flat paths.
  • Also covers: Anse Source d’Argent, La Passe, Veuve Nature Reserve
  • Also watch for: Giant tortoises range loose on the island and the granite boulders at Anse Source d’Argent are the reason most visitors come.

Cousin, Cousine, Aride and Frégate

Seychelles Warbler and Seychelles Magpie-robin on islands under 200 hectares

The book gives low granite islands under 200 hectares their own line, and these four are the ones a visitor can reach. Cousin, Cousine, Aride and Frégate between them hold the Seychelles Warbler, the Seychelles Magpie-robin and the Seychelles Fody, along with breeding seabirds in up to ten species and roosting frigatebirds.

  • Habitat: Low granite islands under 200 hectares, with scrub and woodland over the interior and seabird colonies on the slopes.
  • When: April to September for the breeding seabirds. The landings depend on the sea and the south-east trades close some of them.
  • Getting there: Day boats run from Praslin to Cousin and Aride. Cousine and Frégate are private islands and reached through the island itself.
  • Base yourself in: Praslin
  • All year: Seychelles Magpie-Robin, Seychelles Warbler, Seychelles Fody, Sooty Tern, Lesser Noddy, Brown Noddy, Bridled Tern, Wedge-tailed Shearwater, Tropical Shearwater

Bird and Denis: the northern coral islands

A Sooty Tern colony that fills a coral island each southern winter

Bird and Denis sit on the northern edge of the bank where the granitic islands give way to coral. Bird carries the Sooty Tern colony and both islands take migrant waders through the northern autumn.

  • Habitat: Coral islands with grass, scrub and casuarina, a beach crest around the edge and no high ground.
  • When: May to September for the Sooty Tern colony. October and November for migrant waders.
  • Getting there: Light aircraft from Mahé serve both islands and each is a single lodge, so the flight and the room are arranged together.

Aldabra and the Outer Islands

Twenty-six endemic taxa on the atolls, and the last flightless bird of the Indian Ocean

Aldabra lies 1,100 kilometres south-west of Mahé and the book counts 26 endemic taxa in its group, 22 of them full species, with strong affinities to Madagascar. The White-throated Rail survives here as the last flightless bird in the Indian Ocean, alongside the Aldabra Drongo and the Aldabra Fody.

  • Habitat: Coral islands and atolls, with pemphis scrub over the limestone, mangrove around the lagoon and open pools behind the beach.
  • When: April to October, outside the north-west monsoon, which is when the expedition ships run.
  • Getting there: Aldabra has no airstrip and no hotel. Visits are by expedition ship or by the research station, and the Seychelles Islands Foundation issues the permits.
  • Base yourself in: Assumption
  • All year: Aldabra Drongo, Aldabra Fody, White-throated Rail, Souimanga Sunbird, Red-tailed Tropicbird, Lesser Frigatebird, Red-footed Booby, Crab-plover, Malagasy Sacred Ibis, Malagasy White-eye

    • : A raised coral atoll and World Heritage site. Expedition ships reach it under permit from the Seychelles Islands Foundation.
  • Also covers: Aldabra Atoll, Assumption, Astove
  • Also watch for: Aldabra holds the largest population of giant tortoises anywhere, around 100,000 of them, and the lagoon fills and empties twice a day.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I bird here with limited mobility?

Sites on this page differ from each other more than any rating could carry, so this is the framework the standard British guides use, and the reserve's own page and warden hold the answers.

  • Permission · some reserves open ground to disabled visitors that is closed to everyone else, and it is granted by asking.
  • Priority · parking bays, hides and the nearest paths are often held for disabled visitors. Ask what is reserved and how to claim it.
  • Parking · how close a car reaches the birds shapes the whole day. Ring ahead for the nearest hard standing to the water or the hide.
  • Paths · surfaces, gradients and gate widths vary inside a single reserve. Ask which trail is boarded, surfaced or level.
  • Perches · a hide with a low window, or a bench where the view opens, turns a viewpoint into a morning.

Every reserve above with its own link carries its access detail there, and a warden answers what a page cannot.
