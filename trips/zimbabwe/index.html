At a glance

Best months
May to October for the game country and the big rivers, and late November to February for African Pitta and the wet grassland crakes
Regions
Harare and the HighveldThe Nyanga Highlands and the Honde ValleyThe Bvumba, Chirinda and ChimanimaniThe Southeast Lowveld and GonarezhouVictoria Falls and the ZambeziHwange National ParkThe Matobo Hills and BulawayoMana Pools and the Middle Zambezi
Routes
The Eastern Highlands13 daysVictoria Falls and Hwange9 daysThe Matobo Hills and the southeast lowveld10 daysThe middle Zambezi and the pitta9 days
Getting in
Harare is the main international gateway. Victoria Falls and Bulawayo have airports of their own, which cuts a long drive off a western trip.
Getting around
The main roads are tarred and a hire car reaches most of the sites. A 4WD is needed inside Mana Pools, Gonarezhou and Chizarira and on the road to the Haroni confluence. Fuel can be short outside the cities, so carry enough for the leg ahead and treat access details as liable to change.

When to Go

Zimbabwe runs on one rain season. The rains reach the Mashonaland plateau in late December and end in about March, and they carry the birds the country is visited for: African Pitta calls in the middle Zambezi from late November, and the Harare vleis flood and fill with breeding crakes and flufftails from December. The dry months from May to October are the game season in the western parks, when everything comes down to the pans.

Dry

May to October

The bush thins and the game concentrates on the pans and the rivers, which is the season the western parks are built around.

Go for Hwange for the pumped pans, Mana Pools for the flood plain, and the Zambezi sandbars for African Skimmer from June to December.

Trade off The highveld vleis are dry and the migrant crakes and flufftails have gone north.

Wet

November to April

The rains fill the highveld vleis and bring the intra African migrants, and African Pitta starts calling in the middle Zambezi.

Go for Masoka and Mana Pools for the pitta from late November, and the Harare vleis for Streaky-breasted Flufftail and Striped Crake from December.

Harare and the Highveld

Miombo woodland and seasonal wet grassland inside a capital city

Harare stands at about 1 500 m on the central watershed of the highveld, and the drainage lines through its suburbs hold open grassy vleis between blocks of miombo, the broad-leaved woodland that covers most of the plateau. A few weeks after the first rains reach the Mashonaland plateau the vleis flood to ankle depth and the migrant crakes and flufftails arrive to breed in them. Marondera and Mazowe put granite koppies and taller woodland within an early start of the city.

  • Habitat: Seasonally flooded vlei grassland with permanently wet seepage ground on the lower slopes, miombo woodland on the drier soils, granite koppies with their own dwarf woodland, and two large reservoirs west and north of the city.
  • When: December to February is the window for the wet grassland birds, when the vleis are flooded and the migrant crakes and flufftails are breeding. The miombo birds are resident all year and late August to early September is the easiest spell for them.
  • Getting there: The vleis and woodlands sit inside the city and are reached on made roads all year. Gravel roads inside the parks are closed in wet years, so ask at the gate before driving them. Gosho Park lies about 70 km east of the city and a morning session there needs a very early start.
  • Base yourself in: Harare
  • All year: Streaky-breasted Flufftail, Striped Crake, African Spotted Creeper, Boulder Chat, Miombo Rock Thrush, Whyte's Barbet, Locust Finch, Rosy-throated Longclaw, Cuckoo-finch, Southern Hyliota

  • On passage: Collared Flycatcher, Amur Falcon, Corn Crake
  • In winter: African Spotted Creeper, Boulder Chat, Miombo Rock Thrush, Whyte's Barbet, Marsh Owl

The Nyanga Highlands and the Honde Valley

Montane grassland and forest on the highest ground in the country, falling east to tea estates

The Nyanga massif carries the most extensive run of montane forest in the eastern highlands and rises to 2 593 m at Mount Nyangani. Grassland covers the plateau and holds one of the largest breeding populations of Blue Swallow left anywhere. East of the escarpment the ground drops to about 700 m in the Honde Valley, where tea estates, marsh and low altitude forest carry a set of birds the high ground never sees.

  • Habitat: Afromontane grassland and forest patches on the massif, pine and wattle plantations around the resorts, and low altitude forest, tea estates and reed marsh on the valley floor below.
  • When: Most of the specials are resident. Scarce Swift breeds from August to February and Blue Swallow is present from August to April, so the summer months carry the fullest list.
  • Getting there: The massif is reached on tar through Juliasdale and the main viewpoints have made roads to them. The Honde Valley turns east off the Mutare road and winds down about 60 km of narrow road to the tea estates, which takes far longer than the distance suggests. A 4WD is needed for the reserve tracks on the valley floor.
  • Base yourself in: Nyanga
  • All year: Blue Swallow, Scarce Swift, Roberts's Warbler, Chirinda Apalis, Red-faced Crimsonwing, Gurney's Sugarbird, Marsh Tchagra, Lesser Seedcracker, Moustached Grass Warbler, Striped Flufftail
  • In the breeding season: Blue Swallow, Scarce Swift, Short-winged Cisticola
  • On passage: Ayres's Hawk-Eagle, Palm-nut Vulture
  • In winter: Roberts's Warbler, Chirinda Apalis, Stripe-cheeked Greenbul, Red-faced Crimsonwing, Barratt's Warbler
  • National park: Nyanga National Park
  • Stops:
    • Mount Nyangani : The highest point in the country at 2 593 m, with montane grassland running up to it. The slopes carry Striped Flufftail and Cape Grassbird in even small patches of indigenous grass.
    • Mtarazi Falls : High cliffs where the Mtarazi River plunges into the Honde Valley, and one of the most accessible places anywhere for Scarce Swift. The forest patches beside the picnic site hold Chirinda Apalis and Roberts's Warbler.
    • : Grassland on the south western slopes of Mount Nyangani. Gurney's Sugarbird feeds on the proteas here and Fan-tailed Grassbird calls from the rank grass.
    • Udu Dam : A small dam in open grassland on the plateau. Blue Swallow hunts low over the grass around it through the summer.
    • : A resort area where pines and wattle have taken much of the ground. Red-faced Crimsonwing and Barratt's Warbler hold the tangled forest edges that remain.

The Bvumba, Chirinda and Chimanimani

Mist forest above Mutare and the lowland forest patches at the southern end of the highlands

The Bvumba rises about 30 km south east of Mutare and gives the easiest access in Zimbabwe to Swynnerton's Robin, Roberts's Warbler and Chirinda Apalis, three birds of the eastern forests. South of it Chirinda Forest holds the southernmost tropical evergreen forest in the country, and the lowland patches at the Haroni and Rusitu confluence carry species that reach nowhere else on this side of the border. The Chimanimani range closes the highlands with quartzite peaks and open montane grassland.

  • Habitat: Montane forest, grassland and plantation between about 1 500 m and 1 750 m in the Bvumba, mid altitude evergreen forest at Chirinda, lowland forest and cultivated land at the Haroni confluence, and heath and quartzite grassland on the Chimanimani tops.
  • When: Every forest special can be found through the year. Heavy rain from December to February can last for days on the eastern slopes and makes forest birding hard, so the drier months are the surer bet.
  • Getting there: The Bvumba road climbs from Mutare on tar and the forest sites along it are signposted. Chirinda Forest sits about 30 km from Chipinge on tar. The Haroni confluence is about 60 km of gravel from Chimanimani town and takes two to three hours in a high clearance vehicle, and the road deteriorates from November to May. The Vimba forest is sacred ground and permission to enter is asked for at the community camp.
  • Base yourself in: Mutare
  • All year: Swynnerton's Robin, Roberts's Warbler, Chirinda Apalis, White-tailed Crested Flycatcher, Stripe-cheeked Greenbul, Lowland Tiny Greenbul, Eastern Bronze-naped Pigeon, Livingstone's Turaco, Black-fronted Bushshrike, Gurney's Sugarbird
  • In the breeding season: Blue Swallow, African Broadbill, Scarce Swift
  • On passage: Grey Cuckooshrike, Taita Falcon
  • In winter: Swynnerton's Robin, Roberts's Warbler, Chirinda Apalis, Woodwards' Batis, Red-capped Robin-Chat
  • National parks:
    • Chimanimani National Park, Quartzite peaks, caves and open grassland on the Bundi River plain. Blue Swallow hunts over the grassland and Gurney's Sugarbird feeds on the aloes and proteas.

The Southeast Lowveld and Gonarezhou

Hot mopane and palm country on two big rivers, with the highest raptor density in the region

Gonarezhou and the conservancies around it fill the lowveld, the hot low country in the south east where the Save and the Runde meet. Stands of ilala palm, riverine thicket and well grown mopane run along the rivers, and the region carries one of the highest densities of large raptors in southern Africa. Masvingo and the granite country around Great Zimbabwe sit on the way in and add miombo birding to the trip.

  • Habitat: Mopane and mixed woodland, dense ilala palm stands along the Runde, seasonal pans, riverine thicket and dry forest, with well grown miombo on the granite country to the north west.

Victoria Falls and the Zambezi

Rainforest spray, Kalahari teak and a great river with sandbars and rock islands

The Zambezi drops about 100 m into the gorge over a curtain of falling water some 2 km wide, and the permanent spray keeps a patch of rainforest alive beside it. Above the falls the river braids around wooded islands and exposes sandbars as the level drops, which is where the big river birds are. Teak woodland on Kalahari sand runs west from the town and grassy vleis open out to the south.

  • Habitat: Riverine woodland and wooded islands, spray rainforest at the lip of the gorge, teak forest on Kalahari sand, grassy vleis, and basalt gorges carrying mopane and acacia scrub.
  • When: October to April brings most of the specials in. Rock Pratincole is present from August to January, African Skimmer from June to December, and February is the month for River Warbler in the teak.
  • Getting there: The national park has three entrances, one on foot near the border for the falls themselves, one about 5 km west of town for the riverside drive, and one about 5 km south on a good dirt road for the vlei. A boat and a local guide are what open the river above the falls. Elephant, Buffalo and Lion all use the ground around the town, so walking there needs care.
  • Base yourself in: Victoria Falls

Hwange National Park

Kalahari sand, teak woodland and pumped pans over 14 650 square kilometres

Hwange is the largest national park in Zimbabwe and spans 14 650 square kilometres of Kalahari sandveld. Teak and mixed woodland cover most of it, mopane runs through the north, and open grassland and pans break it up. Water is pumped to the pans through the dry months, so game and birds come down to them in numbers, and the raptor list is one of the strongest in the region. Chizarira sits on the escarpment north east of the park and is the remote alternative.

  • Habitat: Kalahari sandveld carrying teak and mixed woodland, mopane woodland in the north, open grassland, vleis and pumped pans, with escarpment woodland and gorges at Chizarira.
  • When: Birding is good all year and migrants are in from about September to April. The dry months concentrate everything on the pans, which is when a day of pan watching pays best.
  • Getting there: The park turns off the main road between Bulawayo and Victoria Falls and Main Camp lies about 23 km in on a signposted road. The road to the Shumba pans is tarred with dirt loops off it north and south, and the run on to Sinamatella and out through the north gate is dirt. Ask at Main Camp for the state of the roads before driving the loops. Chizarira is remote and a 4WD is needed for it.
  • Base yourself in: Dete
  • All year: Racket-tailed Roller, Bradfield's Hornbill, Three-banded Courser, Arnot's Chat, Tinkling Cisticola, Kalahari Scrub Robin, Southern Pied Babbler, Dickinson's Kestrel, Yellow-billed Oxpecker, Dusky Lark

  • On passage: Dusky Lark, Mosque Swallow
  • In winter: Arnot's Chat, Kalahari Scrub Robin, Southern Pied Babbler, Kori Bustard, Pale Chanting Goshawk

    • Hwange National Park

The Matobo Hills and Bulawayo

Granite whalebacks and wooded valleys south of the second city, with fifty recorded raptor species

The Matobo Hills stretch over 300 000 ha of granite dome landscape south of Bulawayo and hold the longest running raptor study in the world. More than 50 pairs of Verreaux's Eagle have held territories inside the area for forty years and 50 raptor species have been recorded across it. Around Bulawayo itself a thornveld sanctuary and a municipal farm add acacia and wetland birds that the granite country does not carry.

  • Habitat: Granite domes and boulder fields with wooded valleys and moister woodland between them, acacia thornveld on the Bulawayo side, and a chain of dams and reedbeds on the northern edge of the city.
  • When: The raptors are on territory through the year. Dry season visits to the dams around Bulawayo produce the water birds, and the wet season brings migrant warblers, shrikes and waders in.
  • Getting there: Matobo lies about 30 km south of Bulawayo on a made road. Tshabalala sits on the same road on the edge of the city and is walked from the entrance gate. The Aisleby dams are about 10 km north of the city and are reached on a short gravel run off the Victoria Falls road.
  • Base yourself in: Bulawayo
  • All year: Verreaux's Eagle, Boulder Chat, Cape Eagle-Owl, Freckled Nightjar, Mottled Swift, Cape Batis, Ashy Tit, Burnt-necked Eremomela, Cape Penduline Tit, Maccoa Duck

  • On passage: Western Marsh Harrier, Thrush Nightingale, Spotted Crake
  • In winter: Boulder Chat, Maccoa Duck, Black-necked Grebe, Cape Eagle-Owl, Kalahari Scrub Robin

    • Tshabalala Game Sanctuary, The only reserve in the country where acacia thornveld dominates, on the edge of Bulawayo. Ashy Tit, Cape Penduline Tit and Burnt-necked Eremomela are all found on the walk in from the gate.

Mana Pools and the Middle Zambezi

Riverine forest and flood plain woodland under a blue escarpment, and the surest African Pitta in the region

The middle Zambezi runs below Lake Kariba through a hot valley more than a hundred kilometres wide in places, and almost all of the Zimbabwean side of it is given over to wildlife. Mopane covers the ground and the best birding sits in the strips of forest and thicket along the rivers draining off the plateau. Mana Pools is one of very few reserves with dangerous game where visitors walk freely, and Masoka Village to the east is where African Pitta is close to guaranteed when it displays.

  • Habitat: Flood plain woodland of Faidherbia albida on the river terrace, dense Vetivaria grassland, mopane and dry woodland behind it, riverine forest and thicket along the tributaries, and miombo on the escarpment above.
  • When: Peak season runs from August until the rains start in about November, when game concentrates on the river. Late November to January is the window for African Pitta, which arrives with the rains and calls from fallen branches.
  • Getting there: The permit is processed at the national parks office on the escarpment before the descent, and the run in to the camp on the river is about 70 km of dirt. A high clearance vehicle or a 4WD is wanted and a 4WD is essential in the rains. There is no fuel and no shop in the park, so carry enough for the full return leg. Masoka is remote, needs a 4WD in the rains, and a game guide from the community office is what makes birding on foot there safe.
  • Base yourself in: Kariba
  • All year: African Pitta, Lilian's Lovebird, Livingstone's Flycatcher, Racket-tailed Roller, Böhm's Spinetail, African Skimmer, Red-necked Falcon, Southern Ground Hornbill, Arnot's Chat, Meves's Starling

  • On passage: Thick-billed Cuckoo, Dusky Lark
  • In winter: Lilian's Lovebird, Livingstone's Flycatcher, Collared Palm Thrush, Long-toed Lapwing, White-browed Coucal

    • Mana Pools National Park, A World Heritage Site on the river where visitors walk freely among dangerous game. Dense stands of Vetivaria grass hide Lion and Buffalo and are best left alone.

Frequently Asked Questions

When is the best time to go birding in Zimbabwe?

The country runs on one rain season and the two halves suit different birds. May to October is dry, the bush thins, game and birds gather at the Hwange pans and on the Mana Pools flood plain, and African Skimmer works the Zambezi sandbars. Late November to February is wet and it delivers African Pitta in the middle Zambezi and breeding crakes and flufftails on the Harare vleis. Heavy rain makes the eastern highland forests hard work. The two seasons.

What do you need to enter Zimbabwe?

Every visitor needs a visa and most get one on arrival, usually for 30 days, though an e-visa can be applied for beforehand. The passport must have at least six months left from the day of arrival and three blank pages. The KAZA Univisa covers Zimbabwe and Zambia together with day trips into Botswana, and it is sold at Harare, Bulawayo and Victoria Falls airports and at the Victoria Falls and Kazungula land borders. A yellow fever certificate is required from anyone arriving from a country listed as a transmission risk.

Is malaria a risk in Zimbabwe?

The CDC puts malaria transmission across the whole country, with no city exempted, and recommends that every traveller take prescription antimalarials. Plasmodium falciparum is the main parasite and chloroquine resistance is present, so ask a doctor which drug suits you.

Can you drive yourself, and how do the regions link up?

The main roads are tarred and a hire car reaches Harare, the eastern highlands, Bulawayo, Masvingo and Victoria Falls. A 4WD is needed inside Mana Pools, Gonarezhou and Chizarira and on the road to the Haroni confluence. Fuel can be short outside the cities, so carry enough for the leg ahead. The western parks and the eastern forests share no ground, and Victoria Falls and Bulawayo both have airports, which is how most trips avoid the long drive across the country. The routes.

How safe is Zimbabwe, and can you use binoculars and a long lens freely?

The FCDO advises against travel to no part of the country. Its regional warnings cover commercial farms, where invasions still happen occasionally, and mining areas, which are often restricted. It also reports smash and grab robberies at intersections on the road to Harare airport, on Borrowdale Road and Churchill Road, and on the Masvingo to Beitbridge road. Photography is the one to plan around: government offices, airports, military sites, party offices, official residences and embassies cannot be photographed without permission from the Ministry of Information, police and soldiers cannot be photographed at all, and the police enforce this. Armed guards patrol the streets around State House in Harare, where photography is banned and the roads close from 6pm to 6am.

How does money work in Zimbabwe?

Zimbabwe runs a multi-currency system. The US dollar is legal tender alongside Zimbabwe Gold, which replaced the Zimbabwean dollar in April 2024. Physical dollar cash is short, international cards do not always work at ATMs, and change is rarely available, so carry small notes, refuse torn ones and check what a lodge or an operator accepts before you arrive.

Which birds is Zimbabwe the place for?

African Pitta draws most of the trips. It arrives in the middle Zambezi thicket with the rains and displays from late November, and Masoka and Mana Pools are where people go for it. Swynnerton’s Robin, Roberts’s Warbler and Chirinda Apalis live in the eastern highland forests and almost nowhere else, and the Nyanga grasslands hold one of the largest breeding populations of Blue Swallow left. Boulder Chat works the granite of the Matobo and the hills around Harare. Every Zimbabwean species.

Can I bird here with limited mobility?

Often yes, and it varies by site. A good deal of the birding here happens from roadsides, hides and lodge grounds rather than on long walks, and some reserves keep flat, surfaced trails. Ask a lodge or reserve directly about step free access, surfaces and seating before you book. The Birdability Map records those details for birding sites, and their Access Considerations guide lists the questions worth asking. Coverage is thin outside the United States, so read a blank area as unrecorded rather than unsuitable.
