Birding in British Columbia

British Columbia runs from the only true desert in the country to an archipelago a hundred kilometres offshore that holds about half the world population of Ancient Murrelet.

A hire car and several ferries. Vancouver to Penticton is four hours, Tofino four hours from the ferry, and Haida Gwaii a flight or a long crossing

Birds that decide the trip

Ancient Murrelet on Haida Gwaii

Roughly half the world population breeds on these islands, in burrows under the rainforest, and the chicks walk to the sea two days after hatching without ever being fed on land.

When May to July, on the island colonies and from boats.

Where Haida Gwaii and the north coast

Sage Thrasher in the antelope-brush

The southern Okanagan is the only place in the country it breeds, on the northern tip of the Great Basin shrub-steppe, and the population is very small.

When Late April to July, in antelope-brush at dawn.

Where The Okanagan and Similkameen

Western Sandpiper on the Fraser mudflats

Almost the entire world population passes through the delta and the island inlets in about three weeks in late April. The birds feed on the biofilm that coats the mud.

When The last two weeks of April, on the mudflats at mid-tide.

Where The Fraser delta, The west coast of Vancouver Island

White-headed Woodpecker in the Okanagan ponderosa

It reaches the northern edge of its range in the Okanagan ponderosa, and the British Columbia population is small enough that every pair is known about.

When April to July, in mature ponderosa.

Where The Okanagan and Similkameen

Marbled Murrelet in the old growth

A seabird that nests on mossy branches high in old-growth rainforest, which nobody knew until 1974, and it flies inland to the nest at dawn.

When May to July, on the water offshore or heard inland at dawn.

Where The west coast of Vancouver Island, Haida Gwaii and the north coast

Snowy Owl on the Boundary Bay dykes

In an irruption winter the delta fields hold birds that sit on driftwood in plain sight for weeks, and the dyke path walks straight past them.

When November to February in an irruption winter, on the dykes.

Where The Fraser delta

Harlequin Duck on the island rocks

It breeds on fast mountain streams and winters on surf-washed rock, and the southern island shoreline holds one of the more accessible wintering flocks anywhere.

When October to April on the coast, May to July on the mountain streams.

Where Victoria and southern Vancouver Island, The Kootenays and the Rockies

Barrow's Goldeneye on the interior lakes

A very large share of the world population breeds in the interior of this province. The birds nest in tree cavities beside alkaline lakes and winter on the coast.

When May to July inland, and November to March on the coast.

Where The Kootenays and the Rockies, Victoria and southern Vancouver Island

British Columbia is really several countries at once, and they run on different calendars. The coast is a winter and spring destination, the Okanagan a late spring one, the mountains only open from late June, and Haida Gwaii and the seabird colonies work through the summer.

The shorebird passage

Late April and early May

Western Sandpipers move up the coast in enormous numbers on the delta and the island mudflats.

Go for Boundary Bay and Iona, then the Tofino inlets.

Trade off The interior is still cold and the mountain passes are shut.

The desert breeding season

Late May to early July

The Okanagan antelope-brush and ponderosa hold birds that reach nowhere else in the country.

Go for Osoyoos and Vaseux Lake at dawn, and Manning Park for the subalpine.

Trade off It is very hot on the valley floor and the birds go quiet by mid-morning.

June to August

The colonies on Haida Gwaii and the outer coast are full and the boats are running.

Go for Sandspit and the Gwaii Haanas boats, and the Tofino and Ucluelet trips.

Trade off Crossings cancel for weather and the island accommodation is limited.

The coastal winter

November to February

The delta holds the highest raptor densities in the country and the island shore fills with sea duck.

Victoria and southern Vancouver Island

The mildest corner of the country, and the only Garry oak meadows it has

The southern tip of Vancouver Island sits in a rain shadow and stays frost-free most winters, which gives it Garry oak meadow and a wintering bird list that the rest of the country cannot match. Sooke and the Saanich Peninsula put open sea, sheltered inlet and oak woodland within an hour of each other.

  • Habitat: Garry oak meadow on shallow rocky soil, Douglas fir and arbutus woodland, rocky shore and sheltered inlet, tidal flat and eelgrass, and coastal marsh.
  • When: November to March for the wintering sea duck and shorebirds, and April to June for the oak meadow breeders.
  • Getting there: Victoria has an airport and a ferry from Vancouver, and the city is the base. Everything on this list is within an hour of it, and the rocky shore viewpoints are free and open all year.
  • Base yourself in: Victoria
  • Look for: Harlequin Duck, Barrow's Goldeneye, Brant Goose, Black Oystercatcher, Brandt's Cormorant, Pelagic Cormorant, Sooty Grouse, Steller's Jay, Chestnut-backed Chickadee, Varied Thrush, Anna's Hummingbird, Northern Pygmy Owl
  • Parks and reserves: Victoria Harbour Migratory Bird Sanctuary (Reserve), Sooke Hills Wilderness Regional Park (Protected area), Sooke Mountain Provincial Park (State or provincial park), Sooke Lake Road Community Forest (Protected area), Sooke Potholes Provincial Park (State or provincial park)
  • Also covers: Cowichan Valley, Duncan, Saanich Peninsula, Sooke Hills Wilderness Regional Park, Sooke Lake Road Community Forest, Sooke Mountain Provincial Park, Sooke Potholes Provincial Park, Victoria Harbour Migratory Bird Sanctuary
  • Also watch for: Killer whales and grey whales both pass the southern strait, and sea otters have returned to the outer coast.

The west coast of Vancouver Island

Temperate rainforest running down to open Pacific, with shorebirds crossing it in April

The outer coast takes the full weight of the Pacific, which is why the rainforest there is as tall and wet as it is. The mudflats behind Tofino stage very large numbers of Western Sandpipers in April, and the offshore water holds shearwaters and alcids through the summer.

  • Habitat: Temperate rainforest of western hemlock, cedar and Sitka spruce, sand beach and rocky headland, extensive mudflat and eelgrass in the inlets, and open Pacific offshore.
  • When: The last two weeks of April for the shorebird passage, and June to August for the seabirds offshore.
  • Getting there: Victoria or Nanaimo are the ferry ports and Tofino the base, four hours by road across the island. The park reserve charges a day fee, and the boat operators run seabird and whale trips from both Tofino and Ucluelet.
  • Base yourself in: Tofino
  • Look for: Western Sandpiper, Black Oystercatcher, Marbled Murrelet, Rhinoceros Auklet, Cassin's Auklet, Tufted Puffin, Pigeon Guillemot, Sooty Grouse, Varied Thrush, Chestnut-backed Chickadee, American Dipper, Bald Eagle
  • Parks and reserves: Pacific Rim National Park Reserve of Canada, Pacific Rim National Park Reserve - West Coast Trail Unit, Pacific Rim National Park Reserve - Long Beach Unit, Tofino Mudflats Wildlife Management Area (National wildlife refuge), ʔuuʔinmitis Conservancy (Protected area)
  • Also covers: Pacific Rim National Park Reserve - Long Beach Unit, Pacific Rim National Park Reserve - West Coast Trail Unit, Pacific Rim National Park Reserve of Canada, Port Alberni, Port Renfrew, Tofino Mudflats Wildlife Management Area, Ucluelet, ʔuuʔinmitis Conservancy
  • Also watch for: Grey whales feed off the beaches from March, black bears work the intertidal at low tide, and sea otters hold the offshore kelp.

The Fraser delta

The largest estuary on the Pacific coast of the country, with a city built around it

The Fraser drops its sediment into a shallow delta at the edge of Vancouver, and the mudflats and marsh there hold the highest winter raptor densities in the country and shorebirds in enormous numbers on passage. Reifel is a managed sanctuary in the middle of it with tame Sandhill Cranes on the paths.

  • Habitat: Tidal mudflat and eelgrass, brackish and freshwater marsh, managed sanctuary ponds, farmland on the delta soils, and dyke paths throughout.
  • When: Late April and early May for the Western Sandpiper passage, and November to February for the geese, swans and raptors.
  • Getting there: Vancouver is the airport and the base, and the delta starts twenty minutes south of it. Reifel charges a small admission, and the Boundary Bay dykes and Iona jetty are free.
  • Base yourself in: Vancouver
  • Look for: Snow Goose, Sandhill Crane, Western Sandpiper, Dunlin, Snowy Owl, Short-eared Owl, Northern Harrier, Bald Eagle, Peregrine Falcon, Trumpeter Swan, Brant Goose, Grey Plover
  • Parks and reserves: Boundary Bay Wildlife Management Area (National wildlife refuge), Boundary Bay Regional Park (Protected area), Strathcona Provincial Park (State or provincial park), Fraser River Delta -- Ramsar (Protected area), Iona Beach Regional Park (Protected area)
  • Also covers: Boundary Bay Regional Park, Boundary Bay Wildlife Management Area, Fraser River Delta -- Ramsar, Iona Beach Regional Park, Iona Island, Reifel Bird Sanctuary, Richmond, Strathcona Provincial Park, Vancouver
  • Also watch for: Harbour seals work the delta channels and the Fraser carries one of the largest salmon runs left on the coast.

The Okanagan and Similkameen

The only true desert in the country, with birds that reach nowhere else north of the border

The southern Okanagan is antelope-brush and sagebrush on the northern tip of the Great Basin, and it carries species that occur nowhere else in the country: Sage Thrasher, Grey Flycatcher, Canyon Wren and White-headed Woodpecker among them. The valley climbs from desert to subalpine in about an hour.

  • Habitat: Antelope-brush and sagebrush on the valley floor, ponderosa pine on the benches, Douglas fir and larch above that, subalpine at Manning, and lakes and oxbows on the river.
  • When: Late May to early July for the breeding birds. It is very hot on the valley floor from July and the birds go quiet by mid-morning.
  • Getting there: Kelowna has an airport and Penticton is the base, four hours east of Vancouver. Much of the desert is on Indigenous land or private ranch, so the roadside pull-outs and the provincial parks are where a visitor birds.
  • Base yourself in: Penticton
  • Look for: Sage Thrasher, Canyon Wren, Rock Wren, White-headed Woodpecker, Lewis's Woodpecker, Williamson's Sapsucker, Flammulated Owl, Common Poorwill, Lazuli Bunting, Bullock's Oriole, Yellow-breasted Chat, Calliope Hummingbird
  • Parks and reserves: Okanagan Falls Trails Recreation Site (Protected area), Okanagan Falls Biodiversity Ranch (Protected area), Oliver Mountain Recreation Site (Protected area), Vaseux Lake Provincial Park (State or provincial park), Osoyoos Desert Centre (Protected area)
  • Also covers: Manning Park, Okanagan Falls Biodiversity Ranch, Okanagan Falls Trails Recreation Site, Oliver Mountain Recreation Site, Osoyoos Desert Centre, Penticton, Vaseux Lake Provincial Park
  • Also watch for: Bighorn sheep hold the valley bluffs, and the antelope-brush carries rattlesnakes and the only scorpions in the country.

The Kootenays and the Rockies

Wet interior mountains, with a valley-bottom marsh that takes waterbirds in six figures

The Creston valley marsh is a very large managed wetland in the bottom of a mountain trench, and it takes a hundred and fifty thousand waterbirds a year. The national parks east of it climb from cedar and hemlock to icefield, and hold the interior mountain birds.

  • Habitat: Managed valley-bottom marsh and open water, cedar and hemlock on the wet lower slopes, spruce and fir above, alpine tundra and icefield on the highest ground.
  • When: May to July for the marsh and mountain breeders, and the alpine only once the passes clear, usually from late June.
  • Getting there: Cranbrook or Calgary in Alberta are the airports and Creston the base. The wildlife area has a paid interpretive centre and free dyke walks, and the national parks charge an entry fee.
  • Base yourself in: Creston
  • Look for: Barrow's Goldeneye, Trumpeter Swan, Sandhill Crane, Black Tern, Forster's Tern, American Dipper, Harlequin Duck, White-tailed Ptarmigan, Clark's Nutcracker, American Three-toed Woodpecker, Boreal Chickadee, Grey-crowned Rosy Finch
  • Parks and reserves: Mount Robson Provincial Park (State or provincial park), Kootenay National Park, Yoho National Park, Creston Valley Wildlife Management Area (National wildlife refuge), Mount Robson Corridor Protected Area
  • Also covers: Creston Valley Wildlife Management Area, Kootenay National Park, Mount Robson Corridor Protected Area, Mount Robson Provincial Park, Yoho National Park
  • Also watch for: Grizzly and black bear both use the valley, and the Creston marsh holds the northernmost breeding Forster's Terns on the continent.

Haida Gwaii and the north coast

An archipelago a hundred kilometres offshore, with its own subspecies and half the world's Ancient Murrelets

Haida Gwaii sits far enough out that several of its birds and mammals evolved separately, and its seabird colonies hold roughly half the world population of Ancient Murrelet. The mainland coast opposite is fjord and Sitka spruce with the Skeena running through it.

  • Habitat: Sitka spruce and western hemlock rainforest, sea cliff and offshore stack with seabird burrows, sheltered inlet and eelgrass, and river estuary on the mainland.
  • When: May to July for the seabird colonies and the forest breeders, which is also when the boats run.
  • Getting there: Sandspit has an airport with flights from Vancouver, and a ferry runs from Prince Rupert. Gwaii Haanas in the south needs a permit and a boat, and the northern islands are reached by road and a short ferry.
  • Base yourself in: Sandspit
  • Look for: Ancient Murrelet, Cassin's Auklet, Rhinoceros Auklet, Tufted Puffin, Pigeon Guillemot, Black Oystercatcher, Marbled Murrelet, Sooty Grouse, Northern Saw-whet Owl, Varied Thrush, Bald Eagle, Peregrine Falcon
  • Parks and reserves: Gwaii Haanas National Park Reserve and Haida Heritage Site, Woodworth Lake Conservancy (Protected area), Lakelse Lake Provincial Park (State or provincial park), Hai Lake - Mount Herman Provincial Park (State or provincial park), Sleeping Beauty Mountain Provincial Park (State or provincial park)
  • Also covers: Gwaii Haanas National Park Reserve and Haida Heritage Site, Hai Lake - Mount Herman Provincial Park, Haida Gwaii, Lakelse Lake Provincial Park, Prince Rupert, Sandspit, Skeena Valley, Sleeping Beauty Mountain Provincial Park, Terrace, Woodworth Lake Conservancy
  • Also watch for: The islands have their own subspecies of black bear, ermine and saw-whet owl, and no native land mammals reached them from the mainland.

Frequently Asked Questions

When is the best time to go birding in British Columbia?

The last week of April into early May, if the shorebird passage is the point: almost the whole world Western Sandpiper population moves through the Fraser delta in about three weeks. Early June is better for the Okanagan desert and the mountains.

Where is the desert?

The southern Okanagan, around Osoyoos and Oliver, which is the northern tip of the Great Basin shrub-steppe. Sage Thrasher, Canyon Wren, Grey Flycatcher and White-headed Woodpecker all reach their national limit there and the populations are small.

How do I get to Haida Gwaii?

By air to Sandspit or Masset from Vancouver, or by ferry from Prince Rupert, which takes seven hours. The southern islands in Gwaii Haanas need a permit, an orientation and a boat. Book flights, beds and permits months ahead.

Is the Fraser delta worth it with a city on top of it?

Yes, and the city is part of why it works: the dykes, the sanctuary and the roads through the farmland make an enormous estuary unusually easy to bird. Winter raptor densities there are the highest in the country.

When do the mountain passes open?

The main highways run all year, but the alpine in the national parks only clears from late June and the higher trails later than that. A mountain trip before the end of June will spend most of its time in valley-bottom forest.

Can I bird here with limited mobility?

Sites on this page differ from each other more than any rating could carry, so this is the framework the standard British guides use, and the reserve's own page and warden hold the answers.

  • Permission · some reserves open ground to disabled visitors that is closed to everyone else, and it is granted by asking.
  • Priority · parking bays, hides and the nearest paths are often held for disabled visitors. Ask what is reserved and how to claim it.
  • Parking · how close a car reaches the birds shapes the whole day. Ring ahead for the nearest hard standing to the water or the hide.
  • Paths · surfaces, gradients and gate widths vary inside a single reserve. Ask which trail is boarded, surfaced or level.
  • Perches · a hide with a low window, or a bench where the view opens, turns a viewpoint into a morning.

Every reserve above with its own link carries its access detail there, and a warden answers what a page cannot.
