Birds that decide the trip

Whooping Crane over Wood Buffalo

The only natural nesting ground the species has is a closed corner of marsh inside the park, and the birds pass over the roads and the town on their way to and from it.

When June and July, in flight over the park.

Where Wood Buffalo and the Slave River

American White Pelican on the Slave River rapids

The most northerly nesting colony in the world sits on rocks in whitewater at the edge of Fort Smith, and there are viewing platforms above it.

When June to August, from the town platforms.

Where Wood Buffalo and the Slave River

Pacific Loon on the shield lakes

It nests on small lakes across the shield and the tundra, and the Ingraham Trail passes enough of them to make it the easy loon here.

When June and July, on small lakes.

Where Yellowknife and the North Slave, Great Bear Lake and the Sahtu

Tundra Swan on the Mackenzie Delta

The delta is one of the species' main North American nurseries, and a pair on a thaw lake with cygnets is an ordinary sight there in July.

When June and July, on delta lakes.

Where The Mackenzie Delta and the Beaufort

Smith's Longspur on the delta tussock

It breeds on open tussock in a narrow band across the north, sits still when approached, and is missed far more often than it is absent.

When Mid-June to July, on tussock tundra.

Where The Mackenzie Delta and the Beaufort

Gyrfalcon on the treeline

It nests on cliffs and in old raven nests around Great Bear Lake and the barrens beyond, and hunts ptarmigan across open ground.

When June and July, on the northern shores.

Where Great Bear Lake and the Sahtu

American Dipper on the Nahanni creeks

It walks underwater on the bed of fast side creeks, and the springs that feed them keep some of that water open through the winter.

When All year, on fast side creeks.

Where Nahanni and the Dehcho

Northern Hawk-Owl on a burnt spruce

It hunts by day from the top of a dead tree, so the burnt stands along the roads are where it is easiest, and the territory has a great many burnt stands.

When All year, most visible in June.

Where Yellowknife and the North Slave, Wood Buffalo and the Slave River

When to Go

The season here is June and the first half of July, and almost nothing else. The territory sits across four biomes from aspen parkland to arctic coast, and the birds that use each of them are on the ground for about six weeks.

The waterfowl passage

Late May and early June

Geese, swans and ducks move north up the Mackenzie as the ice goes out.

Go for The Mackenzie Delta and the Slave River lowlands.

Trade off Break-up makes roads and river crossings unreliable for weeks.

Typical daily average

The breeding season

June and early July

Every biome in the territory holds its full breeding list at once.

Go for The Ingraham Trail out of Yellowknife, and Wood Buffalo.

Trade off Mosquitoes and blackflies are at their worst and there is no darkness to escape into.

Typical daily average

The pelican summer

June to August

The Slave River rapids hold the most northerly nesting pelicans in the world.

Go for Fort Smith, where the rapids run through the town's edge.

Trade off The rapids are dangerous and the viewpoints are the only safe way to watch them.

Typical daily average

The autumn move

Late August and September

Tundra turns, cranes and geese move south, and the first snow reaches the barrens.

Go for The Mackenzie Delta and the east arm of Great Slave Lake.

Trade off Freeze-up begins and charter flights become weather-dependent.

Wood Buffalo and the Slave River

The largest national park in Canada, and the last wild nesting ground of a bird that nearly went

Wood Buffalo straddles the boundary with Alberta, and the marshy north-east corner of it, which lies in the Northwest Territories, holds the only natural nesting ground the Whooping Crane has left. The nesting area is closed and watched from the air, but the birds pass over Fort Smith and the park roads.

  • Habitat: Boreal spruce and aspen, karst sinkhole country, salt plain and salt flat, river rapids and marshy delta.
  • When: June and early July, when the cranes are on territory and the pelicans are on the rapids.
  • Getting there: Fort Smith has a small airport and a highway from Hay River. The park is free to enter, the crane nesting area is closed to the public, and the Slave River rapids are viewed from marked platforms in and beside the town.
  • Base yourself in: Fort Smith
  • Look for: Whooping Crane, American White Pelican, Sandhill Crane, Bald Eagle, Peregrine Falcon, Northern Hawk-Owl, Great Grey Owl, Boreal Owl, Spruce Grouse, Canada Jay, Blackpoll Warbler, Trumpeter Swan
  • Also covers: Fort Smith, Little Buffalo River Falls, Slave River rapids, Wood Buffalo National Park
  • Also watch for: The park holds the largest free-roaming wood bison herd in the world, and salt plains that leave a white crust across the boreal forest floor.

Yellowknife and the North Slave

Bare shield rock, small lakes and taiga, with a highway running east into all of it

Yellowknife sits on the Canadian Shield at the north arm of Great Slave Lake, and the Ingraham Trail runs 70 km east from it past a string of lakes and rock outcrops. It is the most accessible taiga birding in the territory, and Great Slave Lake below it is the deepest lake in North America.

  • Habitat: Bare Precambrian shield outcrop, black spruce and jack pine taiga, sphagnum bog and muskeg, and thousands of small lakes.
  • When: June and early July for the breeding birds, when the light never really goes.
  • Getting there: Yellowknife has the territory's largest airport. The Ingraham Trail is paved for its first half and gravel after, with public boat launches and picnic sites along it and no fee.
  • Base yourself in: Yellowknife
  • Look for: Pacific Loon, Red-throated Loon, Common Loon, Bonaparte's Gull, Arctic Tern, Northern Hawk-Owl, Spruce Grouse, Canada Jay, Boreal Chickadee, Grey-cheeked Thrush, Blackpoll Warbler, Redpoll
  • Also covers: Great Slave Lake, Ingraham Trail, Prelude Lake, Yellowknife
  • Also watch for: The shield rock here is among the oldest exposed crust on earth, and the lakes between the outcrops are all glacial scour.

The Mackenzie Delta and the Beaufort

The second largest arctic river delta on earth, with a road running to the sea across it

The Mackenzie drops its sediment into a delta of thousands of channels and lakes before reaching the Beaufort Sea, and the whole of it is a waterfowl and shorebird nursery. The highway from Inuvik to Tuktoyaktuk, opened in 2017, was the first road in Canada to reach the Arctic Ocean.

  • Habitat: River delta channel and thaw lake, wet sedge tundra, low willow and dwarf birch, pingo and polygon ground, and coastal sea ice.
  • When: Mid-June to the end of July, which covers both the breeding season and the belugas.
  • Getting there: Inuvik has flights, and the Dempster Highway crosses into the Northwest Territories from the Yukon to reach it. The Tuktoyaktuk highway beyond is 138 km of gravel and open all year, and the delta itself is reached by boat or float plane.
  • Base yourself in: Inuvik
  • Look for: Tundra Swan, Snow Goose, Brant Goose, Long-tailed Jaeger, Red Phalarope, Red-necked Phalarope, Pectoral Sandpiper, Buff-breasted Sandpiper, Snowy Owl, Rough-legged Buzzard, Lapland Longspur, Smith's Longspur
  • Also covers: Aklavik, Inuvik, Mackenzie Delta, Tuktoyaktuk
  • Also watch for: Beluga whales calve in the shallow warm water off the delta mouth each July, in one of the largest gatherings of the species anywhere.

Nahanni and the Dehcho

A river canyon system cut a kilometre deep, with hot springs and a waterfall twice the height of Niagara

The South Nahanni runs through four canyons up to 1,200 m deep and over a waterfall of about 96 m, which is Naili Cho or Virginia Falls. Warm springs along the river keep patches of ground unfrozen and support plants that have no business being at this latitude, and the birds follow the same pattern.

  • Habitat: River canyon and cliff, whitewater and waterfall, boreal spruce forest, alpine tundra on the plateau, and hot spring and tufa terrace.
  • When: Late June to early August, which is the whole river season.
  • Getting there: Fort Simpson is the gateway and has a small airport. The park is reached only by float plane or on a licensed river trip, permits are required and numbers are capped, so book the season ahead.
  • Base yourself in: Fort Simpson
  • Look for: Golden Eagle, Bald Eagle, Peregrine Falcon, American Dipper, Harlequin Duck, Barrow's Goldeneye, Varied Thrush, Spruce Grouse, Northern Hawk-Owl, Boreal Chickadee, Canada Jay, Blackpoll Warbler
  • Also covers: Fort Simpson, Liard River, Nahanni National Park Reserve, Virginia Falls
  • Also watch for: Tufa mounds at the Rabbitkettle hot springs are the largest in Canada, and the springs keep an unfrozen microclimate through the winter.

Great Bear Lake and the Sahtu

The largest lake lying entirely within one country, at the edge of the treeline

Great Bear Lake is the largest lake lying wholly inside one country and takes up a sixth of the Northwest Territories, and it sits right on the treeline, so its northern and eastern shores are tundra and its southern shore is forest. There is no road to any of it. Norman Wells on the Mackenzie is the practical base.

  • Habitat: Deep cold lake and rock shore, treeline transition from spruce to tundra, lichen woodland, and open barrens to the north-east.
  • When: Late June and July. The lake is frozen into June most years and freezes again in November.
  • Getting there: Norman Wells and Deline both have scheduled flights and no road. There is a winter ice road from the south, and everything on the lake itself needs a charter or a local boat.
  • Base yourself in: Norman Wells
  • Look for: Pacific Loon, Red-throated Loon, Arctic Tern, Long-tailed Jaeger, Willow Ptarmigan, Rock Ptarmigan, Gyrfalcon, Golden Eagle, Northern Wheatear, Snow Bunting, American Pipit, Redpoll
  • Also covers: Deline, Great Bear Lake, Norman Wells, Tulita
  • Also watch for: The lake holds one of the least altered large freshwater ecosystems left, and its whole watershed is a UNESCO biosphere reserve run by the Sahtu Dene and Metis.

Frequently Asked Questions

When is the best time to go birding in the Northwest Territories?

June and the first half of July. The territory runs from aspen parkland to arctic coast and every one of those biomes has its birds on the ground for about six weeks. Outside that window most of it is frozen and much of it is unreachable.

Can I see Whooping Cranes?

In flight, yes. The nesting area in the north-east corner of Wood Buffalo is closed and monitored from the air, which is how the population was found in the first place. The birds pass over the park roads and Fort Smith, and the park staff know where they have been showing.

What is at Fort Smith?

The most northerly nesting colony of American White Pelicans in the world, on rocks in the Slave River rapids at the edge of the town, with viewing platforms above them. The rapids themselves are dangerous and the platforms are the only sensible way to watch.

How do I reach the Mackenzie Delta?

Fly to Inuvik, or drive the Dempster Highway, which crosses into the Northwest Territories from the Yukon. The road on to Tuktoyaktuk opened in 2017 and was the first in Canada to reach the Arctic Ocean; it is 138 km of gravel and open all year. The delta channels themselves need a boat or a float plane.

How much can I do with a hire car?

Less than the map suggests. The Ingraham Trail out of Yellowknife and the roads around Fort Smith and Inuvik are the practical driving. Nahanni, Great Bear Lake and everything east of Great Slave need a charter flight or a licensed river trip.

Can I bird here with limited mobility?

Sites on this page differ from each other more than any rating could carry, so this is the framework the standard British guides use, and the reserve's own page and warden hold the answers.

  • Permission · some reserves open ground to disabled visitors that is closed to everyone else, and it is granted by asking.
  • Priority · parking bays, hides and the nearest paths are often held for disabled visitors. Ask what is reserved and how to claim it.
  • Parking · how close a car reaches the birds shapes the whole day. Ring ahead for the nearest hard standing to the water or the hide.
  • Paths · surfaces, gradients and gate widths vary inside a single reserve. Ask which trail is boarded, surfaced or level.
  • Perches · a hide with a low window, or a bench where the view opens, turns a viewpoint into a morning.

Every reserve above with its own link carries its access detail there, and a warden answers what a page cannot.
