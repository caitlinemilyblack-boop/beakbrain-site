Birding in Rhode Island

Block Island sits twelve miles off the Rhode Island coast, so a songbird crossing water at night comes down on it at first light with no other land in reach, and about forty per cent of the island is protected.

Birds that decide the trip

Harlequin Duck at Sachuest Point

A wintering flock works the surf against the rocks below the refuge trail, and it is the most reliable place in southern New England for a duck that otherwise wants northern coasts.

When December to March, in the surf off the point.

Where Sachuest Point and the east bay

Connecticut Warbler on Block Island

One of the hardest warblers to see anywhere becomes a realistic autumn expectation on an island where a migrant that has run out of land has nowhere to hide.

When Mid-September to early October, in low cover at dawn.

Where Block Island

Piping Plover on the south shore

It nests on open sand above the tideline behind rope and warden watch, and Rhode Island beaches carry a meaningful share of the southern New England population.

When April to August, on roped beach sections.

Where The south shore ponds

Saltmarsh Sparrow behind the barrier beach

It nests just above ordinary high tides and loses nests to the higher ones, which makes the salt ponds behind the barrier beaches some of the most closely watched marsh in the state.

When May to August, in high salt marsh.

Where The south shore ponds, Sachuest Point and the east bay

Purple Sandpiper on the point rocks

It winters on wave-washed rock and this state has very little of it, so the flock at Sachuest is concentrated onto the few ledges that stay exposed.

When November to April, on the surf-washed ledges.

Where Sachuest Point and the east bay

Canada Warbler in the north-western hemlock

It breeds in wet thickets under hemlock, and the higher and colder corner in the north-west is the only part of the state that offers that.

When Late May to July, in hemlock swamp and stream thickets.

Where Arcadia and the northwest

Snowy Owl on the barrier beaches

In an irruption winter the open dune and the airport grass take birds that sit in plain sight for days at a time, and the state has several traditional wintering spots.

When November to March in an irruption winter, on dune and open grass.

Where The south shore ponds, Block Island

American Oystercatcher on the breachways

It breeds on the sand and shell at the breachway mouths and gathers into flocks on the flats before moving south. It calls loudly enough to be found by ear.

When April to September, on the breachway sand and flats.

Where The south shore ponds, Sachuest Point and the east bay

When to Go

Rhode Island is small enough that a week covers all of it, and its two strongest seasons are autumn on Block Island and winter on the rocky headlands. Summer is quieter, though the salt ponds hold their nesting terns and plovers.

The beach nesting season

May to July

Piping Plovers and Least Terns hold the barrier beaches and the salt marsh sparrows are singing.

Go for Trustom and Ninigret, and Napatree at the western end.

Trade off The beaches fill with people and parking is expensive.

Typical daily average

The Block Island passage

Mid-September to October

Southbound migrants come down on the island at first light, in numbers the mainland never matches.

Go for Clay Head and Rodmans Hollow, after a north-west wind.

Trade off The ferry and the island accommodation both book out in September.

Typical daily average

The sea duck winter

December to March

Harlequin Ducks work the rocks at Sachuest and the salt ponds hold sea duck and loons.

Go for Sachuest Point, and the south shore breachways.

Trade off The woods are quiet and the island ferry runs a thin winter timetable.

Typical daily average

The breeding season inland

Late April to July

The north-western woods hold Canada Warbler, Blackburnian and Northern Waterthrush.

Go for Buck Hill and George Washington at dawn.

Trade off The coast is between passages and the ponds are quiet.

Block Island in October

Providence (PVD), New Shoreham 3 nights, back to Providence

Three days on the island in the first half of October, ideally after a night of north-west wind. Hire a bike, work Clay Head and the hollows at first light, and expect sparrows and warblers in bayberry thicket that would be hard work anywhere on the mainland.

The whole state

Providence (PVD), Chepachet 1 night, Charlestown 2 nights, New Shoreham 2 nights, back to Providence

Five days in late May. Woods first, then the coast and the island: Canada Warbler in the north-western hemlock, nesting terns on the salt ponds, and a couple of nights on Block Island for the last of the spring passage. Very little driving.

Block Island

Twelve miles offshore, and one of the best autumn migrant traps on the whole coast

Block Island sits far enough out that a songbird crossing water at night comes down on it at first light with no other land in reach. About forty per cent of the island is protected, and the thickets and stone-walled fields hold birds in numbers the mainland shore never matches.

  • Habitat: Bayberry and shadbush thicket, stone-walled grassland and old pasture, freshwater ponds and marsh, clay bluff and beach, and open ocean around.
  • When: Mid-September to October for the passage, which peaks in the first half of October after a north-west wind.
  • Getting there: Providence is the airport and the ferry runs from Point Judith year round, about an hour. Bikes are the usual way round the island, accommodation books out in September, and the refuge and Clay Head trails are free.
  • Base yourself in: New Shoreham
  • Look for: Connecticut Warbler, Cape May Warbler, Blackpoll Warbler, Philadelphia Vireo, Dickcissel, Lincoln's Sparrow, Merlin, Peregrine Falcon, Northern Harrier, American Woodcock, Snowy Owl, Northern Gannet
  • Parks and reserves: Block Island National Wildlife Refuge, Rodman's Hollow Nature Preserve, Boiling Spring (Protected area), Hodge Preserve
  • Also covers: Block Island National Wildlife Refuge, Boiling Spring, Clay Head, Hodge Preserve, Rodman's Hollow Nature Preserve, Rodmans Hollow
  • Also watch for: The Rhode Island island has no native mammals larger than a mouse, and it holds the American burying beetle, reintroduced after it vanished from the mainland.

The south shore ponds

A chain of salt ponds behind barrier beaches, each one breached to the sea at a different rate

The south shore is a line of coastal salt ponds cut off from the sea by barrier beaches, with breachways letting the tide in at intervals. Trustom is the only undeveloped salt pond left in the state, and Napatree at the western end is a sand spit that hooks round into a lagoon.

  • Habitat: Coastal salt pond and breachway, barrier beach and dune, salt marsh and tidal flat, coastal shrub and bayberry, and glacial moraine woodland behind.
  • When: May to July for the beach nesters, August to October for the shorebird passage, and December to March for sea duck and owls.
  • Getting there: Providence is the airport, forty minutes north, and Charlestown the base. Trustom and Ninigret are free refuges with trails and observation platforms, and Napatree is a walk out along the spit from Watch Hill.
  • Base yourself in: Charlestown
  • Look for: Piping Plover, American Oystercatcher, Least Tern, Saltmarsh Sparrow, Seaside Sparrow, Clapper Rail, Glossy Ibis, Snowy Egret, Long-tailed Duck, Red-throated Loon, Northern Harrier, Snowy Owl
  • Parks and reserves: Trustom Pond National Wildlife Refuge, Ninigret National Wildlife Refuge, Great Swamp Management Area (Protected area), Carolina Management Area (Protected area), Burlingame Management Area (Protected area)
  • Also covers: Burlingame Management Area, Carolina Management Area, Great Swamp Management Area, Napatree Point, Ninigret National Wildlife Refuge, Quonochontaug Pond, Trustom Pond National Wildlife Refuge
  • Also watch for: Osprey nest on platforms around every pond and diamondback terrapin reach their northern limit on these marshes.

Sachuest Point and the east bay

A rocky headland at the mouth of Narragansett Bay, with Harlequin Ducks on the rocks below it

Sachuest Point sticks out into the ocean at the eastern side of the bay mouth, and the surf against its rocks holds a wintering Harlequin Duck flock that is the most reliable in southern New England. The sanctuary behind it has ridges of pudding-stone with thicket between them.

  • Habitat: Rocky headland and surf-washed ledge, coastal shrub and bayberry, salt marsh behind the beach, and pudding-stone ridges with thicket inland.
  • When: December to March for the Harlequin Ducks and sea birds, and May to July for the marsh and shrub breeders.
  • Getting there: Providence is the airport, forty minutes north, and Middletown the base. Sachuest is a free refuge with a loop trail round the point, and the Norman Bird Sanctuary charges a small admission.
  • Base yourself in: Middletown
  • Look for: Harlequin Duck, Purple Sandpiper, Common Eider, Long-tailed Duck, Black Scoter, Red-throated Loon, Great Cormorant, Snowy Owl, Northern Harrier, American Oystercatcher, Osprey, Saltmarsh Sparrow
  • Parks and reserves: Norman Bird Sanctuary (Reserve), Sachuest Point National Wildlife Refuge, Narragansett Bay National Estuarine Research Reserve, Weetamoo Woods (Protected area), Pocasset Ridge Conservation Area (Protected area)
  • Also covers: Narragansett Bay National Estuarine Research Reserve, Norman Bird Sanctuary, Pocasset Ridge Conservation Area, Sachuest Point National Wildlife Refuge, Third Beach, Weetamoo Woods
  • Also watch for: Harbour seals haul out on the rocks off the point through the winter and are watched from the refuge trail.

Arcadia and the northwest

The quiet forested corner, holding breeding warblers that never reach the salt ponds

The north-western corner is the highest and coldest part of a very low state, and its forest, beaver ponds and reverting farmland hold breeding warblers that the Rhode Island coastal plain does not. Buck Hill on the northern line is the most northern of it in feel.

  • Habitat: Mixed hardwood and hemlock on low hills, beaver pond and swamp, reverting pasture and old field, and small reservoirs.
  • When: Late April to July for the breeding birds, and October and November for the woodcock passage on the old fields.
  • Getting there: Providence is the airport, thirty minutes south-east, and Chepachet the base. All three management areas are free with gravel roads and marked trails, and they are hunted in autumn so check the season.
  • Base yourself in: Chepachet
  • Look for: Canada Warbler, Blackburnian Warbler, Black-throated Blue Warbler, Northern Waterthrush, Winter Wren, Hermit Thrush, Veery, Northern Saw-whet Owl, Barred Owl, Ruffed Grouse, American Woodcock, Blue-winged Warbler
  • Parks and reserves: Arcadia Management Area (Protected area), George Washington Management Area (Protected area), Buck Hill Management Area (Protected area), Scituate Reservoir Protection Area (Protected area), Big River Management Area (Protected area)
  • Also covers: Arcadia Management Area, Big River Management Area, Buck Hill Management Area, George Washington Management Area, Scituate Reservoir Protection Area
  • Also watch for: Beaver have reshaped most of the watercourses in this corner of Rhode Island, and black bear now wander in from the states either side.

Frequently Asked Questions

When is the best time to go birding in Rhode Island?

The first half of October on Block Island, after a night of north-west wind. January is the other date, for the Harlequin Ducks at Sachuest Point and the sea duck on the salt ponds.

How do I get to Block Island?

The ferry from Point Judith runs all year and takes about an hour, with a faster seasonal service. Bikes are the usual way round once you are there, accommodation books out in September, and about forty per cent of the island is protected land with free access.

Why does a twelve-mile crossing matter so much?

A songbird migrating at night over water and finding land at dawn will come down on it whatever the habitat. Block Island is the first land many of them meet, so its thickets and stone-walled fields hold concentrations the mainland shore does not.

Where do I find Harlequin Duck?

Sachuest Point, December to March. The refuge loop trail runs round the headland and the birds work the surf against the rocks below it, usually with Purple Sandpipers and Common Eiders in the same view.

Is there anything inland?

The north-western corner, which is the highest and coldest part of a very low state. Its hemlock swamps and beaver ponds hold Canada Warbler, Blackburnian and Northern Waterthrush, none of which breed on the coastal plain.

Can I bird here with limited mobility?

Sites on this page differ from each other more than any rating could carry, so this is the framework the standard British guides use, and the reserve's own page and warden hold the answers.

  • Permission · some reserves open ground to disabled visitors that is closed to everyone else, and it is granted by asking.
  • Priority · parking bays, hides and the nearest paths are often held for disabled visitors. Ask what is reserved and how to claim it.
  • Parking · how close a car reaches the birds shapes the whole day. Ring ahead for the nearest hard standing to the water or the hide.
  • Paths · surfaces, gradients and gate widths vary inside a single reserve. Ask which trail is boarded, surfaced or level.
  • Perches · a hide with a low window, or a bench where the view opens, turns a viewpoint into a morning.
