Birding in Namibia

Dune, gravel plain and tropical river in one country, with a set of endemics that live in the driest part of it.

At a glance

Best months
  • January to April for the birds, when the rains bring the intra-African migrants in and the desert larks sing
  • July to October for the dry season, when the roads are perfect and game concentrates on the waterholes
Regions
The Namib: Sossusvlei and the NaukluftWalvis Bay and the central coastThe Erongo, Brandberg and DamaralandWaterberg and the central highlandsEtosha and the Grootfontein woodlandThe Kavango and the ZambeziThe south: Fish River Canyon and the Orange
Routes
The Desert and the Coast8 daysEtosha and the Northern Highlands10 daysThe Classic Namibia Loop14 daysThe Kavango and the Zambezi8 daysThe South and the Orange6 daysNamibia End to End21 days
Getting in
Windhoek Hosea Kutako (WDH) takes the international flights and is 45 km east of the city. Every itinerary here starts and ends there.
Getting around
Self-drive, and the main routes are tarred. A saloon car covers the classic loop, and a 4x4 is worth it for the Kavango sand tracks and the deep sand near the coast.

When to Go

Namibia inverts the usual advice, and the inversion is the single most useful thing to know before booking. The rains fall from January to April, and that is when the desert larks sing, the palearctic waders are still on the coast, the intra-African migrants are in, and the Zambezi floodplains fill. It is also when the gravel roads wash out and Etosha's game disperses away from the waterholes. From June to October the roads are perfect and the game viewing is the best in Africa, and the birding is at its quietest. Most visitors come in the dry season. Most birders should not.

Peak

January to April

The rains. The desert larks sing, the Zambezi floodplains fill, the intra-African migrants are in and the palearctic waders are still on the coast.

Go for Dune Lark singing on the Kuiseb dunes, the Zambezi wetlands at full flood, and the Namib in green after a storm.

Trade off Gravel roads wash out, the far north can be cut off for days, and Etosha's game scatters away from the waterholes.

The rains have stopped and the country has not yet dried out. Roads are good and the woodland is still calling.

Trade off The quietest birding of the year. No migrants, little song, and the desert is bare.

The Namib: Sossusvlei and the Naukluft

The oldest desert on Earth, and the only place the country's one true endemic lives

Sossusvlei's dunes are the picture everyone arrives with, and the bird that matters here is easy to miss beside them. Dune Lark lives in the grassed dune hollows of the Kuiseb and nowhere else in the world, and it is the only species whose entire range sits inside Namibia. The gravel plains between the dunes and the escarpment hold Gray's Lark and Tractrac Chat, the pans hold Rüppell's Korhaan, and Ludwig's Bustard walks the open ground east towards the Naukluft. Spreetshoogte Pass drops eighteen hundred metres off the escarpment in ten kilometres and is worth the detour for the transition alone.

  • Habitat: Linear and star dunes, gravel plains, ephemeral riverbeds with camelthorn, and the Naukluft escarpment behind.
  • When: January to April, when the plains green and the larks sing. The dunes are worth seeing in any month, but a silent Dune Lark is a hard bird.
  • Getting there: Sesriem is a five hour drive from Windhoek on good gravel, and the last stretch into Sossusvlei needs four-wheel drive or the park shuttle. The Kuiseb riverbed for Dune Lark is reached from the Walvis Bay road rather than from Sesriem.
  • Base yourself in: Sesriem or Solitaire, both of which put the dune road within an hour before sunrise.
  • Look for: Dune Lark, Gray's Lark, Tractrac Chat, Rüppell's Korhaan, Ludwig's Bustard, Namaqua Sandgrouse, Stark's Lark, Burchell's Courser, Pale Chanting Goshawk
  • Also recorded in the parks here: Black-eared Sparrow-Lark
  • National park: Namib-Naukluft National Park
  • Stops:
    • : The dune road, best entered at gate opening.
    • : The grassed dune hollows where Dune Lark lives.
    • : Eighteen hundred metres of escarpment in ten kilometres.
  • Also covers: Kuiseb River, Namib-Naukluft National Park, Sesriem, Solitaire, Sossusvlei, Spreetshoogte Pass
  • Also watch for: Oryx, springbok and Hartmann's mountain zebra on the escarpment, and brown hyena tracks on the plains.

Walvis Bay and the central coast

A lagoon that holds tens of thousands of flamingos against a desert that gets no rain at all

Walvis Bay lagoon is a Ramsar wetland of international importance, and the Benguela current makes this coast one of the richest stretches of water in Africa and one of the driest strips of land, and the two meet at Walvis Bay lagoon. Lesser Flamingo and Greater Flamingo hold the shallows in numbers that run into tens of thousands, with Great White Pelican, Cape Teal and Pied Avocet among them. Damara Tern breeds on the gravel just inland and is the reason many birders come. The rocky sections north towards Swakopmund hold Bank Cormorant, Crowned Cormorant and African Oystercatcher, and the palearctic waders are on the mudflats from September to April.

  • Habitat: Tidal lagoon and saltworks, rocky shore, and the gravel plains of the Namib immediately inland.
  • When: November to March for the palearctic waders at their peak and for breeding Damara Tern. Flamingo numbers hold most of the year.
  • Getting there: Walvis Bay is four hours from Windhoek on tar and thirty minutes from Swakopmund. The lagoon is walkable from town and the saltworks road is public.
  • Base yourself in: Walvis Bay for the lagoon at first light, or Swakopmund for the rocky coast and more to eat.
  • Look for: Lesser Flamingo, Greater Flamingo, Damara Tern, Great White Pelican, Cape Cormorant, Bank Cormorant, Crowned Cormorant, African Oystercatcher, Hartlaub's Gull, Cape Teal, Pied Avocet, Curlew Sandpiper, Ruddy Turnstone, Common Ringed Plover, Caspian Tern, Cape Gannet, Gray's Lark
  • Parks and reserves: Walvis Bay Lagoon Ramsar Site
  • Stops:
    • : Flamingos and waders, walkable from town.
    • : Public road through the pans.
    • : Seals, terns and the outer lagoon.
  • Also covers: Pelican Point, Sandwich Harbour, Swakopmund, Walvis Bay Lagoon Ramsar Site, Walvis Bay Salt Works
  • Also watch for: Cape fur seals at Pelican Point, and Heaviside's dolphin close inshore.

The Erongo, Brandberg and Damaraland

Granite inselbergs holding almost every bird that is only found in this corner of Africa

This is the endemic country. The granite of the Erongo and the Brandberg, and the broken hills running north into Damaraland, hold the birds that bring people to Namibia in the first place: Hartlaub's Spurfowl calling off the boulders at dawn, Herero Chat on the scrubby slopes, Monteiro's Hornbill and Damara Red-billed Hornbill in the camelthorn, and White-tailed Shrike, Carp's Tit and Rockrunner in the same hour if the morning goes well. Rüppell's Parrot comes to water, Bare-cheeked Babbler works the thicket, and further north towards the Kunene the palms hold Rufous-tailed Palm Thrush and the riverine scrub Cinderella Waxbill.

  • Habitat: Granite inselbergs and boulder slopes, camelthorn on the dry riverbeds, and mopane running north into Damaraland.
  • When: January to April for song and for the waxbills, but the resident endemics are here all year and the dry months make the waterholes predictable.
  • Getting there: Omaruru and Uis are three to four hours from Windhoek on tar and good gravel. The Erongo lodges sit on private ground with their own waterholes, which is how most of these birds are actually seen.
  • Base yourself in: Omaruru for the Erongo, or Uis for the Brandberg and the road north.
  • Look for: Hartlaub's Spurfowl, Herero Chat, Monteiro's Hornbill, Damara Red-billed Hornbill, White-tailed Shrike, Carp's Tit, Rockrunner, Rüppell's Parrot, Bare-cheeked Babbler, Rosy-faced Lovebird, Rufous-tailed Palm Thrush, Cinderella Waxbill, Verreaux's Eagle, Bradfield's Swift, Crimson-breasted Shrike, Violet-eared Waxbill
  • Parks and reserves: Brandberg National Monument, Spitzkoppe Community Conservancy
  • Stops:
    • : Hartlaub's Spurfowl and the granite endemics.
    • : Herero Chat on the lower slopes.
    • : Granite domes with the same suite and easier access.
  • Also covers: Brandberg National Monument, Erongo Mountains, Omaruru, Spitzkoppe Community Conservancy, Tandala Ridge, Uis
  • Also watch for: Desert-adapted elephant on the Ugab and Huab riverbeds, and black rhino on the Damaraland conservancies.

Waterberg and the central highlands

A sandstone plateau standing six hundred metres above the thornveld, with its own set of birds on top

The Waterberg is a single block of sandstone lying across the highveld, and the difference between the plateau and the plain below is enough to change the bird list. Rockrunner works the boulder slopes on the way up, Bradfield's Swift screams along the cliff face, and Verreaux's Eagle holds the escarpment. The broadleaved woodland on top carries Rüppell's Parrot and Bennett's Woodpecker. Closer to Windhoek, Daan Viljoen sits twenty minutes from the capital and is the easiest morning in the country: Rockrunner, Pririt Batis, Sociable Weaver and Scaly-feathered Weaver without leaving the city's water catchment.

  • Habitat: Sandstone escarpment and plateau broadleaved woodland, over acacia thornveld and highland grassland.
  • When: January to April for song and breeding. June and July nights drop close to freezing on the plateau.
  • Getting there: Waterberg is three hours north of Windhoek on tar. The plateau itself is reached on foot up a marked path from the rest camp, or by park vehicle. Daan Viljoen is twenty minutes west of Windhoek.
  • Base yourself in: The Waterberg rest camp, or Windhoek if Daan Viljoen is the target.
  • Look for: Rockrunner, Bradfield's Swift, Verreaux's Eagle, Rüppell's Parrot, Bennett's Woodpecker, Pririt Batis, Sociable Weaver, Scaly-feathered Weaver, Red-billed Spurfowl, Pale Chanting Goshawk, Black-chested Snake Eagle, Grey Go-away-bird
  • National park:
    • Waterberg Plateau Park, Escarpment path for Rockrunner and Bradfield's Swift.
  • Stops:
    • : Twenty minutes from Windhoek, and the easiest Rockrunner in the country.
  • Also covers: Daan Viljoen Game Park, Otjiwarongo, Waterberg Plateau Park, Windhoek
  • Also watch for: Sable and roan on the plateau, both introduced, and leopard on the escarpment.

Etosha and the Grootfontein woodland

A salt pan the size of a small country, ringed by the waterholes its game and birds must come to

Etosha works on a simple mechanism: the pan itself is dead ground, and the waterholes around its southern and eastern edge are where everything appears. For birds that means Kori Bustard and Secretarybird walking the open plain, Lappet-faced Vulture on the kills, Common Ostrich in numbers, and after rain a pan holding flamingos in thousands when it floods. The mopane and thicket on the park's fringes carry Bare-cheeked Babbler and Black-faced Babbler, the latter reliable in the Grootfontein woodland east of the park and hard almost everywhere else.

  • Habitat: Saline pan, open plains, mopane woodland, and dry thicket on the eastern fringe.
  • When: June to October for game and for concentrated waterhole activity. January to April if the pan floods, which is when the flamingos come and the birding is at its best.
  • Getting there: Okaukuejo is five hours from Windhoek on tar. All main routes inside the park are saloon-car gravel, and visitors must be inside a camp by sunset.
  • Base yourself in: Okaukuejo or Halali inside the park, both with floodlit waterholes.
  • Look for: Kori Bustard, Secretarybird, Lappet-faced Vulture, Common Ostrich, Bare-cheeked Babbler, Black-faced Babbler, Southern Ground Hornbill, Lesser Flamingo, Greater Flamingo, Bateleur, Red-necked Falcon, African Hawk-Eagle, Crimson-breasted Shrike, Rufous-bellied Tit
  • National park: Etosha National Park
  • Stops:
    • : Floodlit through the night.
    • : Concession on the park's western boundary.
    • : Grootfontein thicket, and the Black-faced Babbler site.
  • Also covers: Etosha National Park, Grootfontein, Halali, Hobatere, Namutoni, Okaukuejo waterhole
  • Also watch for: Elephant, lion, black rhino at the floodlit waterholes, and the largest population of black-faced impala anywhere.

The Kavango and the Zambezi

The wet corner of a dry country, and a bird list that belongs to central Africa rather than the Namib

The strip running east from Rundu to Katima Mulilo carries perennial rivers, and with them a list that has almost nothing in common with the rest of the country. African Skimmer cuts the Okavango at Popa Falls, Slaty Egret and Rufous-bellied Heron work the floodplain, and Coppery-tailed Coucal, Swamp Boubou, Hartlaub's Babbler and Chirping Cisticola hold the papyrus. Pel's Fishing Owl is in the riverine forest at Mahango for those who work for it. The broadleaved woodland behind the rivers carries Racket-tailed Roller, Souza's Shrike, Bradfield's Hornbill, Arnot's Chat and Sharp-tailed Starling, and Southern Carmine Bee-eater colonies open in the riverbanks from September.

  • Habitat: Perennial river, papyrus and reed floodplain, riverine forest, and Kalahari broadleaved woodland behind.
  • When: September to April. The carmine colonies open in September, the migrants arrive in October and the floodplain is fullest from February.
  • Getting there: Rundu is seven hours from Windhoek on tar, Katima Mulilo four hours further east. The main road is tar the whole way and the park tracks need four-wheel drive when wet.
  • Base yourself in: Divundu for Mahango and Popa Falls, or Katima Mulilo for the eastern floodplains.
  • Look for: African Skimmer, Slaty Egret, Rufous-bellied Heron, Coppery-tailed Coucal, Swamp Boubou, Hartlaub's Babbler, Chirping Cisticola, Luapula Cisticola, Long-toed Lapwing, Wattled Crane, Pel's Fishing Owl, Racket-tailed Roller, Souza's Shrike, Bradfield's Hornbill, Arnot's Chat, Sharp-tailed Starling, Southern Carmine Bee-eater, Meyer's Parrot, Broad-billed Roller, African Golden Oriole, Grey Kestrel, African Fish Eagle, African Wood Owl
  • National parks: Bwabwata National Park, Nkasa Rupara National Park
  • Stops:
    • : Riverine forest and floodplain in one small park.
    • : Rapids on the Okavango, and African Skimmer.
    • : Eastern floodplain and the Zambezi itself.
  • Also covers: Bwabwata National Park, Divundu, Katima Mulilo, Mahango Game Reserve, Nkasa Rupara National Park, Popa Falls, Rundu
  • Also watch for: Hippo and crocodile throughout, sitatunga in the Linyanti reeds, and elephant moving between here and Botswana.

The south: Fish River Canyon and the Orange

The Karoo's low grey shrubland, the second largest canyon in the world, and a river that is the border

Namibia's south is the thinnest part of the country for birds and the easiest to skip, which is a mistake if the Karoo species are missing from the list. The scrub around Keetmanshoop and the canyon rim holds Karoo Chat, Layard's Warbler, Rufous-eared Warbler and Karoo Long-billed Lark, with Sclater's Lark on the gravel for those who look properly. Ludwig's Bustard walks the plains. On the Orange River at the border, Orange River White-eye works the riverine scrub, and Bokmakierie and Cape Sparrow here are at the northern edge of a range that belongs to South Africa.

  • Habitat: Nama Karoo dwarf shrubland, the low grey scrub that reaches its northern limit in southern Namibia, with quiver tree slopes, canyon rim and riverine scrub on the Orange.
  • When: January to April, when the Karoo larks sing and the scrub is in flower. Winter mornings here are the coldest in Namibia.
  • Getting there: Keetmanshoop is five hours south of Windhoek on tar, and the Fish River Canyon viewpoints are two hours further on gravel. The canyon hiking trail is closed in summer.
  • Base yourself in: Keetmanshoop for the Karoo birds, or Hobas for the canyon rim at dawn.
  • Look for: Karoo Chat, Layard's Warbler, Rufous-eared Warbler, Karoo Long-billed Lark, Sclater's Lark, Ludwig's Bustard, Orange River White-eye, Bokmakierie, Cape Sparrow, Namaqua Sandgrouse, Spike-heeled Lark, Sociable Weaver, Rock Kestrel, Booted Eagle
  • National park: Ai-Ais
  • Stops:
    • : Hobas viewpoint, best at first light.
    • : Keetmanshoop, and the Karoo scrub around it.
    • : Riverine scrub where Namibia meets South Africa across the water.
  • Also covers: Ai-Ais, Aus, Fish River Canyon, Hobas, Keetmanshoop, Noordoewer, Orange River, Quiver Tree Forest
  • Also watch for: Hartmann's mountain zebra on the canyon slopes, and klipspringer on the rim.

Frequently Asked Questions

When is the best time to go birding in Namibia?

January to April. The rains bring the intra-African migrants in, the desert larks sing, the Zambezi floodplains fill and the Palearctic waders are still on the coast. July to October is the classic dry season, when the roads are perfect and the game concentrates on the Etosha waterholes, and the bird list is shorter for it.

How many bird species does Namibia have?

Around 689 have been recorded. That is modest against Kenya or South Africa, and the point of the country is which ones: the arid west holds a set of endemics and near-endemics that live nowhere else, and the northern rivers add a tropical list on top.

Can I drive Namibia myself?

Yes, and most birders do. The main routes are tarred, the gravel roads are graded and signposted, and a saloon car reaches Sossusvlei, Walvis Bay, the Erongo and Etosha. A 4x4 earns its keep on the Kavango sand tracks and in deep sand near the coast.

Which birds should I plan the trip around?

Dune Lark is the country's only true endemic and it holds the Namib sand. Herero Chat, Monteiro's Hornbill, Rockrunner, White-tailed Shrike, Hartlaub's Spurfowl and Ruppell's Parrot are the escarpment set, and Damara Tern breeds on the coast. The Zambezi adds Pel's Fishing Owl, African Skimmer and Slaty Egret.

How long do I need?

Ten days covers the desert, the coast and Etosha. Two weeks does the classic loop without hurrying. Adding the Kavango and the Zambezi takes it to three, because the drive north and back is most of a week on its own.

Is Etosha worth several nights?

Yes, and the reason is the camps rather than the pan. Okaukuejo, Halali and Namutoni each have a floodlit waterhole open all night, so a night inside the park is a different set of birds and mammals from a day drive through it.

What makes the Caprivi different?

Everything. The strip is flat broad-leaved woodland cut by tropical rivers, and the daily tallies in summer approach 300 species against a fraction of that in the desert. It is the only part of the country where the Okavango and Zambezi birds occur.

Where should I look for the Namib endemics?

The tarred road to Sossusvlei crosses gravel plains that hold Namaqua Sandgrouse, Ludwig's Bustard and both Burchell's and Double-banded Coursers, and early morning is when they move. Dune Lark wants the sand itself, and the Kuiseb riverbed is the usual place to walk in.

Do I need a guide?

For the standard loop, no. A local guide is worth it for Herero Chat and Angola Cave Chat, both of which take specific escarpment sites and a lot of patience, and for the Zambezi rivers, where a boat reaches what a road does not.

Birding here with limited mobility

Namibia is unusually workable. Etosha is a self-drive park where the waterholes are approached by car and the camp hides are level, Walvis Bay lagoon is watched from a tarred road, and the Sossusvlei gravel plains are birded from roadside pull-ins. The escarpment sites at Spitzkoppe and the Erongo want rough walking.
