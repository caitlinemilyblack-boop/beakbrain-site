Birding in California

California runs eleven hundred kilometres from the Oregon line to the border and holds more birds than any other state, because the Pacific Flyway, the Sierra crest and two deserts all end inside it.

Birds that decide the trip

Bald Eagles over the Klamath Basin

The largest winter gathering of Bald Eagles in the lower forty-eight sits on the Klamath basins, drawn by the waterfowl that winter there. They roost in the timber above the refuges and come out onto the ice at first light.

When December to February, when the birds are concentrated and the marshes are frozen.

Where Tule Lake National Wildlife Refuge, Lower Klamath National Wildlife Refuge

Two million grebes on Mono Lake

The lake is three times as salty as the sea and grows brine shrimp and alkali flies in quantities no other water on the flyway matches. Almost the entire Pacific population of Black-necked Grebe stops at Mono Lake to fatten before moving south.

When August to October, when the birds gather in their greatest numbers.

Where Mono Lake

A million geese on flooded rice

The Sacramento Valley refuges flood their rice ground each winter for the Pacific Flyway, and the result is one of the densest waterfowl concentrations in North America. The auto tour routes put a car within metres of the flocks.

When November to February, with the peak after the first heavy rains.

Where Sacramento National Wildlife Refuge, Colusa National Wildlife Refuge

Seabirds on the Farallones

The Farallones lie thirty miles outside the Golden Gate and carry the largest seabird colony in the contiguous states. Landing is closed, so the birds are watched from a boat that also crosses deep water on the way out.

When May to July for the breeding colony, and autumn for shearwater passage on the crossing.

Where Farallon Islands, San Francisco Bay

Great Grey Owl at the end of its range

California holds the southernmost Great Grey Owls in the world, a few hundred birds in Sierra meadows. They hunt the meadow edge at dusk and the population is small enough that sites are not published casually.

When June to August, at dusk on the meadow edges once the passes are open.

Where Yosemite National Park, Tahoe National Forest

Ridgway's Rail in the last salt marshes

Almost all of the southern California coastal marsh has gone, and the rail survives in the fragments that are left. Upper Newport Bay holds the largest of them, inside a city, and the birds walk the channel edges on a rising tide.

When All year, and easiest on the highest tides when the marsh floods.

Where Upper Newport Bay Ecological Reserve, San Francisco Bay

Yellow-billed Magpie, which lives only here

The Yellow-billed Magpie is found in the Central Valley and the oak country around it and nowhere else on earth. It is common where it occurs, which makes it one of the easiest endemics in North America to see.

When All year, in valley oak and roadside groves.

Where Sacramento National Wildlife Refuge, Merced National Wildlife Refuge

LeConte's Thrasher in the open desert

One of the hardest common birds in the country to find, living at very low density in saltbush flats with almost no cover. It runs rather than flies, and the way to find one is to be out at dawn in the right flat listening for song.

When February to April, at dawn, while the birds are singing.

Where Salton Sea, Anza-Borrego Desert State Park

When to Go

California has no single birding season. The Central Valley and the Klamath Basin work from October to March when the flyway arrives, the Sierra opens only once the passes clear in June, and the coast holds birds all year with seabird passage strongest in autumn.

The flyway arrives

October to February

Waterfowl come down the Pacific Flyway into the Central Valley and the Klamath Basin in millions.

Go for The Sacramento Valley refuges for geese on flooded rice, Tule Lake for the eagle concentration, and the Salton Sea for wintering waterbirds.

Trade off The Sierra passes are shut and the high country is unreachable.

Typical daily average

Spring on the coast

March to May

Shorebirds move north through the bays and the coastal scrub comes into song.

Go for San Francisco Bay and Elkhorn Slough for waders, Upper Newport Bay for the rails, and the deserts before they get hot.

Trade off The valley refuges empty as the geese leave.

Typical daily average

The high country opens

June to September

The Sierra passes clear and the mountain birds become reachable, while seabird passage builds offshore.

Go for Tioga Pass to Mono Lake, the sequoia groves, and Monterey Bay for shearwaters from a boat.

Trade off The deserts and the valley are very hot and largely birdless by day.

The Klamath Basin and the north-east

The largest winter gathering of Bald Eagles in the lower forty-eight

Four shallow basins straddling the Oregon line take the Pacific Flyway at its narrowest. Waterfowl come through in millions in autumn, and the eagles that follow them make the largest winter concentration anywhere south of Alaska.

  • Habitat: Shallow lakes, flooded marsh and grain stubble in a high basin, with sagebrush and juniper on the rims and timber on the ridges above.
  • When: December to February for the Bald Eagle concentration and the wintering waterfowl. Autumn passage from October is when the flyway numbers peak.
  • Getting there: Redding or Klamath Falls are the practical airports and Tulelake is the base. Both Tule Lake and Lower Klamath have gravel auto tour routes, and the eagle roost counts are watched at dawn from the road.
  • Base yourself in: Tulelake
  • Look for: Bald Eagle, Snow Goose, Ross's Goose, Northern Pintail, Tundra Swan, American Avocet, Wilson's Phalarope, Tricolored Blackbird, Sandhill Crane, Golden Eagle, Rough-legged Buzzard, Short-eared Owl, American White Pelican
  • Also covers: Clear Lake National Wildlife Refuge, Goose Lake, Lower Klamath National Wildlife Refuge, Tule Lake National Wildlife Refuge
  • Also watch for: Mule deer winter on the basin edges and pronghorn range the sagebrush above them.

The north coast and the Klamath forests

Shorebirds on a marsh built out of a sewage works, under redwoods

Humboldt Bay is the largest estuary between San Francisco and the Columbia, and the marsh at Arcata was made from the town's own water treatment. Behind it the Klamath forests climb into old growth where Spotted Owl still holds territory.

  • Habitat: A large estuary with mudflat, salt marsh and treated-water ponds, backed by coastal dune and by redwood and Douglas fir forest inland.
  • When: All year. Shorebird passage peaks in April and again from August, and the forest birds are resident. The coast is wet from October to April.
  • Getting there: Arcata is the base, on Highway 101. The marsh is a walk-in reserve on the edge of town with a car park and level paths, and the Klamath forests run east from it on winding two-lane roads.
  • Base yourself in: Arcata
  • Look for: Marbled Godwit, Black Turnstone, Heermann's Gull, Allen's Hummingbird, Spotted Owl, Hermit Warbler, Marbled Murrelet, Brant Goose, Long-billed Curlew, Wrentit, Chestnut-backed Chickadee, Varied Thrush
  • Also covers: Arcata Marsh, Klamath National Forest
  • Also watch for: Roosevelt elk range the coastal prairies and grey whales pass offshore in winter and spring.

Lassen, Shasta and the southern Cascades

Great Grey Owl at the southern end of its range, in volcanic country

The Cascades end in California at Lassen, a volcano still steaming, and the conifer forest around it carries the state's northern owls. Shasta-Trinity spreads west from it over a great deal of very quiet ground.

  • Habitat: Red fir and lodgepole forest around a live volcano, with hydrothermal basins, wet mountain meadow, aspen stands and manzanita chaparral lower down.
  • When: June to September, once the park road is clear of snow. The owls call from April but the high country is unreachable until early summer.
  • Getting there: Redding is the airport and Chester the base. The Lassen park road crosses the volcano and closes with snow, often from November to June, and the Shasta-Trinity roads west are largely gravel.
  • Base yourself in: Chester
  • Look for: Great Grey Owl, Spotted Owl, Flammulated Owl, Hermit Warbler, Willow Flycatcher, Williamson's Sapsucker, American Three-toed Woodpecker, Clark's Nutcracker, Mountain Chickadee, Pine Grosbeak, Cassin's Finch, American Dipper
  • Also covers: Lassen Volcanic National Park, Plumas National Forest, Shasta-Trinity National Forest
  • Also watch for: Black bear are common and the hydrothermal basins at Lassen steam through the winter.

The Sacramento Valley refuges

A million geese on flooded rice, an hour north of Sacramento

The valley floor is farmed almost end to end, and the refuges strung along it flood their rice ground each winter for the Pacific Flyway. The result is one of the densest waterfowl concentrations in North America, watched from an auto tour route.

  • Habitat: Flooded rice and managed seasonal wetland on a farmed valley floor, with remnant valley oak, riparian willow along the sloughs and vernal pool grassland.
  • When: November to February, after the first heavy rains have flooded the rice. The concentration builds through December and thins in March.
  • Getting there: Sacramento is the airport and Willows the base, an hour and a half north on Interstate 5. Each refuge has a gravel auto tour route driveable in any car, and the birds ignore a stopped vehicle.
  • Base yourself in: Willows
  • Look for: Snow Goose, Ross's Goose, Greater White-fronted Goose, Northern Pintail, American Wigeon, Tundra Swan, Sandhill Crane, Tricolored Blackbird, Western Sandpiper, Nuttall's Woodpecker, Oak Titmouse, Yellow-billed Magpie
  • Also covers: Colusa National Wildlife Refuge, Delevan National Wildlife Refuge, East Park Reservoir, Sacramento National Wildlife Refuge, Sutter National Wildlife Refuge
  • Also watch for: Tule elk were brought back to the valley floor and river otter work the refuge sloughs.

The northern Sierra and Tahoe

Old growth conifer and mountain meadow between the passes

North of Yosemite the Sierra is lower, wetter and far less visited, and the national forests either side of Tahoe hold the same owls and woodpeckers without the queues. The meadows are the thing to find: Willow Flycatcher and Great Grey Owl both want them.

  • Habitat: Mixed conifer of Jeffrey pine, white fir and incense cedar, with wet mountain meadow, aspen, willow carr along the creeks and granite above the treeline.
  • When: June to September, once the passes clear. The meadows are the thing to find and they hold their birds through midsummer.
  • Getting there: Sacramento or Reno are the airports and Truckee the base. Interstate 80 crosses the range and stays open in winter, but the forest roads either side of it do not.
  • Base yourself in: Truckee
  • Look for: Great Grey Owl, Willow Flycatcher, Flammulated Owl, Spotted Owl, Hermit Warbler, Mountain Quail, White-headed Woodpecker, Black-backed Woodpecker, Williamson's Sapsucker, Green-tailed Towhee, Mountain Bluebird, Pine Grosbeak
  • Also covers: Eldorado National Forest, Stanislaus National Forest, Tahoe National Forest
  • Also watch for: Black bear are widespread and the Sierra Nevada yellow-legged frog survives in a few high lakes.

Yosemite, Mono Lake and the eastern Sierra

Two million Eared Grebes on an alkali lake below the Sierra crest

Tioga Pass drops from Yosemite granite to a lake with no outlet, three times as salty as the sea. Mono Lake grows brine shrimp and alkali flies in quantities that stop the entire Pacific population of Eared Grebe on migration, and it holds the largest inland California Gull colony.

  • Habitat: Granite domes and mixed conifer on the western slope, dropping east through Jeffrey pine to sagebrush and to an alkali lake three times as salty as the sea, ringed with tufa towers.
  • When: June to October. Tioga Pass usually opens in late May or June and closes with the first heavy snow. The grebe numbers on the lake peak from August to October.
  • Getting there: Sacramento or Reno are the airports and Lee Vining the base on the east side. Tioga Pass is the only direct crossing and is shut for roughly eight months, which forces a long detour in winter.
  • Base yourself in: Lee Vining
  • Look for: Black-necked Grebe, California Gull, Wilson's Phalarope, Red-necked Phalarope, Sage Thrasher, Great Grey Owl, Sagebrush Sparrow, Pinyon Jay, Clark's Nutcracker, Mountain Bluebird, American Dipper, White-headed Woodpecker
  • Also covers: Mono Lake, Sierra National Forest, Yosemite National Park
  • Also watch for: The lake's tufa towers are exposed carbonate springs, and bighorn sheep were returned to the crest above it.

Sequoia, Kings Canyon and the southern Sierra

The biggest trees on earth, with the high Sierra behind them

The southern Sierra carries the giant sequoia groves and rises straight from them to the highest ground in the contiguous states. The birding follows elevation: oak and chaparral at the bottom, sequoia and fir in the middle, and almost nothing but rock at the top.

  • Habitat: Giant sequoia groves in mixed conifer, with oak woodland and chaparral on the foothills below and alpine rock and meadow on the crest above.
  • When: May to October. The foothills are pleasant from spring, the groves hold their birds all summer, and the high country opens last.
  • Getting there: Fresno is the airport and Three Rivers the base. The Generals Highway climbs from the entrance through the groves and is often chained in winter.
  • Base yourself in: Three Rivers
  • Look for: White-headed Woodpecker, Mountain Quail, Spotted Owl, Flammulated Owl, Hermit Warbler, Clark's Nutcracker, Mountain Chickadee, Pygmy Nuthatch, Green-tailed Towhee, California Thrasher, Wrentit, American Dipper
  • Also covers: Kings Canyon National Park, Sequoia National Forest, Sequoia National Park
  • Also watch for: Black bear are common in the groves and the sequoias themselves are the largest living things by volume.

San Francisco Bay and the Farallones

A seabird island thirty miles offshore, and the bay that feeds the flyway

San Francisco Bay is the largest estuary on the Pacific coast of the Americas and takes shorebirds in hundreds of thousands. The Farallones sit outside the Golden Gate with the largest seabird colony in the contiguous states on them, watched from a boat.

  • Habitat: A very large estuary of mudflat, salt pond and cordgrass marsh, with rocky granite islands thirty miles offshore and deep water on the crossing to them.
  • When: All year. Shorebirds peak on passage in spring and autumn, the island seabird colony runs from May to July, and the crossing is best for seabirds in autumn.
  • Getting there: San Francisco is the airport. Bay shore reserves are reached from the freeways around the estuary, and the Farallones are watched from a boat because landing is closed to protect the colony.
  • Base yourself in: San Francisco
  • Look for: Brandt's Cormorant, Tufted Puffin, Common Murre, Pigeon Guillemot, Heermann's Gull, Black Oystercatcher, Marbled Godwit, Western Sandpiper, Snowy Plover, Ridgway's Rail, Burrowing Owl, California Quail
  • Also covers: Farallon Islands, San Francisco Bay
  • Also watch for: White sharks gather off the Farallones in autumn and grey whales pass through the Gate.

The central coast

Sea otters in a slough, and Monterey Bay dropping to deep water at the shore

Elkhorn Slough cuts inland from the head of a submarine canyon on the central California coast, which is why deep-water seabirds come so close here. The slough itself holds otters and shorebirds, and Morro Bay and the Los Padres ridges add estuary and chaparral.

  • Habitat: A tidal slough cutting inland from the head of a submarine canyon, with salt marsh, mudflat and eelgrass, backed by coastal chaparral, oak and Monterey pine.
  • When: All year. The otters and shorebirds are permanent, deep-water seabirds come closest in autumn, and the chaparral birds sing in spring.
  • Getting there: San Jose or Monterey are the airports and Moss Landing the base. The slough is entered from Highway 1 with a visitor centre and level trails, and the pelagic trips leave from Monterey harbour.
  • Base yourself in: Moss Landing
  • Look for: Black-vented Shearwater, Sooty Shearwater, Marbled Godwit, Long-billed Curlew, Snowy Plover, Black Oystercatcher, Mountain Quail, Wrentit, California Thrasher, Nuttall's Woodpecker, Oak Titmouse, California Quail
  • Also covers: Elkhorn Slough, Los Padres National Forest, Merced National Wildlife Refuge, Morro Bay
  • Also watch for: Sea otters raft in the slough in their hundreds and the canyon brings whales within sight of the beach.

The southern California coast

Ridgway's Rail in the last salt marshes between Los Angeles and the border

Almost all of the southern California coastal wetland is gone, and what survives is a short list of small places carrying birds that live nowhere else in the state. Upper Newport Bay is the largest of them and it sits inside a city.

  • Habitat: Cordgrass salt marsh and tidal channel inside a city, with coastal sage scrub on the bluffs above and estuary mouths cut through the beach.
  • When: All year, with the rails easiest on the highest tides when the marsh floods and pushes them into view. Winter adds the waders.
  • Getting there: Los Angeles or San Diego are the airports. Upper Newport Bay has a paved back-bay road and an interpretive centre, and Mugu Lagoon adjoins a naval base and is largely watched from Highway 1.
  • Base yourself in: Newport Beach
  • Look for: Ridgway's Rail, Snowy Plover, Savannah Sparrow, California Gnatcatcher, Elegant Tern, Least Tern, Black Skimmer, Long-billed Curlew, Marbled Godwit, Wrentit, California Thrasher, Allen's Hummingbird
  • Also covers: Lake Hodges, Mugu Lagoon, Upper Newport Bay Ecological Reserve
  • Also watch for: California ground squirrel colonies feed the Burrowing Owls, and grey whales pass close inshore in winter.

The southern deserts and the Salton Sea

A sea below sea level, and the desert that surrounds it

The Salton Sea was made by accident in 1905 and is now one of the most important inland waterbird sites in North America, shrinking and saltier every year. The deserts around it hold a completely different list within an hour's drive.

  • Habitat: A shrinking hypersaline lake below sea level, with agricultural drains, barnacle-shell shoreline, creosote and saltbush flats, palm oases in the canyons and Joshua tree woodland higher up.
  • When: November to March. The lake holds its waterbird numbers through winter and the desert is comfortable. Summer is dangerous and the shore smells strongly.
  • Getting there: Palm Springs or San Diego are the airports and Brawley the base. The refuge at the south end is reached from Highway 111, Anza-Borrego from Borrego Springs, and Joshua Tree has paved roads through both deserts it straddles.
  • Base yourself in: Brawley
  • Look for: Ridgway's Rail, American White Pelican, Wood Stork, Gull-billed Tern, Burrowing Owl, LeConte's Thrasher, Crissal Thrasher, Bendire's Thrasher, Verdin, Phainopepla, Gambel's Quail, Greater Roadrunner, Costa's Hummingbird
  • Also covers: Anza-Borrego Desert State Park, Joshua Tree National Park, Salton Sea, San Bernardino National Forest
  • Also watch for: Desert bighorn sheep hold the Anza-Borrego canyons and the Joshua trees are a yucca rather than a tree.

Frequently Asked Questions

When is the best time to go birding in California?

It depends which California. October to February is the answer for the Central Valley and the Klamath Basin, when the Pacific Flyway arrives in millions. June to September is the only window for the Sierra, because the passes are shut the rest of the year. The coast holds birds all year and its seabird passage is strongest in autumn.

Which birds live only in California?

Yellow-billed Magpie is found in the Central Valley and the oak country around it and nowhere else on earth, and it is common where it occurs. California Gnatcatcher, California Thrasher, Wrentit, Oak Titmouse, Nuttall’s Woodpecker and Tricolored Blackbird are all effectively Californian too, reaching only a little way into Oregon or Baja.

How long does a first trip need?

A week covers one of the valley, the Sierra, the coast or the south properly. Three weeks is what the whole state needs, and even then the Sierra half only works from June to September.

When does Tioga Pass open?

Usually late May or June and it closes with the first heavy snow, often in November. Everything in the eastern Sierra depends on it: a trip planned for Mono Lake before June has to come the long way round through the desert.

Is the Salton Sea still worth going to?

Yes, and it is changing fast. It was made by accident in 1905, has no outlet, and is shrinking and getting saltier every year, so the fish and the birds that eat them are declining. It remains one of the most important inland waterbird sites in North America and it is worth seeing now.

Can I get to the Farallon Islands?

Not onto them. Landing is closed to protect the colony, so the islands are watched from a boat that runs out from San Francisco, and the crossing itself is often the better half for seabirds.

Do I need a car?

Yes. The state is eleven hundred kilometres end to end, several refuges are reached only on gravel, and no birding region here is served by public transport.

Can I bird here with limited mobility?

Sites on this page differ from each other more than any rating could carry, so this is the framework the standard British guides use, and the reserve's own page and warden hold the answers.

  • Permission · some reserves open ground to disabled visitors that is closed to everyone else, and it is granted by asking.
  • Priority · parking bays, hides and the nearest paths are often held for disabled visitors. Ask what is reserved and how to claim it.
  • Parking · how close a car reaches the birds shapes the whole day. Ring ahead for the nearest hard standing to the water or the hide.
  • Paths · surfaces, gradients and gate widths vary inside a single reserve. Ask which trail is boarded, surfaced or level.
  • Perches · a hide with a low window, or a bench where the view opens, turns a viewpoint into a morning.
