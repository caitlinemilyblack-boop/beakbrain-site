Birding in the Brazilian Amazon

Sixty per cent of the largest rainforest on earth, divided by rivers into blocks of forest that each hold their own birds.

At a glance

Best months
July to November for low water, open trails and the canopy towers; May and June for the flooded forest at its most navigable
Regions
Manaus and the Rio NegroAlta Floresta and the CristalinoThe Tapajos and Amazonia National ParkBelem and the eastern AmazonCarajas and southern ParaThe Roosevelt and the AripuanaRondonia and the MadeiraMamiraua, Tefe and the JapuraMonte Alegre and the north bank
Getting in
Manaus (MAO) for the centre, Belem (BEL) for the east, Santarem (STM) for the Tapajos, Alta Floresta (AFL) for the Cristalino, Porto Velho (PVH) for the south-west
Getting around
Domestic flights between the hubs and a boat from there. Very little of the region is reachable by road

When to Go

The Amazon has a dry season, and it is not the same month everywhere. The southern edge dries hard from June, the centre from July, and Belem at the mouth never really dries at all. What the calendar is really about is water level: the rivers peak months after the rain that filled them, so high water and heavy rain are different seasons.

Peak

July to November

The low-water season across most of the basin. Beaches appear, trails that were under water are walkable, and the rain stops being an all-day event.

Go for Terra firme, the forest above the flood line, with its trails and canopy towers at their most workable, white sand beaches on the Tapajos and the Negro, and far fewer mosquitoes on the black-water rivers.

Trade off The busiest months at the lodges, and the varzea reserves are harder to reach by canoe once the flooded forest has drained.

Typical daily average Manaus 22 to 33 °C / 72 to 91 °F, Alta Floresta 19 to 36 °C / 66 to 97 °F, Belem 23 to 31 °C / 73 to 88 °F

Shoulder

May, June and December

The turn of the water either side of the low season, with the rain easing or just returning.

Go for High water still standing in the varzea, forest flooded each year by white-water rivers, in May and June, which is when Mamiraua is canoed through the canopy, at lower prices than July.

Trade off December is already wet again and the trails go under before the beaches appear.

Typical daily average Manaus 23 to 31 °C / 73 to 88 °F, Alta Floresta 20 to 34 °C / 68 to 93 °F, Belem 23 to 31 °C / 73 to 88 °F

Quiet

January to April

The wet season. Manaus takes about 287 mm in January and Belem 379 mm, and much of the floodplain forest is under several metres of water.

Go for Canopy-level canoeing through flooded igapo, forest flooded by black-water rivers, and varzea, breeding activity, and the lowest prices of the year.

Trade off Terra firme trails flood, river travel gets slower against the current, and an all-day downpour is normal rather than remarkable.

Typical daily average Manaus 23 to 30 °C / 73 to 86 °F, Alta Floresta 21 to 32 °C / 70 to 90 °F, Belem 22 to 30 °C / 72 to 86 °F

Black water, white sand and a canopy tower on a research road, reached from a city of two million

Manaus is the only place in Amazonia where a birder can be in terra firme forest before breakfast and back at an international airport the same evening. Downstream the Anavilhanas archipelago is hundreds of black-water islands, and Presidente Figueiredo's sandstone gorges hold Guianan Cock-of-the-rock two hours north of the city.

The Tapajos and Amazonia National Park

White sand, clear water and the west bank of a river two kilometres across

The Tapajos is a clear-water river with white sand beaches, and the forest either side of it holds a set of birds found between that river and the Madeira and nowhere else. Amazonia National Park sits on the west bank at Itaituba and the Tapajos National Forest covers 549,000 hectares on the east. Santarem, where the Tapajos meets the Amazon, is the way in.

  • Look for: Golden Parakeet, Vulturine Parrot, Bald Parrot, Black-girdled Barbet, Snow-capped Manakin, Dark-winged Trumpeter, Cryptic Forest Falcon, Zimmer's Tody-Tyrant

  • Stops:

Belem and the eastern Amazon

The mouth of the river, and the most threatened forest in Amazonia

Belem sits at the mouth of the Amazon in the Belem area of endemism, which has lost more of its forest than any other part of the basin and holds birds found in no other interfluvium. What survives is in fragments and in a handful of reserves, so this is the part of Amazonia where the reserve boundaries matter most. Utinga State Park is inside the city.

  • Look for: Golden Parakeet, Red-necked Aracari, Bare-necked Fruitcrow, Hoatzin, Paradise Jacamar, Amazonian Umbrellabird, Zigzag Heron, Grey-winged Trumpeter
  • Stops:
    • , 1,300 hectares of forest and two water-supply lakes inside Belem itself, with made trails, and the easiest Amazonian forest anywhere to reach
    • , a river island an hour from the city by road, with beach, mangrove and secondary forest watched from the road

    • , the Atlantic mangrove belt east of Belem, the largest continuous mangrove in the world, searched for shorebirds and Scarlet Ibis
  • Also watch for: Scarlet Ibis works the mangrove coast east of the city, and the Marajo floodplain north of it carries Water Buffalo that were introduced and are now part of how the island is farmed.

Carajas and southern Para

Canga ironstone savanna in southern Para, and the longest bird list in Brazil

Carajas in southern Para holds the second largest iron ore mine on earth and, around it, 400,000 hectares of protected national forest carrying more bird species than any other single site in Brazil: 594 recorded. The canga is a natural ironstone savanna on the plateau tops, a habitat that exists almost nowhere else, and access is controlled because the mine controls the road.

  • Look for: Horned Screamer, Razor-billed Curassow, White-crested Guan, Ornate Hawk-Eagle, Harpy Eagle, Xingu Scale-backed Antbird, Marbled Wood Quail, Grey-winged Trumpeter
  • Stops:
    • , over 400,000 hectares around the mine, with 594 bird species recorded, the largest list of any site in Brazil. Entry is through the mine's own controlled road
    • , the town at the gate, with the airport, and where the permits and the guides are arranged
    • , the ironstone plateau tops inside the forest, open low scrub over rock, with a flora and a bird list that share nothing with the forest below
  • Also watch for: The national forest carries 44 species of medium and large mammal on its own surveys, jaguar and giant armadillo among them, and the caves in the canga hold a bat fauna that is a conservation issue in its own right.

The Roosevelt and the Aripuana

Two remote rivers in the heart of the basin, and species described to science in 2013

Theodore Roosevelt nearly died descending the river that now carries his name, and the forest along it and along the neighbouring Aripuana was so little worked that new birds were still being described from it in 2013. Both are lodge or live-aboard trips with no road alternative.

  • Look for: Rondonia Bushbird, Aripuana Antwren, Crimson-bellied Parakeet, White-browed Hawk, Black-girdled Barbet, Brown-chested Barbet, Zigzag Heron, Fiery-capped Manakin

    • , the River of Doubt, reached by air and boat, with a wilderness lodge on the bank and terra firme trails behind it

    • , the town at the head of the navigable river, and the usual place the trips form up
  • Also covers: Amazonas
  • Also watch for: Milton's Titi and the Aripuana Marmoset are both named in the itineraries that come here, and both were themselves described recently. Giant Otter works the whole of both rivers.

Rondonia and the Madeira

South-west Amazonia, where three interfluvia meet on one river system

The Madeira and its tributaries divide the south-west of the basin into blocks of forest that each hold their own set of birds, and the region has been reached by road only recently. A trip here works Rondonia, southern Amazonas and Acre together along the Madeira, the Machado and the Aripuana.

Mamiraua, Tefe and the Japura

The largest protected flooded forest on earth, and a reserve run by the people who live in it

Mamiraua is 1,124,000 hectares of varzea, flooded forest on white water, and the largest protected area of its kind anywhere. It sits between the Japura and the Solimoes upriver of Tefe and is a sustainable development reserve, meaning the communities inside it manage it. The birds are varzea specialists that the terra firme sites above do not hold.

Monte Alegre and the north bank

Table hills, rock art and white sand campina north of the Amazon

The north bank of the lower Amazon is a different faunal region from the south, and Monte Alegre is the accessible point on it: sandstone table hills rising out of the floodplain, campina on white sand at their feet, and rock paintings that are among the oldest dated art in the Americas. It sits between the Tapajos and Belem.

  • Look for: Golden Parakeet, Paradise Jacamar, Point-tailed Palmcreeper, Pompadour Cotinga, Bare-necked Fruitcrow, Channel-billed Toucan, Grey-winged Trumpeter, Hoatzin
  • Stops:
    • , 3,678 hectares of sandstone hills and campina on the north bank, with the painted rock shelters inside it
    • , the river town the park is reached from, reached by boat or a short flight from Santarem
    • , the table hill above the town, climbed for the campina birds on the sand at its base and the view over the floodplain. OpenStreetMap carries no feature for the hill, so the coordinate here is the town it stands over
  • Also watch for: The campina here is a low white-sand scrub that looks like poor ground and holds a specialised list, birds and plants alike, found nowhere in the surrounding forest.

Frequently Asked Questions

When is the best time to go birding in the Brazilian Amazon?

July to November. The water is low, the terra firme trails and the canopy towers are workable, and the beaches are out on the clear-water rivers. May and June are the counter-argument: the water is at its peak and the flooded forest is canoed through the treetops, which is an experience the dry season cannot give. The three seasons.

Which part of the Brazilian Amazon should a first trip go to?

Manaus if you want the widest range of habitats in one base and an international airport at the end of it, or Alta Floresta and the Cristalino if you want one lodge, two canopy towers and more than 600 species recorded on its own land. Most trips use one or the other.

Why do the birds change from one river to the next?

Because the rivers are the barriers. Amazonian birds are mostly poor over-water dispersers, so the blocks of forest between the major rivers, the interfluvia, each carry their own set of closely related species. It is why an itinerary crossing the Madeira or the Tapajos is not doing it for the scenery.

Is Brazil one page or four?

Four, because nobody books Brazil. The Pantanal, the Amazon, the Atlantic Forest and the cerrado, the dry savanna of the Brazilian interior, and caatinga, the thorn scrub of the northeast, are different months, different airports and almost entirely different birds. This guide covers the rainforest; the other three link from the foot of it.

Do I need a guide?

Yes, in practice. Almost no site here is reachable without a boat, several need a permit that a resident arranges, and Carajas is entered through a mining company's own road. The lodges and the cruise operators are the route in.

How does it compare with Peru or Ecuador for Amazon birding?

Peru and Ecuador put lowland Amazon within a day of an Andean gradient, so a two-week trip crosses several thousand metres of altitude. Brazil has no Andes and offers the opposite: the sheer breadth of the basin, and interfluvia that hold birds found in Brazil and nowhere else. The two trips answer different questions.

Is there anywhere certified to stay?

Almost nothing. The Wild and Worthy supplier archive holds seventy Brazilian properties and under the certification rule this site applies, hardly any qualify, and none of those is in Amazonia. The listings here are lodges inside their own private reserves, which is a different claim and is labelled as one. How a certification is judged.

How bad are the insects?

It depends entirely on the water. Black-water rivers such as the Negro carry very few mosquitoes because the water is too acidic for the larvae, and white-water floodplain such as the Solimoes and the varzea reserves carries a great many. It is one of the real practical differences between the regions above.
