Birding in Louisiana

A bird crossing the Gulf of Mexico in one flight makes landfall on the Louisiana coast, and the first trees it meets are oak ridges a few hundred metres wide with a car park at the end.

Birds that decide the trip

Painted Bunting in the chenier oaks

It arrives off the Gulf of Mexico with the warblers and feeds on the ground under live oak, and there is no better place to see a male well than a chenier in April.

When April to July, in oak scrub and edge.

Where The Cameron coast and the cheniers

Roseate Spoonbill at Lake Martin

The rookery there holds spoonbills among the herons and egrets, and a levee path runs along its edge so the birds are watched from a few metres without a boat.

When March to June, at the rookery.

Where The Atchafalaya basin, The Cameron coast and the cheniers

Red-cockaded Woodpecker in the Kisatchie longleaf

It excavates in living pine and needs fire to keep the stand open, and Kisatchie is the only substantial longleaf left in the state. Cavity trees are ringed with white paint.

When April to June, at first light around the clusters.

Where The Kisatchie pine hills

Cerulean Warbler at Tensas

The bottomland hardwood at Tensas is the largest block left in the alluvial valley, which is why a canopy warbler in long decline still breeds there.

When Late April to June, from the canopy.

Where The Tensas bottomlands

Prothonotary Warbler over the swamp water

It nests in holes above standing water and is the bird that makes a cypress swamp worth being in, gold against grey bark and never far from the boat.

When April to July, over standing water.

Where The Atchafalaya basin, The Tensas bottomlands

Reddish Egret on the coastal flats

It hunts by running, staggering and canopying its wings over the water, which makes it the only heron identifiable at a distance by behaviour alone.

When All year, on shallow saline flats.

Where The Cameron coast and the cheniers, The delta and the barrier islands

Black Skimmer on the barrier islands

It feeds by ploughing the lower mandible through the water, and the offshore islands hold colonies in numbers the eroding mainland shore no longer supports.

When May to August, on island colonies.

Where The delta and the barrier islands

King Rail in the coastal marsh

The largest of the American rails, and the Louisiana marsh holds it in better numbers than almost anywhere. It walks out onto refuge road edges at dawn.

When April to July, along marsh roads at first light.

Where The Cameron coast and the cheniers

When to Go

The Louisiana year turns on one thing: the spring crossing. Birds leave the Yucatan at dusk and arrive on this coast the following afternoon, and if a north wind meets them they drop into the first cover they find. That is the trip most people come for, and it lasts about six weeks.

The trans-Gulf arrival

Late March to early May

Warblers, tanagers and buntings arrive off the Gulf of Mexico and drop into the chenier oaks.

Go for Peveto Woods and the Cameron ridges, then the delta at Venice.

Trade off It only happens properly on a north wind, so a still week can be quiet.

The swamp breeding season

April to June

The rookeries are full and the bottomland warblers are singing.

Go for Lake Martin for the rookery, Tensas for Cerulean and Swainson, Kisatchie for the woodpeckers.

Trade off It is hot and humid and the mosquitoes are serious.

Typical daily average

The tern colonies

May to July

The barrier islands hold tern and skimmer colonies the mainland cannot support.

Go for The delta and the offshore islands by boat.

Trade off The colonies are closed to landing and the heat offshore is punishing.

Typical daily average

The wintering months

November to February

Geese fill the coastal marsh and the greentree reservoirs flood for ducks.

Go for Sabine and Cameron Prairie, then Tensas and Catahoula.

Trade off The migrants have gone and the swamp is quieter, though far more comfortable.

The Cameron coast and the cheniers

Oak ridges a few hundred metres wide, catching birds that have just crossed the Gulf of Mexico

A chenier is an old Louisiana beach ridge left standing in the marsh with live oak on it, and to a bird that has flown across the Gulf of Mexico in one go it is the first tree in six hundred miles. Peveto Woods is a few acres of that, and in a spring fallout it holds more warblers than a county elsewhere.

  • Habitat: Live oak and hackberry on old beach ridges, brackish and fresh marsh behind them, coastal prairie, and beach and mudflat on the Gulf edge.
  • When: Late March to early May for the trans-Gulf arrivals, best on a day when a north wind has knocked them down, and October to February for the geese and waders.
  • Getting there: Lake Charles is the airport and Cameron the base. Highway 82 runs the coast with a ferry crossing at Cameron, and Peveto Woods is a small Baton Rouge Audubon sanctuary with a car park and no facilities.
  • Base yourself in: Cameron
  • Look for: Painted Bunting, Prothonotary Warbler, Hooded Warbler, Kentucky Warbler, Northern Parula, Roseate Spoonbill, Reddish Egret, Mottled Duck, Seaside Sparrow, King Rail, Black Skimmer, Royal Tern
  • Parks and reserves: Sabine National Wildlife Refuge, Rockefeller Wildlife Refuge (National wildlife refuge), Cameron Prairie National Wildlife Refuge - East Cove Unit, Cameron Prairie National Wildlife Refuge -Gibbstown Unit, Peveto Woods Sanctuary (Reserve)
  • Also covers: Cameron Prairie National Wildlife Refuge - East Cove Unit, Cameron Prairie National Wildlife Refuge -Gibbstown Unit, Peveto Woods Sanctuary, Rockefeller Wildlife Refuge, Sabine National Wildlife Refuge
  • Also watch for: Alligators lie on the refuge walking trails and the marsh holds one of the densest populations in the country.

The Atchafalaya basin

A million acres of cypress and tupelo, and a rookery you can walk the edge of

The Atchafalaya carries a distributary of the Mississippi through cypress and tupelo, and it is the largest river swamp in the country. Lake Martin on its western edge has a wading bird rookery with a levee path along it, which is the easiest way into that world.

  • Habitat: Bald cypress and tupelo swamp, bottomland hardwood on the natural levees, open lake and bayou, and crawfish ponds on the margins.
  • When: March to May for the rookery and the breeding warblers. The water is high in spring, which is what the swamp is for.
  • Getting there: Lafayette is the airport and the base. Lake Martin has a levee walk and a boat launch, and much of the basin itself is reached only by boat or from the elevated interstate.
  • Base yourself in: Lafayette
  • Look for: Roseate Spoonbill, Little Blue Heron, Tricolored Heron, Anhinga, Purple Gallinule, Limpkin, Prothonotary Warbler, Swainson's Warbler, Barred Owl, Bald Eagle, Neotropic Cormorant, Glossy Ibis
  • Parks and reserves: Atchafalaya National Wildlife Reserve
  • Also covers: Atchafalaya National Wildlife Refuge, Atchafalaya National Wildlife Reserve, Lake Martin, Sherburne Wildlife Management Area
  • Also watch for: The swamp holds alligator, nutria, black bear and one of the larger river otter populations in the south.

The delta and the barrier islands

The mouth of the Mississippi, where the continent runs out into open water

The birds that cross the Gulf of Mexico make landfall here first, and the barrier islands offshore hold tern and skimmer colonies in numbers the mainland cannot support. The delta is also disappearing: this is some of the fastest land loss anywhere on earth.

  • Habitat: Freshwater delta marsh and crevasse splay, sand and shell barrier island, open bay and pass, and willow scrub on the higher ground.
  • When: April for the trans-Gulf arrivals and the tern colonies, and November to February for the geese and wintering shorebirds.
  • Getting there: New Orleans is the airport, two hours north, and Venice the base at the end of Highway 23. The barrier islands are boat-only and the tern colonies are closed in the breeding season.
  • Base yourself in: Venice
  • Look for: Brown Pelican, Sandwich Tern, Royal Tern, Least Tern, Black Skimmer, Wilson's Plover, Piping Plover, American Oystercatcher, Magnificent Frigatebird, Reddish Egret, Mottled Duck, Snow Goose
  • Parks and reserves: Delta National Wildlife Refuge, Pass a Loutre Wildlife Management Area (National wildlife refuge), Elmer's Island Wildlife Refuge (National wildlife refuge)
  • Also covers: Breton National Wildlife Refuge, Coastal Louisiana Islands, Delta National Wildlife Refuge, Elmer's Island Wildlife Refuge, Pass a Loutre Wildlife Management Area
  • Also watch for: Bottlenose dolphins work the passes and the marsh holds alligator right to the river mouth.

The Kisatchie pine hills

Longleaf pine on sandstone hills, kept open by fire, with the state's woodpeckers in it

Kisatchie is the only national forest in the state and the only real hill country in it, and the longleaf pine there is burned to keep it open. Red-cockaded Woodpecker, Bachman's Sparrow and Brown-headed Nuthatch all depend on that management.

  • Habitat: Longleaf pine savanna on sandstone hills kept by fire, mixed pine and hardwood in the drains, bottomland along the bayous, and a large shallow lake at Catahoula.
  • When: April to June for the woodpeckers and the pine savanna birds, and November to February for the Catahoula waterfowl.
  • Getting there: Alexandria is the airport and the base. The Longleaf Trail scenic byway runs through the Kisatchie district, and the cavity trees are marked with white paint rings.
  • Base yourself in: Alexandria
  • Look for: Red-cockaded Woodpecker, Bachman's Sparrow, Brown-headed Nuthatch, Northern Bobwhite, Chuck-will's-widow, Prairie Warbler, Summer Tanager, Blue Grosbeak, Bald Eagle, Wood Duck, Anhinga, Barred Owl
  • Parks and reserves: Kisatchie National Forest (Forest or grassland)
  • Also covers: Catahoula National Wildlife Refuge, Kisatchie National Forest
  • Also watch for: The forest holds the Louisiana pine snake, one of the rarest snakes in the country, and armadillo are everywhere.

The Tensas bottomlands

The largest block of bottomland hardwood left in the Mississippi valley

Tensas protects about eighty thousand acres of bottomland hardwood, which is the biggest piece of that forest left in the whole alluvial valley. It is the last place the Ivory-billed Woodpecker was confidently recorded, and it is what the whole valley used to look like.

  • Habitat: Bottomland hardwood of oak, sweetgum and overcup, cypress brakes and sloughs, greentree reservoir flooded in winter, and old field on the margins.
  • When: April to June for the breeding warblers, and December to February when the greentree reservoirs are flooded for ducks.
  • Getting there: Monroe or Jackson in Mississippi are the airports and Tallulah the base. The refuge has a visitor centre, boardwalk and gravel roads, and the interior tracks flood in spring.
  • Base yourself in: Tallulah
  • Look for: Cerulean Warbler, Prothonotary Warbler, Swainson's Warbler, Kentucky Warbler, Hooded Warbler, Barred Owl, Mississippi Kite, Swallow-tailed Kite, Wood Duck, Bald Eagle, Anhinga, Painted Bunting
  • Parks and reserves: Tensas River National Wildlife Refuge
  • Also covers: Bayou Cocodrie National Wildlife Refuge, Tensas River National Wildlife Refuge
  • Also watch for: Louisiana black bear were brought back from near-extinction here and the refuge is the core of that population.

Frequently Asked Questions

When is the best time to go birding in Louisiana?

The second half of April. The trans-Gulf migrants are arriving on the coast, the rookeries are full and the bottomland warblers are singing, all in the same fortnight. November to February is the alternative, for geese and flooded bottomlands in far more comfortable weather.

What is a chenier and why does it matter?

An old beach ridge left standing in the marsh, with live oak growing on it. A bird that has flown across the Gulf of Mexico overnight sees no tree at all until it reaches one, so a few acres of chenier can hold an extraordinary number of birds on the right day.

Will I see a fallout?

Only if the weather cooperates. Migrants leaving the Yucatan at dusk arrive here the next afternoon, and they carry on inland unless a north wind or rain stops them. Give yourself several days on the coast rather than one, and watch the forecast.

Is the swamp safe to walk?

Yes, on the made paths. Alligators are common and lie on the Lake Martin levee and on refuge trails, so give them room and do not stand between one and the water. The mosquitoes are a bigger practical problem than the alligators.

Why does Tensas matter so much?

It is about eighty thousand acres of bottomland hardwood, the biggest block left in the whole Mississippi alluvial valley, and it shows what the valley looked like before the forest was cleared. It also holds the core of the recovered Louisiana black bear population.

Can I bird here with limited mobility?

Sites on this page differ from each other more than any rating could carry, so this is the framework the standard British guides use, and the reserve's own page and warden hold the answers.

  • Permission · some reserves open ground to disabled visitors that is closed to everyone else, and it is granted by asking.
  • Priority · parking bays, hides and the nearest paths are often held for disabled visitors. Ask what is reserved and how to claim it.
  • Parking · how close a car reaches the birds shapes the whole day. Ring ahead for the nearest hard standing to the water or the hide.
  • Paths · surfaces, gradients and gate widths vary inside a single reserve. Ask which trail is boarded, surfaced or level.
  • Perches · a hide with a low window, or a bench where the view opens, turns a viewpoint into a morning.

Every reserve above with its own link carries its access detail there, and a warden answers what a page cannot.
