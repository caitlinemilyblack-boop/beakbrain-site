Birding in Idaho

Idaho puts several hundred nesting Prairie Falcons on eighty miles of one canyon and the largest bulrush marsh in North America two hundred miles east of them, with the Sawtooths in between.

Birds that decide the trip

Prairie Falcon on the canyon wall

Several hundred pairs nest along eighty miles of the Snake River canyon, which is a meaningful fraction of the whole species in one place. The ledges face shrub-steppe full of ground squirrels, and that is the entire reason.

When March into June, from the canyon rim viewpoints south of Kuna.

Where The Snake River raptor canyon

Ferruginous Hawk over the shrub-steppe

The largest of the American buteos, and the Idaho conservation area holds around fifty-five occupied nests. It hunts the same ground squirrels the falcons take, from a lower and slower flight.

When March to June, over the steppe north of the river.

Where The Snake River raptor canyon

Sandhill Crane at Grays Lake

Grays Lake carries the largest breeding concentration of the greater form anywhere, between 150 and 225 pairs in the biggest bulrush marsh on the continent. The refuge shuts in April, so the birds are watched from the loop road.

When Late April to June, from the loop road with a scope.

Where The southeastern marshes

Trumpeter Swan on the upper Snake

Camas and Harriman both nest them, and the Henrys Fork holds birds through the winter where the water stays open. Sand Creek is the only wildlife management area in the state with a nesting pair.

When April to July for nesting, and through winter on open water.

Where The upper Snake wetlands

Franklin's Gull over the bulrush

Up to forty thousand birds breed at Grays Lake, the largest colony in the intermountain west, with another thirteen thousand at Bear Lake. They feed out over the farmland and come back to the marsh in a black-hooded stream.

When Late April to late June, over the marsh and the surrounding fields.

Where The southeastern marshes

Pygmy Nuthatch in the Panhandle ponderosa

The Coeur d'Alene area is the easiest place in the state to find them, in the mature ponderosa around the lake. They travel in noisy parties and give themselves away long before they are seen.

When All year, in the ponderosa stands around the lake.

Where The Panhandle lakes

American Three-toed Woodpecker in the Idaho Sawtooths

It works burned and beetle-killed lodgepole, and flakes bark rather than drumming. The Sawtooth recreation area has plenty of both, and the bird is quiet enough that most people walk past it.

When Late June to August, in burned stands off the Galena Summit road.

Where The Sawtooths and Silver Creek

Sage Grouse on the Sand Creek leks

The sagebrush at Sand Creek holds both this and the Columbian form of Sharp-tailed Grouse. Lek counts here fell from a mean of 600 males in the 1970s to 200 by the 1990s, which is the range-wide story in one place.

When April and May at first light, from the road rather than the lek.

Where The upper Snake wetlands

When to Go

Idaho runs on two clocks. The desert south is a March-to-June proposition and empties in high summer, while the mountains only open once the snow leaves in late June. The Panhandle is the exception and is at its best in winter.

The raptor season

March to June

The canyon falcons and hawks are nesting, and the desert wetlands take the duck and goose passage.

Go for Snake River Birds of Prey from Kuna, then Market Lake and Camas for the March flood of ducks.

Trade off The Sawtooth passes are still under snow and the high country is closed.

Typical daily average

The marsh breeding season

Late April to June

Grays Lake fills with cranes and Franklin's Gulls, and Bear Lake with White-faced Ibis.

Go for Grays Lake from the loop road, Bear Lake from Montpelier, Sand Creek for the grouse leks in April.

Trade off The Grays Lake refuge itself shuts to access on 1 April and is watched from outside.

Typical daily average

The mountain summer

Late June to August

The Sawtooth roads are open and the conifer breeders are singing.

Go for The Galena Summit to Cape Horn drive over two days, with Silver Creek below it.

Trade off The desert south is hot and quiet, and the shorebird mudflats have not yet appeared.

Typical daily average

The Panhandle winter

November to February

Wolf Lodge Bay gathers Bald Eagles on the spawning kokanee and the uncommon gulls arrive.

Go for Coeur d'Alene for gulls and eagles, Farragut for the lake, Kootenai for wintering waterfowl.

Trade off Most of the rest of the state is under snow with the side roads unplowed.

10 days

The whole state, south to north

Boise (BOI) 2 nights, Ketchum 2 nights, Idaho Falls 2 nights, Pocatello 1 night, Coeur d'Alene 3 nights, out of Spokane (GEG)

An open-jaw run that takes every region in one line, which is the only way to see the state without doubling back. Start on the canyon rim at Kuna in early June, cross the Sawtooths on the Galena Summit road, work the upper Snake wetlands and Grays Lake, then drive north for the Panhandle lakes and fly out of Spokane. Roughly 1,100 miles of driving.

A winter weekend on the lakes, and the one part of Idaho that is better in January than in June. The Snake River raptor canyon

Several hundred Prairie Falcons on eighty miles of basalt cliff, and the ground squirrels that keep them there

Six hundred feet of canyon wall runs beside shrub-steppe carrying dense Townsend ground squirrel and jackrabbit, and that pairing of nest ledge and prey is why so many raptors breed on one stretch of river. Several hundred pairs of Prairie Falcon nest along it, which is a meaningful share of the whole species.

  • Habitat: Basalt canyon wall up to six hundred feet, shrub-grass steppe above it, cottonwood gallery along the river, and two large irrigation reservoirs with mudflats when they draw down.
  • When: March into June, while the raptors are nesting. The Deer Flat drawdown in late summer and early autumn brings shorebirds instead.
  • Getting there: Boise is the airport and the base. Kuna is the turning for the Birds of Prey area, seventeen miles of paved then gravel road, and Deer Flat is signed from Nampa.
  • Base yourself in: Boise
  • Look for: Prairie Falcon, Ferruginous Hawk, Swainson's Hawk, Golden Eagle, Burrowing Owl, Long-eared Owl, American Kestrel, Sage Thrasher, Brewer's Sparrow, Sagebrush Sparrow, Common Merganser, Northern Pintail
  • Also covers: C.J. Strike Wildlife Management Area and Reservoir, Deer Flat National Wildlife Refuge, Snake River Birds of Prey National Conservation Area
  • Also watch for: Townsend ground squirrels and blacktail jackrabbits carry the whole raptor population, and mule deer come down to the river.

The Panhandle lakes

Cattail marsh where cattail marsh is rare, and the easiest gulls and Pygmy Nuthatches in the state

The Purcell Trench funnels birds down to the Kootenai marsh, which is unusual ground for a corner of the state that is mostly conifer and lake. Coeur d'Alene is the one place here where uncommon gulls are a reasonable expectation, and Pygmy Nuthatch is easy in the ponderosa around it.

  • Habitat: Cattail and bulrush marsh at Kootenai, mixed and dry conifer forest, and the shores of two very large lakes at Pend Oreille and Coeur d'Alene.
  • When: April to June for the breeding birds and November to February for the gull and eagle gathering at Wolf Lodge Bay.
  • Getting there: Spokane in Washington is the nearest airport, forty minutes west. Coeur d'Alene is the base, Bonners Ferry is ninety minutes north for the refuge, and Farragut charges an entrance fee.
  • Base yourself in: Coeur d'Alene
  • Look for: Pygmy Nuthatch, Eurasian Wigeon, Northern Waterthrush, Barrow's Goldeneye, Bald Eagle, Common Loon, Vaux's Swift, Red-naped Sapsucker, Pileated Woodpecker, Osprey, Tundra Swan, Bohemian Waxwing
  • Also covers: Coeur d'Alene Area, Farragut State Park, Heyburn State Park, Kootenai National Wildlife Refuge
  • Also watch for: Mountain goats are visible from the Farragut viewpoints and moose work the lake margins at either end of the day.

The Sawtooths and Silver Creek

Ninety miles of mountain road from Galena Summit to Cape Horn, with a spring creek and a lava field beside it

The Sawtooth National Recreation Area is three quarters of a million acres and the drive through it takes two unhurried days. Silver Creek below it is a spring-fed trout stream with wetland birds along the boardwalk, and the Craters lava field is a short and strange detour on the way east.

  • Habitat: Mixed conifer forest, sagebrush valley floor, spring-fed wetland at Silver Creek, and bare basalt with scattered limber pine at Craters of the Moon.
  • When: Late June to August for the mountain breeders, and April to June for Silver Creek and the lava field, when the wildflowers are out.
  • Getting there: Boise is three hours west and Ketchum is the base. Highways 75 and 21 make the Sawtooth loop, Silver Creek is signed from Picabo, and Craters of the Moon charges a park fee.
  • Base yourself in: Ketchum
  • Look for: American Goshawk, American Three-toed Woodpecker, Clark's Nutcracker, Mountain Bluebird, Rock Wren, Black Rosy Finch, Calliope Hummingbird, Williamson's Sapsucker, Great Grey Owl, American Dipper, American Bittern, Yellow-breasted Chat
  • Also covers: Craters of the Moon National Monument, Sawtooth Valley Route, Silver Creek Preserve
  • Also watch for: Elk, pronghorn and mountain goat all use the recreation area, and the Silver Creek preserve is a working trout fishery.

The upper Snake wetlands

A wetland complex on a desert plain, holding nesting Trumpeter Swans and a hundred and fifty thousand ducks on passage

Three wetlands within an hour of each other sit in sagebrush that takes eleven inches of rain a year, fed by springs and artesian wells. Each does something different: Camas nests Trumpeter Swans and Peregrines, Market Lake takes the spring flood of ducks and Snow Geese, and Sand Creek is the grouse ground.

  • Habitat: Bulrush and cattail marsh with wet meadow, surrounded by sagebrush and grassland desert, plus riparian willow on the Snake and Henrys Fork.
  • When: March through early July. The duck and goose passage peaks in March and the grouse are on the leks in April and May.
  • Getting there: Idaho Falls is the airport and the base. Camas is reached from Interstate 15 at Hamer, Market Lake from Roberts, and Harriman is eighteen miles north of Ashton.
  • Base yourself in: Idaho Falls
  • Look for: Trumpeter Swan, Red-necked Grebe, Peregrine Falcon, Grasshopper Sparrow, White-faced Ibis, Franklin's Gull, Snow Goose, Sage Grouse, Sharp-tailed Grouse, Long-billed Curlew, Lewis's Woodpecker, Sandhill Crane
  • Also covers: Camas National Wildlife Refuge, Harriman State Park, Market Lake Wildlife Management Area, Sand Creek Wildlife Management Area
  • Also watch for: Moose are regular at Harriman and the Sand Creek sagebrush carries pronghorn.

The southeastern marshes

The largest bulrush marsh in North America, and the world's biggest breeding concentration of Greater Sandhill Cranes

Grays Lake is a marsh rather than a lake, at six thousand four hundred feet in south-eastern Idaho, and it holds more breeding Greater Sandhill Cranes than anywhere on earth along with forty thousand Franklin's Gulls. Bear Lake to the south adds a five thousand strong White-faced Ibis colony, and American Falls takes the shorebird passage when the reservoir drops.

  • Habitat: Bulrush marsh, wet meadow, aspen and willow at six thousand feet, with sagebrush foothills above and reservoir mudflats to the west.
  • When: Late April through late June for the breeding colonies, and July into the autumn at American Falls Reservoir, when the water drops and the Idaho mudflats appear.
  • Getting there: Pocatello is the airport and the base. Grays Lake has no refuge access from April to early October and is watched from the loop road with a scope, and Bear Lake is signed from Montpelier.
  • Base yourself in: Pocatello
  • Look for: Sandhill Crane, White-faced Ibis, Franklin's Gull, Redhead, Canvasback, American Avocet, Willet, Brewer's Sparrow, Marbled Godwit, Baird's Sandpiper, Western Sandpiper, Trumpeter Swan
  • Also covers: American Falls Reservoir/Springfield Bottoms, Bear Lake National Wildlife Refuge, Grays Lake National Wildlife Refuge
  • Also watch for: Moose and elk use the wet meadows around Grays Lake, and the marsh was the site of the failed attempt to raise Whooping Cranes under Sandhill foster parents.

Frequently Asked Questions

When is the best time to go birding in Idaho?

March into June for the Snake River raptors and the desert wetlands, late April to June for the Grays Lake cranes and gull colonies, and late June to August once the Sawtooth roads clear. The Panhandle is the exception: its best month is January, for the eagle gathering and the gulls.

Can I visit Grays Lake refuge?

Not the refuge itself between 1 April and early October, which covers the whole breeding season. There is a loop road around it from which the cranes, ibis and gulls are watched with a spotting scope, and that is how the site is birded.

How much driving does Idaho take?

A great deal. Idaho is 480 miles from its southern line to the Canadian border and the Panhandle is closer to Spokane than to Boise, so a full circuit runs to about 1,100 miles. Flying into Boise and out of Spokane saves a day of backtracking.

Where do I see Greater Sage-Grouse?

Sand Creek Wildlife Management Area, on the sagebrush north of St. Anthony, in April and May. Watch from the road at first light rather than approaching a lek, because disturbance moves displaying males off it and the counts here have already fallen by two thirds since the 1970s.

Are the Sawtooth roads open all year?

The main highways are plowed, but the side roads that hold the best birding are under snow into June and the Galena Summit drive is a summer proposition. Plan the mountain half of any trip for late June onwards.

Can I bird here with limited mobility?

Sites on this page differ from each other more than any rating could carry, so this is the framework the standard British guides use, and the reserve's own page and warden hold the answers.

  • Permission · some reserves open ground to disabled visitors that is closed to everyone else, and it is granted by asking.
  • Priority · parking bays, hides and the nearest paths are often held for disabled visitors. Ask what is reserved and how to claim it.
  • Parking · how close a car reaches the birds shapes the whole day. Ring ahead for the nearest hard standing to the water or the hide.
  • Paths · surfaces, gradients and gate widths vary inside a single reserve. Ask which trail is boarded, surfaced or level.
  • Perches · a hide with a low window, or a bench where the view opens, turns a viewpoint into a morning.

Every reserve above with its own link carries its access detail there, and a warden answers what a page cannot.
