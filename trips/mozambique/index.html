Getting in
Maputo takes the international flights. Beira, Vilanculos, Chimoio, Tete, Quelimane and Lichinga are the domestic airports the routes here open at.
Getting around
The EN1 up the coast and the EN6 inland from Beira are tar. Everything else on this list wants high clearance, and the forest and delta tracks want a 4WD in any month.

When to Go

The year splits into a hot wet season from November to April and a dry one from May to October. August to November is the strongest window inland, because the roads are open and the birds are active. The coast peaks from October to March when the Palearctic waders and the terns are in. African Pitta needs the rains, so the one bird that pulls hardest against everything else is also the one people come furthest for.

Hot and wet

November to April

The rains bring African Pitta and the widest diversity of the year, and they close most of the tracks into the forests while they do it.

Go for African Pitta displaying in the lowland forest from late November to January, the Urema plain at Gorongosa in full water, and Palearctic waders on the coast.

Trade off Cyclones cross the central coast between January and March, the Chinizuia and delta tracks become impassable, and every inland road is slow.

Cool and dry

May to August

The roads firm up, the days are mild, and Mozambique's winter visitors from Madagascar arrive.

Go for Mascarene Martin and Malagasy Pond Heron through the middle of the year, Mangrove Kingfisher in the mangroves, and miombo bird parties in the woodland.

Trade off The Palearctic migrants have gone, the forest is quieter, and the water birds spread out as the pans dry.

Hot and dry

September to October

The strongest all-round window, with every road open and the first migrants back.

Go for Green-headed Oriole calling on Mount Gorongosa, miombo bird parties at Chinizuia, and the first waders returning to the Bazaruto flats.

Trade off It is the hottest part of the year and water is scarce away from the rivers.

Maputo National Park and Maputaland

Sand forest, freshwater lakes and tidal lagoon at the southern tip of the country

The park runs from Maputo Bay south to the border and it holds the Maputaland specials in tall sand forest behind the dunes. Neergaard's Sunbird, Rudd's Apalis, Woodwards' Batis and Pink-throated Twinspot all live in that forest, and Brown Scrub Robin is common in it. Grassland around the Mirador lookout and Lake Nele carries Rosy-throated Longclaw, Senegal Lapwing and Shelley's Francolin, and the tidal lagoons in the north bring in Sooty Falcon and Mangrove Kingfisher.

  • Habitat: Tall sand forest and dune forest, mangrove and tidal lagoon, freshwater lakes, riverine woodland along the Futi, and open grassland.

Panda and the inland miombo

A relict woodland island in the coastal plain, and the southern outpost of Olive-headed Weaver

Panda sits inland from the Inhambane coast in a patch of tall miombo, the broad-leaved woodland that covers much of south-central Africa, and this stand is the last one south of the Zambezi that holds Olive-headed Weaver. The weaver forages along branches hung with old-man's-beard lichen and builds its nest out of the same stuff. Bird parties in the same woodland carry Southern Hyliota, Pale Batis, Red-faced Crombec and Black-eared Seedeater, and Racket-tailed Roller is common without ever being obvious. Dambos along the Inharrime road flood after rain and hold African Pygmy Goose, Lesser Jacana and Black Coucal.

  • Habitat: Tall miombo woodland in a mosaic with subsistence farming, mixed woodland to the west, and grassy dambos that flood in the rains.
  • When: Olive-headed Weaver is possible all year, and it joins mixed bird parties in winter. The dambos are worth walking from January to April while they hold water.
  • Getting there: Panda is reached on tar from Inharrime in the south or from Homoine in the north. The best woodland lies between 12 and 18 km south west of the town, and the tracks into it are deep sand.
  • Base yourself in: Panda
  • All year: Olive-headed Weaver, Southern Hyliota, Pale Batis, Red-faced Crombec, Racket-tailed Roller, Black-eared Seedeater, Neergaard's Sunbird, Green-backed Honeybird
  • In the breeding season: Grey-rumped Swallow, Broad-tailed Paradise Whydah, Lemon-breasted Canary
  • On passage: Blue-cheeked Bee-eater, Lesser Moorhen, Allen's Gallinule
  • In winter: Mascarene Martin, Black-rumped Buttonquail, Pale-crowned Cisticola, Olive-headed Weaver
  • Stops:
    • : The town the woodland is named for. The road south west out of it reaches the weaver stand after about 13 km.

Inhambane and the Barra coast

Mangrove lagoon, mudflats and coconut plantation on a low sandy coast

A long sand spit shelters the Inhambane lagoon and mangrove lines the whole of its inner shore. Mangrove Kingfisher is common along it, migrant waders feed on the flats in front of Maxixe, and flocks of Crab-plover reach several hundred birds on Ilha do Barro in summer. Collared Palm Thrush lives in the coconut plantations at Ponta da Barra and Morrungulo, Magpie Mannikin turns up in the thickets behind them, and an isolated population of White-fronted Bee-eater holds on at Pomene.

Vilanculos and the Bazaruto archipelago

Sand islands, tern roosts and the flats that hold Crab-plover

Vilanculos faces the Bazaruto islands across a shallow bay, and the spits and mudflats between them carry the best shorebirding in Mozambique. Crab-plover comes in small groups to the west coast of Benguerra and to the sandspit running north from the Sao Sebastiao peninsula, tern roosts on the same banks hold six species or more, and Olive Bee-eater breeds in the dune slacks of all three main islands. Lily-covered pans along the access roads hold African Pygmy Goose, Saddle-billed Stork and the occasional Malagasy Pond Heron, and African Cuckoo-Hawk perches on the telephone lines above them.

  • Habitat: Sandy shoreline, wide intertidal mudflat and spit, dune slack and coastal forest on the islands, and lily-covered freshwater pans on the mainland.
  • When: October to March for waders, terns and Crab-plover. Mascarene Martin passes over the pans from May to September.
  • Getting there: Vilanculos lies 21 km off the EN1 on tar and has an airport. Boats to Bazaruto, Benguerra and the Sao Sebastiao spit leave from the town, and the spit dries at low tide so the crossing has to be timed.
  • Base yourself in: Vilanculos
  • All year: Crab-plover, Olive Bee-eater, Mascarene Martin, Lemon-breasted Canary, Malagasy Pond Heron, Saddle-billed Stork, African Pygmy Goose, Green Malkoha
  • In the breeding season: Olive Bee-eater, Eastern Golden Weaver, Rufous-winged Cisticola
  • On passage: Crab-plover, Bar-tailed Godwit, Terek Sandpiper, Greater Crested Tern
  • In winter: Mascarene Martin, Malagasy Pond Heron, Eurasian Oystercatcher
  • Also recorded in the parks here: Black-headed Apalis, Horus Swift
  • National park: Bazaruto National Park
  • Stops:
    • : The mainland town and the departure point for the islands. Olive Bee-eater lives along the beachfront and in the gardens all year.
    • : A quieter fishing village 50 km up the coast, with lily-covered pans along its access road.

Zinave and the Save valley

Baobab, mopane and fever-tree pans on the south bank of the Save

Zinave sits in dry country a long way from any coast road, and the track in is sand the whole way. Woodland of baobab, mopane and miombo runs back from the river, pans are lined with fever trees, and the Save itself carries White-crowned Lapwing, Collared Pratincole and African Skimmer while the sandbars stand above water. Around the camp, Double-banded Sandgrouse and Bronze-winged Courser come to open ground and Southern Ground Hornbill booms from the dry riverbed at first light.

  • Habitat: Baobab forest, mopane and miombo woodland, sand forest on the approach, riverine forest along the Save, and pans lined with fever trees.

Gorongosa National Park and Mount Gorongosa

A rift-valley flood plain under a forested granite massif

Gorongosa sits at the southern end of the Great Rift Valley, and the Urema plain in the north of the park floods every wet season and holds waterbirds in thousands. Wattled Crane, Grey Crowned Crane, Saddle-billed Stork and Greater Painted-snipe gather there, and the woodland between the gate and Chitengo carries Pennant-winged Nightjar, Thick-billed Cuckoo and Arnot's Chat. Mount Gorongosa rises to 1,863 m above the same plain and its forest holds Green-headed Oriole, which occurs nowhere else south of the Zambezi. Chirinda Apalis and Swynnerton's Robin live in that forest too.

  • Habitat: Seasonally flooded grassland on the Urema plain, palm savanna, dry woodland and miombo on the flats, and montane forest above 800 m on the mountain with grassland between the patches.
  • When: The flood plain is at its best from January to July while the water is still on it, and the road to Chitengo turns to mud in the rains. Forest birding on the mountain is good all year, and Green-headed Oriole is noisiest in September and October.
  • Getting there: The park turn-off leaves the EN1 north of the Pungwe bridge and a gravel road runs to the gate and on to Chitengo. Mount Gorongosa is climbed from Vinduzi village, where permission, a guide and a car guard are arranged, and the walk to the forest takes two to five hours.

Beira and the Rio Savane flood plain

Wet coastal grassland, mangrove and estuary an hour north of Beira

The Savane river mouth lies at the end of a gravel road north of Beira, and the flooded meadows on the way to it hold birds that are hard to find anywhere else in Mozambique. Cheringoma and the Chinizuia forests

Tall miombo on a low plateau running down into lowland riverine forest

The Cheringoma plateau carries some of the tallest miombo left in the region, and the track from Muanza to the Chinizuia stream crosses it before dropping into a band of lowland forest. East Coast Akalat is common and very vocal at dawn and dusk in that forest, White-chested Alethe sings at dusk near the bridge, and several pairs of Barred Long-tailed Cuckoo can be heard within walking distance of the stream crossing from July to January. Racket-tailed Roller, Southern Ground Hornbill, Chestnut-fronted Helmetshrike and Yellow-bellied Hyliota all reach high density in the woodland above, and Lesser Seedcracker turns up in the denser undergrowth.

  • Habitat: Tall closed miombo woodland on the Cheringoma plateau, lowland riverine forest along the Chinizuia stream, and grassy glades that flood in the rains.
  • When: The forest is quieter through the dry season and the roads are far easier then. African Pitta is present from late November to January, White-chested Alethe is most vocal from October to June, and Mascarene Martin crosses the clearings from June to September.
  • Getting there: The turn-off north to Muanza leaves the Beira road west of Dondo, and the Chinizuia track runs east from a point north of Muanza. A 4WD is needed at any time of year, more than one vehicle is advised, and there is no fuel beyond Dondo.

Manica and the Chimanimani highlands

Afromontane forest and quartzite grassland along the western border

Chimanimani National Park covers the Mozambican side of the range and rises to Monte Binga at 2,436 m, the highest ground in the country. Mount Tsetserra to the north gives a complete succession from tall miombo at the base through grassland to Afromontane forest near the top, and the forest birds there are the ones of the border highlands: Chirinda Apalis, Roberts's Warbler, Barratt's Warbler, Orange Ground Thrush and Black-fronted Bushshrike. Chicamba dam below holds waterbirds, and the woodland along its entrance road is worth a morning on the way through.

  • Getting there: Manica and Chimoio sit on the EN6 between Beira and the border. Mount Tsetserra is reached by turning south at Chicamba and driving about 60 km before the road climbs, and 4WD is wanted for the ascent because of loose gravel.

Mopane along a very large lake, and rich woodland on the plateau above it

Mount Namuli and the Zambezia highlands

A granite dome above tea estates, holding the country's only endemic bird

Namuli rises to 2,412 m behind the tea estates above Gurue, and the montane forest on its flanks holds Namuli Apalis, a bird that lives here and nowhere else in the world. Dapple-throat and Thyolo Alethe skulk in the same understorey, Green Barbet calls from the canopy, and Bar-tailed Trogon and Black-fronted Bushshrike work the tall trees above. The riparian forest lower down at Ukusini carries Thyolo Alethe at a pair every 150 m along the stream, and Red-rumped Swallow and Anchieta's Tchagra are seen on the walk up through the tea.

  • Habitat: Afromontane forest, montane grassland with tree ferns and orchids, protea woodland on the ridges, and miombo woodland and tea plantation on the lower slopes.
  • When: All the forest specials are resident. Namuli Apalis occurs from about 1,200 m upward and is straightforward once its call is known.
  • Getting there: Gurue is reached on good gravel through Molumbo, about four hours from the border post at Milange. Njesi, Lichinga and Lake Niassa

A patchily forested plateau above Lichinga, and the lake shore below it

The Njesi Plateau, called Serra Jeci on modern maps, is the only place outside Tanzania that holds both Long-billed Forest Warbler and Red-capped Forest Warbler. The forest patch the 2001 expedition reached is small and broken and still gives Orange Ground Thrush, Red-capped Robin-Chat, Evergreen Forest Warbler and Livingstone's Turaco, and the tall miombo on the climb carries Olive-headed Weaver in mixed foraging flocks. Meponda, on the lake shore west of Lichinga, is the easiest point of access to the Mozambican side of the lake, and Bohm's Bee-eater lives in the riparian strip there.

The plateau track leaves the road before Mapacha and ends at a farm, and the nearest montane forest is a four-hour hike beyond it with porters and a guide. Meponda is 57 km from Lichinga on reasonable gravel.

Frequently Asked Questions

When is the best time to visit Mozambique for birding?

August to November is the strongest window. The roads into the lowland forests are open, the miombo bird parties are running, and the first intra-African migrants are back. The coast peaks a little later, from October to March, when the Palearctic waders and the terns are in. African Pitta needs the rains and is worth its own trip from late November to January. The three seasons.

How many bird species does Mozambique have?

The figure above counts the species with a card in the BeakBrain guide rather than a number typed in by hand. Mozambique holds one bird found nowhere else, Namuli Apalis on the Namuli massif, and a set of montane forest birds it shares only with southern Malawi and the Tanzanian mountains.

Can you self-drive?

The EN1 up the coast and the EN6 from Beira to the western border are tar and take an ordinary car. Everything inland wants high clearance, and the tracks to Chinizuia, the Zambezi concessions, Zinave and Maputo National Park want a 4WD in any month. Carry fuel, water and a tow rope, and travel in more than one vehicle on the remoter routes.

Which parts of the country should be left alone at the moment?

The Foreign, Commonwealth and Development Office advises against all travel to Cabo Delgado except Palma town and the area around Pemba and Afungi, and against all but essential travel to those three. It also advises against all travel to the Memba and Erati districts of Nampula and to the Mecula and Marrupo districts of Niassa, which takes in Niassa Special Reserve. Check the current advice before booking anything in the far north.

Do you need a local guide?

Mount Gorongosa, Mount Namuli and the Njesi Plateau all need permission from the local authority, porters and a guide, and each arrangement is made in person in the village at the foot of the climb. Portuguese carries much further in Mozambique than English does away from the coast resorts.

What about landmines?

Mozambique was declared free of all known mined areas in September 2015, after twenty-two years of clearance that lifted more than 171,000 mines. Old tracks, railway lines and abandoned buildings in the far north are still worth treating with local advice rather than curiosity.

Can I bird here with limited mobility?

Often yes, and it varies by site. A good deal of the birding here happens from roadsides, hides and lodge grounds rather than on long walks, and some reserves keep flat, surfaced trails. Ask a lodge or reserve directly about step free access, surfaces and seating before you book. The Birdability Map records those details for birding sites, and their Access Considerations guide lists the questions worth asking. Coverage is thin outside the United States, so read a blank area as unrecorded rather than unsuitable.
