Birding in Alberta

Wood Buffalo National Park is the only place on earth where Whooping Cranes nest in the wild, in muskeg so remote that nobody found the nests until 1954.

Birds that decide the trip

Whooping Crane at Wood Buffalo

The only wild nesting population on earth breeds in the muskeg here, and the nests were not found until 1954. The nesting area is closed and monitored from the air, which is why the birds are still there.

When May to August on the breeding grounds, which are closed to visitors.

Where Wood Buffalo and the boreal north

White-tailed Ptarmigan on the alpine

The only ptarmigan that stays in the alpine all year, and the high trails off the Icefields Parkway put it within a walk of a car park from July.

When July and August, on alpine tundra and scree.

Where Banff, Jasper and the Rockies

Sprague's Pipit over the short-grass

It song-flights very high over unbroken native prairie for minutes at a time, and the short-grass in the south-east is one of the strongholds left.

When May to July, in flight over native prairie.

Where Dinosaur and the dry south-east, Cypress Hills and the grasslands

Ferruginous Hawk on the prairie

The largest American buteo, hunting ground squirrels over open short-grass and nesting on low bluffs and lone trees, and the dry south-east holds a good share of the Canadian population.

When April to August, over short-grass prairie.

Where Dinosaur and the dry south-east, Cypress Hills and the grasslands

Clark's Grebe on the parkland lakes

It nests alongside Western Grebe on the shallow alkaline lakes, and the two are separated on bill colour and how much black comes down around the eye.

When May to August, on the open lakes.

Where The parkland lakes

Harlequin Duck on a mountain river

It nests along fast clear mountain streams and swims up rapids that look impassable, and the park rivers hold a closely watched population.

When May to July, on fast rivers in the parks.

Where Banff, Jasper and the Rockies

Connecticut Warbler in the northern muskeg

It walks rather than hops, sings from high in a tamarack and then disappears, and the boreal north holds it in as good numbers as anywhere.

When June, in tamarack and spruce bog at dawn.

Where Wood Buffalo and the boreal north

Burrowing Owl in the short-grass

The Canadian population has fallen very sharply and the south-eastern short-grass holds most of what is left, nesting in old ground squirrel and badger burrows.

When May to August, on short-grass prairie.

Where Dinosaur and the dry south-east

When to Go

Alberta gives about ten useful weeks. Everything on the prairie and in the parkland breeds from mid-May, the boreal north from June, and the alpine only opens once the snow leaves the passes at the end of June.

The spring passage

April and May

Waterfowl and cranes move north through the parkland lakes and the observatory counts the passerines.

Go for Frank Lake and the parkland lakes, and Lesser Slave Lake for the migration monitoring.

Trade off The mountain passes are shut and the boreal breeders have not arrived.

Typical daily average

The prairie and parkland season

Mid-May to July

The grebe colonies are on the lakes and the grassland birds hold the short-grass south.

Go for Frank Lake, Dinosaur, and the Cypress Hills.

Trade off The badlands are very hot and shadeless from July.

Typical daily average

The alpine window

Late June to September

The mountain parks clear and the alpine birds are reachable from the Icefields Parkway.

Go for Banff, Jasper and the Parkway, and Waterton in the south.

Trade off This is peak tourist season and the park accommodation is booked and expensive.

Typical daily average

The boreal season

June and early July

The northern muskeg holds its warblers and the Whooping Cranes are on the nest.

Go for Wood Buffalo and the Peace-Athabasca Delta, and Lesser Slave Lake.

Trade off Access to the north is slow and the nesting area itself is closed.

10 days

Mountains, parkland and prairie

Calgary (YYC), Banff 3 nights, Jasper 2 nights, Edmonton 1 night, Brooks 2 nights, Medicine Hat 1 night, back to Calgary

Nine days in the first half of July, which is when both the alpine and the prairie work. Ptarmigan and rosy finches off the Icefields Parkway, grebe colonies on the parkland lakes, then badlands and short-grass in the south-east. About 1,500 km.

5 days

Banff and the Icefields Parkway

Calgary (YYC), Banff 2 nights, Jasper 2 nights, back to Calgary

Four days in July driving 230 km of montane and alpine with pull-outs the whole way. Harlequin Duck on the fast rivers, American Dipper under the bridges, and White-tailed Ptarmigan on the high trails. Book beds a long way ahead.

The prairie south-east

Calgary (YYC), Brooks 2 nights, Medicine Hat 2 nights, back to Calgary

Four days in June in the driest part of the province. Ferruginous Hawk and Burrowing Owl on the short-grass, Sprague's Pipit song-flighting above it, and the Cypress Hills as a forested island the ice sheets went round. Start early: there is no shade.

6 days

The boreal north

Edmonton (YEG), Slave Lake 2 nights, Fort Chipewyan 3 nights, back to Edmonton

Five days in June for the northern muskeg. The Lesser Slave Lake observatory first, then north to the largest national park in the country and one of the largest freshwater deltas anywhere. The Whooping Crane nesting area is closed, and that is the point of it.

Banff, Jasper and the Rockies

The oldest national park in the country, with alpine on a road and icefield above it

Banff was the first national park here and Jasper is the largest in the Rockies, and the Icefields Parkway between them runs 230 km of montane and subalpine with pull-outs the whole way. Waterton in the south adds prairie meeting mountain in a single view.

  • Habitat: Montane spruce and lodgepole, subalpine fir and larch, alpine tundra and scree above treeline, icefield and glacier, and fescue prairie running up to the mountain front at Waterton.
  • When: Late June to September, once the alpine has cleared. The parks are busiest in July and August.
  • Getting there: Calgary is the airport, ninety minutes east of Banff. All the mountain parks charge a daily entry fee, the Icefields Parkway is open all year but closes in storms, and accommodation books out a year ahead in summer.
  • Base yourself in: Banff
  • Look for: White-tailed Ptarmigan, Grey-crowned Rosy Finch, American Dipper, Harlequin Duck, Clark's Nutcracker, American Three-toed Woodpecker, Black-backed Woodpecker, Boreal Chickadee, Dusky Grouse, Northern Pygmy Owl, Golden Eagle, Varied Thrush
  • Parks and reserves: Jasper National Park, Banff National Park, Waterton Lakes National Park, Waterton-Glacier International Peace Park (Protected area), Castle Wildland Provincial Park (State or provincial park)
  • Also covers: Banff National Park, Castle Wildland Provincial Park, Jasper National Park, Lake Louise, Waterton Lakes National Park, Waterton-Glacier International Peace Park
  • Also watch for: Grizzly and black bear, elk, bighorn sheep, mountain goat and wolves all use the parks, and Waterton is one of the better places anywhere to see all of them in a week.

The parkland lakes

Shallow prairie and parkland lakes, each one holding tens of thousands of waterbirds

The aspen parkland belt across the middle of the province is dotted with shallow alkaline and freshwater lakes, and the ones with managed water levels take extraordinary numbers of grebes, ducks and shorebirds. Frank Lake south of Calgary is the best known.

  • Habitat: Shallow alkaline and freshwater lakes with managed levels, cattail and bulrush marsh, aspen parkland and willow, and cropland between.
  • When: Mid-May to July for the breeding colonies, and April and September for the waterfowl and shorebird passage.
  • Getting there: Calgary or Edmonton are the airports. Frank Lake has a blind and a gravel road, and most of the parkland lakes are watched from public roads and municipal reserves.
  • Base yourself in: Edmonton
  • Look for: Western Grebe, Clark's Grebe, Black-necked Grebe, Red-necked Grebe, American White Pelican, Franklin's Gull, White-faced Ibis, Black Tern, Wilson's Phalarope, Ruddy Duck, Canvasback, Marbled Godwit
  • Parks and reserves: Frank Lake Conservation Area (Protected area), Wabamun Lake Provincial Park (State or provincial park), Edmonton and Area Land Trust - Ministik (Protected area), Edmonton and Area Land Trust - Boisvert's GreenWoods (Protected area), River Valley Area (Protected area)
  • Also covers: Edmonton and Area Land Trust - Boisvert's GreenWoods, Edmonton and Area Land Trust - Ministik, Frank Lake Conservation Area, Gull Lake, Lac La Biche, River Valley Area, Wabamun Lake Provincial Park, Weed Lake
  • Also watch for: The lakes hold muskrat and beaver, and moose come down to the parkland margins in winter.

Dinosaur and the dry south-east

Eroded coulees and hoodoos cut into short-grass prairie, with a lake in the driest country in the province

The Red Deer River has cut hoodoos and coulees into the plains at Dinosaur, and the cottonwood along the bottom is a green line through very dry country. Pakowki Lake to the south is a shallow alkaline lake that fills and empties, and when it is full it is the best waterbird site in the region.

  • Habitat: Eroded badland with hoodoos and coulees, short-grass and mixed-grass prairie on the plains above, cottonwood along the river, and shallow alkaline lakes.
  • When: Late May to early July for the breeding birds. It is very hot and exposed from July with no shade in the badlands.
  • Getting there: Calgary is the airport, two hours west, and Brooks the base. Dinosaur charges a provincial park fee and most of the badland interior is a preserve entered only on guided walks.
  • Base yourself in: Brooks
  • Look for: Ferruginous Hawk, Prairie Falcon, Golden Eagle, Burrowing Owl, Loggerhead Shrike, Rock Wren, Say's Phoebe, Sprague's Pipit, Chestnut-collared Longspur, Lark Bunting, American White Pelican, Piping Plover
  • Parks and reserves: Dinosaur Provincial Park (State or provincial park), Brooks Aqueduct Conservation Site (Protected area), Kinbrook Island Provincial Park (State or provincial park), Tillebrook Provincial Park (State or provincial park)
  • Also covers: Brooks Aqueduct Conservation Site, Dinosaur Provincial Park, Kinbrook Island Provincial Park, Lake Newell, Pakowki Lake, Tillebrook Provincial Park
  • Also watch for: Pronghorn range the short-grass and the badlands hold prairie rattlesnake and one of the richest dinosaur fossil beds anywhere.

Cypress Hills and the Milk River

An island of forest above the plains that the ice sheets went round

The Cypress Hills stood above the last ice sheet, so they kept species the surrounding plains lost and gained others that never left. They are the highest ground between the Rockies and Labrador, and their lodgepole and aspen hold birds a hundred kilometres from anything similar. The Milk River coulees south of them are different again.

  • Habitat: Lodgepole pine and aspen on a plateau standing above the plains, fescue grassland on the slopes, sandstone hoodoo and coulee along the Milk River, and short-grass prairie between.
  • When: Late May to July for the breeding birds. The hills are cooler than the plains around them by several degrees.
  • Getting there: Calgary or Medicine Hat are the airports and Medicine Hat the base. The interprovincial park charges a fee and straddles the Saskatchewan line, and Writing-on-Stone has guided access to the rock art areas.
  • Base yourself in: Medicine Hat
  • Look for: Dusky Grouse, American Three-toed Woodpecker, Clark's Nutcracker, Northern Pygmy Owl, Rock Wren, Canyon Wren, Say's Phoebe, Lark Bunting, Chestnut-collared Longspur, Sprague's Pipit, Ferruginous Hawk, Prairie Falcon
  • Parks and reserves: Writing-on-Stone Provincial Park (State or provincial park), Cypress Hills Provincial Park (State or provincial park), Twin River Heritage Rangeland Natural Area (Protected area), Cypress Hills Interprovincial Park (Protected area)
  • Also covers: Cypress Hills Interprovincial Park, Cypress Hills Provincial Park, Milk River, Twin River Heritage Rangeland Natural Area, Writing-on-Stone Provincial Park
  • Also watch for: Elk and moose hold the hills, and the Milk River valley carries the only Canadian population of several southern plants and reptiles.

Wood Buffalo and the boreal north

The only place on earth where Whooping Cranes nest in the wild

Wood Buffalo is the largest national park in the country and the last natural nesting ground of the Whooping Crane, in muskeg so remote that the nests were not found until 1954. The Peace-Athabasca Delta inside it is one of the largest freshwater deltas anywhere, and Lesser Slave Lake to the south runs a bird observatory on the migration route.

  • Habitat: Muskeg and sedge fen over karst, boreal spruce and aspen, the largest freshwater delta on the continent, salt plains, and lake shore and dune to the south.
  • When: June for the boreal breeders, and May and September for the passage at the Lesser Slave Lake observatory.
  • Getting there: Edmonton is the airport and Slave Lake the base for the observatory. Wood Buffalo is reached by road from Fort Smith in the Northwest Territories or by air, and the Whooping Crane nesting area is closed and watched from the air only.
  • Base yourself in: Slave Lake
  • Look for: Whooping Crane, Sandhill Crane, Yellow Rail, Connecticut Warbler, Cape May Warbler, Bay-breasted Warbler, Black-backed Woodpecker, American Three-toed Woodpecker, Canada Jay, Boreal Chickadee, Great Grey Owl, Boreal Owl
  • Parks and reserves: Wood Buffalo National Park, Lesser Slave Lake Provincial Park (State or provincial park), Kazan Wildland Provincial Park (State or provincial park), Richardson Wildland Provincial Park (State or provincial park), Grizzly Ridge Wildland Provincial Park (State or provincial park)
  • Also covers: Grizzly Ridge Wildland Provincial Park, Kazan Wildland Provincial Park, Lesser Slave Lake Provincial Park, Peace-Athabasca Delta, Richardson Wildland Provincial Park, Wood Buffalo National Park
  • Also watch for: Wood buffalo hold the park that is named for them, and the delta is one of the most important waterfowl staging areas on the continent.

Frequently Asked Questions

When is the best time to go birding in Alberta?

The first half of July, when the alpine has cleared and the prairie birds are still singing. Mid-May to June is better for the parkland grebe colonies and the boreal warblers, and April for the waterfowl passage through the lakes.

Can I see Whooping Cranes at Wood Buffalo?

No, and that is deliberate. The only wild nesting population on earth breeds in muskeg inside the park, the nesting area is closed and monitored from the air, and the birds are still there because of it. The Texas wintering ground is where they are watchable.

When does the alpine open?

Late June most years. The Icefields Parkway is open all year but the high trails that reach ptarmigan and rosy finch habitat hold snow well into June, so a mountain trip before then will stay in valley-bottom forest.

What are the Cypress Hills?

A plateau that stood above the last ice sheet, so it kept species the surrounding plains lost. It is the highest ground between the Rockies and Labrador, and its lodgepole and aspen hold birds a hundred kilometres from anything similar.

How hard is the north to reach?

Wood Buffalo is a long drive from Edmonton or an approach from Fort Smith in the Northwest Territories, and Fort Chipewyan has no all-season road at all. Lesser Slave Lake, with its bird observatory, is three hours from Edmonton and gives much of the boreal list without the logistics.

Can I bird here with limited mobility?

Sites on this page differ from each other more than any rating could carry, so this is the framework the standard British guides use, and the reserve's own page and warden hold the answers.

  • Permission · some reserves open ground to disabled visitors that is closed to everyone else, and it is granted by asking.
  • Priority · parking bays, hides and the nearest paths are often held for disabled visitors. Ask what is reserved and how to claim it.
  • Parking · how close a car reaches the birds shapes the whole day. Ring ahead for the nearest hard standing to the water or the hide.
  • Paths · surfaces, gradients and gate widths vary inside a single reserve. Ask which trail is boarded, surfaced or level.
  • Perches · a hide with a low window, or a bench where the view opens, turns a viewpoint into a morning.

Every reserve above with its own link carries its access detail there, and a warden answers what a page cannot.
