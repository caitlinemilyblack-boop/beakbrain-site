Birding in North Dakota

North Dakota hatches up to half the American White Pelicans in the country on one alkali lake, and its prairie potholes raise half the ducks in the lower states.

Birds that decide the trip

American White Pelican at Chase Lake in North Dakota

Up to half of every American White Pelican in the country hatches on this one North Dakota alkali lake, on islands that shrink and grow with the water level.

When May to July, from the refuge boundary rather than the colony.

Where The Missouri Coteau potholes

Baird's Sparrow at Lostwood

It needs unbroken native mixed-grass prairie that has been burned, which is exactly what Lostwood is managed to be. Nowhere else in the state holds it in comparable numbers.

When Late May to early July, in the first hour of light.

Where Lostwood and the north-west grasslands

Sprague's Pipit over the grass

It song-flights so high that it is heard long before it is seen, sometimes for half an hour without landing, and drops out of the sky in one movement when it finishes.

When Late May to early July, over unbroken native prairie at dawn.

Where Lostwood and the north-west grasslands, The Little Missouri badlands

Piping Plover on the alkali lakes

Between 300 and 350 breed on the McLean County alkali shores out of a Great Plains population of about 1,700, and Appam takes as many as fifty pairs in a wet year.

When May to July, on gravel and alkali shorelines.

Where The Missouri Coteau potholes, Lostwood and the north-west grasslands

Greater Prairie Chicken on the Sheyenne

Fewer than two hundred birds hold the only viable population left in the state, on tallgrass sand dunes, and the count has been falling.

When Mid-April to early May, at dawn from a blind.

Where The Sheyenne grassland

Burrowing Owl in a prairie dog town

It nests down old prairie dog burrows and stands on the mound beside them in daylight, which makes a dog town the fastest way to find one in the badlands.

When May to August, at dog towns along the park loop.

Where The Little Missouri badlands

Chestnut-collared Longspur on grazed prairie

It wants short grass rather than tall, so grazed pasture and the edges of alkali flats hold it where the deeper prairie does not. The males display in a fluttering song-flight.

When Late May to July, on short-grazed prairie.

Where The Missouri Coteau potholes

Sandhill Crane staging on the Coteau

Horsehead Lake is a staging point on the autumn route south, and the birds roost on the shallow water and feed in the stubble around it.

When September and October, on the lake at dawn and dusk.

Where The Missouri Coteau potholes

When to Go

The season here is short and it is almost all in June. The prairie birds arrive late, sing for a few weeks and go quiet, and the two ends of the year are frozen. Coming in the wrong month means an empty landscape.

The prairie chicken leks

Mid-April to early May

The Sheyenne birds are on their booming grounds before the grass has greened.

Go for Sheyenne National Grassland at first light, and the first waterfowl on the potholes.

Trade off The grassland songbirds have not arrived and most refuge roads are still soft.

Typical daily average

The prairie breeding season

Mid-May to early July

Baird's Sparrow and Sprague's Pipit are singing and every pothole holds ducks and grebes.

Go for Lostwood at sunrise, the Coteau potholes, and the badlands grassland.

Trade off This is the only window, so everything competes for the same three weeks.

Typical daily average

The crane staging

September and October

Sandhill Cranes stage on the Coteau lakes and the waterfowl pile up before freeze-up.

Go for Horsehead Lake and Long Lake, and Upper Souris for the snow geese.

Trade off The grassland birds have gone and the weather turns without warning.

Typical daily average

The frozen months

November to March

Almost every breeding bird has left, and the winter list is Snowy Owl, Snow Bunting and Sharp-tailed Grouse.

Go for The badlands for wintering raptors and grouse on the road edges.

Trade off It is severely cold, the light is short and most refuge roads are closed.

8 days

The prairie in June

Bismarck (BIS), Jamestown 2 nights, Kenmare 3 nights, Medora 2 nights, back to Bismarck

The state's whole season in one loop, in the first three weeks of June. Chase Lake for the pelican colony, then three days at Lostwood, which is where Baird's Sparrow and Sprague's Pipit are most reliable, then south to the badlands. About 800 miles, mostly on tarmac with gravel at the refuges.

5 days

Lostwood and the north-west

Minot (MOT), Kenmare 4 nights, back to Minot

Four dawns on unbroken native prairie in the second half of June. Baird's Sparrow and Sprague's Pipit both sing in the first hour of light and go quiet by mid-morning, so the days are front-loaded, with the afternoons on the refuge impoundments for grebes and ducks.

4 days

The badlands

Bismarck (BIS), Medora 3 nights, back to Bismarck

Three days in the eroded country in mid-June. The park scenic loop for Golden Eagle, bighorn and prairie dog towns with Burrowing Owls in them, then out onto the national grassland for Long-billed Curlew and Lark Bunting on unmarked gravel. Take water and watch the gumbo after rain.

  1. 1Sheyenne National Grassland
  2. 2Tewaukon National Wildlife Refuge

4 days

The Sheyenne leks

Fargo (FAR), Lisbon 3 nights, back to Fargo

A late April trip for the last Greater Prairie Chickens in the state, fewer than two hundred birds on tallgrass sand dunes. Arrange lek viewing through the forest service office, be in place well before light, and spend the rest of each day on the refuge for early waterfowl.

The Missouri Coteau potholes

Half the American White Pelicans in the country hatch on one North Dakota alkali lake

The Coteau is a belt of glacial hummock and pothole running across the middle of North Dakota, and Chase Lake in the centre of it hatches up to half of all American White Pelicans. Between 300 and 350 Piping Plovers breed on the McLean County alkali lakes, out of a Great Plains population of about 1,700.

  • Habitat: Alkali and freshwater pothole lakes with gravel shorelines, mixed-grass prairie on glacial hummock, wet meadow and emergent marsh.
  • When: Mid-May to early July for the breeding birds, and September and October for the crane staging at Horsehead Lake.
  • Getting there: Bismarck is the airport and Jamestown a workable base. Chase Lake refuge is reached on gravel section roads north-west of Medina, and the pelican colony itself is closed and watched from a distance.
  • Base yourself in: Jamestown
  • Look for: American White Pelican, Piping Plover, Sandhill Crane, American Avocet, Wilson's Phalarope, Marbled Godwit, Willet, Western Grebe, Clark's Grebe, Nelson's Sparrow, Chestnut-collared Longspur, Yellow-headed Blackbird
  • Also covers: Chain of Lakes, McLean County, Chase Lake National Wildlife Refuge, Horsehead Lake, Long Lake National Wildlife Refuge
  • Also watch for: The prairie holds badger, coyote and the largest remaining populations of Richardson ground squirrel.

Lostwood and the Souris grasslands

Native mixed-grass prairie that has never been ploughed, and the sparrows and pipits that need it

Lostwood is unbroken native prairie kept by fire, and it holds better numbers of Baird's Sparrow and Sprague's Pipit than anywhere else in the state. Des Lacs and Upper Souris in the same corner add the wetland list, and the Appam alkali lake takes as many as fifty pairs of Piping Plover in a wet year.

  • Habitat: Unbroken mixed-grass prairie on glacial moraine, aspen bluff, alkali and freshwater pothole, and a river valley with impoundments.
  • When: Mid-May to early July, and the pipits are best in the first hour after sunrise when they song-flight.
  • Getting there: Minot is the airport and Kenmare the base. Lostwood has a gravel auto tour route and a designated wilderness on foot, and the refuge roads close when wet.
  • Base yourself in: Kenmare
  • Look for: Baird's Sparrow, Sprague's Pipit, LeConte's Sparrow, Clay-colored Sparrow, Grasshopper Sparrow, Sharp-tailed Grouse, Piping Plover, Snow Goose, Tundra Swan, Canvasback, Redhead, Ruddy Duck
  • Also covers: Appam Lake Waterfowl Protection Area, Des Lacs National Wildlife Refuge, Lostwood National Wildlife Refuge, Upper Souris National Wildlife Refuge
  • Also watch for: Moose have moved into the aspen bluffs and the prairie holds white-tailed jackrabbit.

Devils Lake and the pothole district

A lake with no outlet that has trebled in size, and hundreds of small wetlands around it

Devils Lake has no outlet, so it rises and floods farmland and falls again, and the Waterfowl Production Areas around it are the working core of the prairie pothole system. Sullys Hill on its south shore is wooded hill with bison and elk, which is unusual ground in this state.

  • Habitat: Large alkaline lake with drowned timber, hundreds of small pothole wetlands and Waterfowl Production Areas, wooded morainal hill, and cropland between.
  • When: Late May to early July for the breeding birds, and April and October for the waterfowl passage.
  • Getting there: Grand Forks is the airport, ninety minutes east, and Devils Lake the base. Sullys Hill has a paid auto tour loop, and most of the Production Areas are open access reached from gravel section roads.
  • Base yourself in: Devils Lake
  • Look for: LeConte's Sparrow, Swamp Sparrow, Grasshopper Sparrow, Clay-colored Sparrow, Bobolink, Western Meadowlark, Sedge Wren, American Bittern, Black-crowned Night Heron, White-faced Ibis, Northern Harrier, Wild Turkey
  • Also covers: Devils Lake Wetland Management District, Kellys Slough National Wildlife Refuge, Lake Alice National Wildlife Refuge, Sullys Hill National Game Preserve
  • Also watch for: Sullys Hill carries a fenced bison and elk herd and the surrounding country holds moose in the aspen.

The Little Missouri badlands

Eroded clay buttes, with grassland birds in very large numbers on the tables above

The Little Missouri has cut the western edge of the state into buttes and coulees, and the national grassland around the park carries tens of thousands of grassland birds: good numbers of Sprague's Pipit, Baird's Sparrow, Long-billed Curlew, Lark Bunting and Dickcissel on the same ground.

  • Habitat: Eroded clay and sandstone badland, mixed-grass prairie on the tables above, juniper draw and cottonwood along the river, and prairie dog town.
  • When: Mid-May to early July for the breeding birds. The park road is open all year but the gravel grassland tracks turn to gumbo when wet.
  • Getting there: Bismarck is the airport, two hours east, and Medora the base. Theodore Roosevelt charges a park fee and has a paved scenic loop, and the grassland around it is public land on unmarked gravel roads.
  • Base yourself in: Medora
  • Look for: Sprague's Pipit, Baird's Sparrow, Long-billed Curlew, Lark Bunting, Dickcissel, Golden Eagle, Ferruginous Hawk, Burrowing Owl, Mountain Bluebird, Say's Phoebe, Rock Wren, Sharp-tailed Grouse
  • Also covers: Little Missouri National Grassland, Theodore Roosevelt National Park
  • Also watch for: Bison, elk, bighorn sheep, feral horses and prairie dog towns are all in the park, and the dog towns are what the Burrowing Owls use.

The Sheyenne grassland

The last viable prairie chicken population in the state, on tallgrass sand dunes

The Sheyenne is tallgrass on sand dunes at the eastern edge of the state, and it holds the last viable Greater Prairie Chicken population there: fewer than two hundred birds, and declining. It is the only tallgrass national grassland in the country.

  • Habitat: Tallgrass prairie on stabilised sand dune, sedge meadow in the swales, oak savanna and riparian woodland along the Sheyenne River.
  • When: Mid-April to early May at dawn for the prairie chicken leks, and June for the grassland breeders.
  • Getting there: Fargo is the airport, an hour east, and Lisbon the base. The grassland is public land on gravel roads with cattle grazing throughout, and lek viewing is arranged through the forest service office.
  • Base yourself in: Lisbon
  • Look for: Greater Prairie Chicken, Sharp-tailed Grouse, Upland Sandpiper, Bobolink, Grasshopper Sparrow, Field Sparrow, Sedge Wren, Dickcissel, Western Meadowlark, Northern Harrier, Short-eared Owl, American Bittern
  • Also covers: Sheyenne National Grassland, Tewaukon National Wildlife Refuge
  • Also watch for: The dunes carry the western prairie fringed orchid, and moose use the wooded draws along the river.

Frequently Asked Questions

When is the best time to go birding in North Dakota?

The first three weeks of June. The prairie birds arrive late and stop singing early, so the useful window is genuinely that short. Mid-April is the exception, for the Sheyenne prairie chicken leks, and September for the crane staging.

Where do I find Baird's Sparrow and Sprague's Pipit?

Lostwood National Wildlife Refuge in the north-west, which holds better numbers of both than anywhere else in the state because it is unbroken native prairie managed with fire. Both sing in the first hour after sunrise and go quiet soon after.

Can I see the Chase Lake pelican colony?

From a distance. Up to half the American White Pelicans in the country hatch at Chase Lake in North Dakota, and the nesting islands are closed. The refuge boundary and the surrounding section roads give the view without putting the colony at risk.

What are the roads like?

The interstates and state highways are fast and empty. The refuge and grassland tracks are gravel section road, and after heavy rain the clay turns to gumbo that will strand an ordinary car. Ask locally before committing to one in wet weather.

Is there anything to see in winter?

Not much, honestly. Snowy Owl, Snow Bunting, Sharp-tailed Grouse and wintering raptors in the badlands, in extreme cold and short daylight. The state is a June destination and there is little point pretending otherwise.

Can I bird here with limited mobility?

Sites on this page differ from each other more than any rating could carry, so this is the framework the standard British guides use, and the reserve's own page and warden hold the answers.

  • Permission · some reserves open ground to disabled visitors that is closed to everyone else, and it is granted by asking.
  • Priority · parking bays, hides and the nearest paths are often held for disabled visitors. Ask what is reserved and how to claim it.
  • Parking · how close a car reaches the birds shapes the whole day. Ring ahead for the nearest hard standing to the water or the hide.
  • Paths · surfaces, gradients and gate widths vary inside a single reserve. Ask which trail is boarded, surfaced or level.
  • Perches · a hide with a low window, or a bench where the view opens, turns a viewpoint into a morning.

Every reserve above with its own link carries its access detail there, and a warden answers what a page cannot.
