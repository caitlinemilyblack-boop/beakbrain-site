Birding in Pennsylvania

Hawk Mountain was bought in 1934 to stop the shooting of migrating raptors, which makes it the first refuge for birds of prey established anywhere in the world, and the ridge it sits on runs the length of the state.

At a glance

Best months
Mid-September to November for the ridge raptor flight, late February for the Middle Creek geese, and late May to July for the Pocono bogs and the plateau forest

Birds that decide the trip

Broad-winged Hawk over Hawk Mountain

They migrate in kettles of hundreds circling on rising air, and the third week of September on the Kittatinny is when the largest of those pass over.

When The third week of September, on a north-west wind.

Where Hawk Mountain and the Kittatinny

Golden Eagle down the ridge

The eastern population is small and passes late, so the Kittatinny lookouts see them in early November when most watchers have already gone home for the year.

When Late October to November, on a north-west wind.

Where Hawk Mountain and the Kittatinny

Snow Goose at Middle Creek

More than a hundred thousand can be on one lake in the last week of February, and they lift off together at first light in a sheet that takes minutes to clear the water.

When Late February and early March, at dawn.

Where Middle Creek and the lower Susquehanna

Piping Plover on Presque Isle

The Great Lakes population is small and separate, and birds returned to nest on this spit after decades away, which is why the beach sections are roped in summer.

When May to August, on roped beach sections.

Where Presque Isle and the Lake Erie shore

Canada Warbler in the Pocono bog

It breeds in wet thickets under spruce and tamarack, and the Pocono kettle bogs are close to the southern edge of that habitat on the continent.

When Late May to July, in bog thickets.

Where The Pocono bogs

Blackburnian Warbler in the old hemlock

It sings from the very top of tall conifers, and the four-hundred-year-old hemlock at Tionesta and Cook Forest is exactly what it wants and what the state mostly no longer has.

When Late May to July, in mature hemlock.

Where The Allegheny Plateau, The Pocono bogs

Little Gull on Lake Erie

The smallest gull in the world turns up among the Bonaparte's flocks off the peninsula most autumns and winters, and picking one out is a matter of patience with a scope.

When October to March, among the Bonaparte's flocks.

Where Presque Isle and the Lake Erie shore

Short-eared Owl over the grassland

The grassland managed for nesting sparrows at Middle Creek holds hunting owls through the winter. They work the fields on long floppy wings from mid-afternoon.

When December to February, from mid-afternoon.

Where Middle Creek and the lower Susquehanna

When to Go

Pennsylvania is best known for its autumn, and rightly: the ridge lookouts count a whole raptor migration from September to December. Late February brings the goose spectacle at Middle Creek, and June is when the northern breeding birds in the bogs and on the plateau are worth the drive.

The goose peak

Late February and early March

Middle Creek fills with Snow Geese and Tundra Swans, often more than a hundred thousand birds.

Go for Middle Creek at dawn, and the lower Susquehanna for eagles.

Trade off The ridge lookouts are shut and the plateau is under snow.

Typical daily average

The spring passage

Late April to May

Migrants pile onto Presque Isle before the lake crossing.

Go for Presque Isle at first light, and the Delaware Water Gap for the first breeders.

Trade off The bogs are still cold and the geese have gone north.

Typical daily average

The breeding season

Late May to July

The Pocono bogs and the Allegheny old growth hold their northern breeding lists.

Go for Tannersville bog, the Tionesta old growth, and Cook Forest.

Trade off The passage sites are quiet and the lake shore is full of beach visitors.

Typical daily average

The raptor flight

Mid-September to early December

The Kittatinny lookouts count the whole autumn raptor migration off one ridge.

Go for Hawk Mountain, Bake Oven Knob and Waggoners Gap on a north-west wind.

Trade off The best days depend on the wind, and the lookouts are cold and exposed.

The Kittatinny in autumn

Ridge, plateau and bogs

Philadelphia (PHL), Stroudsburg 2 nights, Kempton 1 night, Warren 3 nights, out of Pittsburgh (PIT)

A June week from the glaciated bogs in the east to the old growth in the north-west. Tannersville is one of the southernmost boreal bogs anywhere, and the Tionesta hemlock and beech are four hundred years old, which is very little of what the state has left.

Hawk Mountain and the Kittatinny

The first refuge for birds of prey anywhere in the world, on a ridge that carries the whole autumn flight

Hawk Mountain was bought in 1934 to stop the shooting of migrating raptors on the ridge, which makes it the first sanctuary for birds of prey established anywhere. The Kittatinny runs the length of the state and the updraft along it carries the flight, so several lookouts count the same birds on the same days.

  • Habitat: Quartzite ridge with boulder fields and stunted oak, mixed hardwood on the slopes, and farmland in the valleys either side.
  • When: Mid-September to early December. Broad-wings peak in the third week of September and Golden Eagles in early November.
  • Getting there: Allentown or Philadelphia are the airports and Kempton the base. Hawk Mountain charges an admission fee and has a paid trail to the lookouts, and Bake Oven Knob and Waggoners Gap are free.
  • Base yourself in: Kempton
  • Look for: Broad-winged Hawk, Sharp-shinned Hawk, Red-tailed Hawk, American Kestrel, Merlin, Peregrine Falcon, Golden Eagle, Bald Eagle, American Goshawk, Northern Harrier, Osprey, Red-shouldered Hawk
  • Parks and reserves: Hawk Mountain Sanctuary (Reserve), State Game Lands 217 (Protected area), Weiser State Forest (Forest or grassland), Tuscarora State Forest (Forest or grassland), State Game Lands Number 168 (Protected area)
  • Also covers: Bake Oven Knob, Hawk Mountain Sanctuary, State Game Lands 217, State Game Lands Number 168, Tuscarora State Forest, Waggoners Gap, Weiser State Forest
  • Also watch for: Black bear cross the ridge regularly and the boulder fields hold timber rattlesnake.

The Allegheny Plateau

Old-growth hemlock and beech on a plateau that was logged flat and grew back

Almost all of northern Pennsylvania was cut over between 1890 and 1930, and the forest that has grown back is the second-growth that makes up most of the state. Tionesta and Cook Forest hold small stands the loggers missed, with hemlock and beech four hundred years old.

  • Habitat: Second-growth northern hardwood of cherry, maple and beech, old-growth hemlock and beech stands, spruce plantations, and reservoir shoreline.
  • When: Late May to July for the breeding birds, and September for the elk rut on the plateau to the south.
  • Getting there: Pittsburgh is the airport, two hours south, and Warren the base. The national forest is free with gravel roads, Cook Forest charges a small state park fee, and the Tionesta stands are reached on short marked trails.
  • Base yourself in: Warren
  • Look for: Blackburnian Warbler, Black-throated Green Warbler, Magnolia Warbler, Canada Warbler, Winter Wren, Northern Saw-whet Owl, Barred Owl, Ruffed Grouse, Pileated Woodpecker, Hermit Thrush, Veery, Northern Raven
  • Parks and reserves: Allegheny National Forest (Forest or grassland), Cook Forest State Park (State or provincial park), Hickory Creek Wilderness (Protected area), Clear Creek State Forest (Forest or grassland), Clarion Wild and Scenic River (Protected area)
  • Also covers: Allegheny National Forest, Clarion Wild and Scenic River, Clear Creek State Forest, Cook Forest State Park, Hickory Creek Wilderness, Tionesta Scenic Area
  • Also watch for: Elk were reintroduced to the plateau and the herd now runs to about a thousand animals, with a viewing centre near Benezette.

Presque Isle and the Lake Erie shore

A sand spit curving seven miles into Lake Erie, and the only stretch of Great Lakes shore the state has

Presque Isle is a moving sand spit that hooks out into Lake Erie, with beach, dune, pond and thicket packed into a narrow arc. Pennsylvania has only about fifty miles of Great Lakes shore and this is the best of it, taking a whole migration into a peninsula a birder can walk.

  • Habitat: Sand beach and moving dune, interdunal pond and marsh, cottonwood and willow thicket, mature ridge forest on the older sand, and open lake water.
  • When: Early May and September to October for the passage, and December to March for sea duck and gulls.
  • Getting there: Erie has a small airport and is the base. Presque Isle is free with a road the length of the peninsula, and the car parks fill in summer with beach visitors rather than birders.
  • Base yourself in: Erie
  • Look for: Piping Plover, Common Tern, Long-tailed Duck, White-winged Scoter, Red-necked Grebe, Red-throated Loon, Bonaparte's Gull, Little Gull, Snowy Owl, Connecticut Warbler, Prothonotary Warbler, American Bittern
  • Parks and reserves: Presque Isle State Park (State or provincial park), Erie Bluffs State Park (State or provincial park), State Game Lands Number 101 (Protected area), State Game Lands Number 314 (Protected area), Wintergreen Gorge (Protected area)
  • Also covers: Conneaut Harbor, Erie Bluffs State Park, Presque Isle State Park, State Game Lands Number 101, State Game Lands Number 314, Wintergreen Gorge
  • Also watch for: The spit moves measurably along the shore each year, and its ponds hold a rich amphibian community.

The Pocono bogs

Glacial bogs on a plateau, holding a northern breeding list at the southern edge of it

The Poconos are the southern end of the glaciated Appalachian plateau, and the kettle bogs left behind hold sphagnum, tamarack and black spruce. Tannersville is one of the southernmost boreal bogs on the continent and the birds in it belong several hundred miles north.

  • Habitat: Sphagnum kettle bog with tamarack and black spruce, hemlock ravines, northern hardwood on the Pennsylvania plateau, and the river gap cut through the ridge at the state line.
  • When: Late May to July for the breeding birds, and mid-September to November for the raptor flight through the gap.
  • Getting there: Newark or Philadelphia are the airports and Stroudsburg the base. The recreation area is free, Tannersville bog is a Nature Conservancy boardwalk with guided access only, and Bruce Lake is a walk-in natural area.
  • Base yourself in: Stroudsburg
  • Look for: Northern Saw-whet Owl, Canada Warbler, Blackburnian Warbler, Magnolia Warbler, Nashville Warbler, Alder Flycatcher, Winter Wren, Veery, Golden-winged Warbler, Cerulean Warbler, Bald Eagle, Ruffed Grouse
  • Parks and reserves: Delaware Water Gap National Recreation Area (National site), Bruce Lake Natural Area (Protected area), Tannersville Cranberry Bog Preserve, Delaware State Forest (Forest or grassland), Cherry Valley National Wildlife Refuge
  • Also covers: Bruce Lake Natural Area, Cherry Valley National Wildlife Refuge, Delaware State Forest, Delaware Water Gap National Recreation Area, Tannersville Cranberry Bog Preserve
  • Also watch for: The bogs hold carnivorous plants and bog turtles, and black bear are common through the whole plateau.

Middle Creek and the lower Susquehanna

A hundred thousand Snow Geese on one lake for about three weeks in late winter

Middle Creek fills with Snow Geese in late February and early March, often more than a hundred thousand of them on a single lake, with Tundra Swans among them. The lower Susquehanna below it holds the state's Bald Eagles and a mudflat that appears when the river drops.

  • Habitat: Shallow reservoir and managed impoundment, farmed goose fields, grassland managed for nesting sparrows, and river mudflat and island in the Susquehanna.
  • When: Late February and early March for the goose peak, and May to July for the grassland breeders.
  • Getting there: Harrisburg or Philadelphia are the airports and Lancaster the base. Middle Creek is free with a driving tour and a visitor centre, the goose numbers are posted through the season, and the Conejohela mudflats are watched from the shore or by boat.
  • Base yourself in: Lancaster
  • Look for: Snow Goose, Tundra Swan, Bald Eagle, American White Pelican, Sandhill Crane, Short-eared Owl, Northern Harrier, Stilt Sandpiper, Pectoral Sandpiper, American Golden Plover, Grasshopper Sparrow, Dickcissel
  • Parks and reserves: Middle Creek Wildlife Management Area (National wildlife refuge), State Game Lands Number 46 (Protected area), William Penn State Forest (Forest or grassland), Susquehannock State Park (State or provincial park), State Game Lands Number 156 (Protected area)
  • Also covers: Conejohela Flats, Middle Creek Wildlife Management Area, State Game Lands Number 156, State Game Lands Number 46, Susquehanna River, Susquehannock State Park, William Penn State Forest
  • Also watch for: The area holds a large white-tailed deer population and the river carries one of the more important shad runs on the east coast.

Frequently Asked Questions

When is the best time to go birding in Pennsylvania?

The second half of September, for the Broad-winged Hawk peak on the Kittatinny ridge. Late February is the other date, for a hundred thousand Snow Geese at Middle Creek, and June for the bogs and the old growth.

Why is Hawk Mountain historically important?

Raptors were shot in large numbers from the ridge for bounties, and in 1934 the land was bought specifically to stop it. That makes it the first refuge for birds of prey anywhere in the world, and it has counted the flight from the same lookouts ever since.

Which lookout should I use?

Hawk Mountain has the visitor centre, the counters and the interpretation, and charges admission. Bake Oven Knob and Waggoners Gap are free, quieter and see the same birds on the same days. On a good north-west wind any of the three works.

How reliable are the Middle Creek goose numbers?

The area posts counts through the season, so the peak is checkable rather than guessed. It usually falls in the last week of February, and the birds can leave in a day when the weather changes, so watch the reports and be flexible.

Is there any old-growth forest left?

Very little. Almost all of northern Pennsylvania was cut between 1890 and 1930, and Tionesta and Cook Forest hold a few hundred acres each that the loggers missed, with hemlock and beech four hundred years old. They are worth the detour for that reason alone.

Can I bird here with limited mobility?

Sites on this page differ from each other more than any rating could carry, so this is the framework the standard British guides use, and the reserve's own page and warden hold the answers.

  • Permission · some reserves open ground to disabled visitors that is closed to everyone else, and it is granted by asking.
  • Priority · parking bays, hides and the nearest paths are often held for disabled visitors. Ask what is reserved and how to claim it.
  • Parking · how close a car reaches the birds shapes the whole day. Ring ahead for the nearest hard standing to the water or the hide.
  • Paths · surfaces, gradients and gate widths vary inside a single reserve. Ask which trail is boarded, surfaced or level.
  • Perches · a hide with a low window, or a bench where the view opens, turns a viewpoint into a morning.
