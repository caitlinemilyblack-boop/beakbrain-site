Atlas / Vermont

Birding in Vermont

Vermont is small, wooded and steep, and the birds that make it worth the drive sit at the two ends of its elevation: boreal bog in the northeast and a thrush on the summit ridge.

Birds that decide the trip

Bicknell's Thrush on Mount Mansfield

It breeds only in stunted fir near the summits of a handful of north-eastern mountains, and the Mansfield toll road takes a car most of the way to that habitat. It sings at dusk and again before first light.

When Mid-June to mid-July, at dusk on the summit ridge.

Where Mount Mansfield and the Green Mountains

Spruce Grouse in the Nulhegan spruce

It sits still and lets people walk past, which is why it is missed far more often than it is absent. The Nulhegan Basin gravel roads at first light are the way to find one.

When All year, most easily in June on quiet forest tracks.

Where The Northeast Kingdom and the boreal bogs

Canada Jay at Moose Bog

It comes to a stopped car rather than the other way round, and the small parking area at Moose Bog is where that most reliably happens in the state.

When All year, at the bog car park and along the road.

Where The Northeast Kingdom and the boreal bogs

Snow Goose at Dead Creek

Tens of thousands stage on the fields beside the road in late autumn, and the state maintains a viewing area specifically for the crowd that comes to watch them.

When Late October to November, at dawn and dusk.

Where The Champlain Valley

Black-backed Woodpecker in the bog spruce

It works dead and dying spruce and flakes bark rather than drumming. The flooded margins of the Kingdom bogs give it a steady supply of standing dead timber.

When All year, in dead spruce at the bog edges.

Where The Northeast Kingdom and the boreal bogs

Blackpoll Warbler on the ridge

It breeds in the same stunted fir as the thrush and sings a very high thin trill that many people cannot hear at all, then flies to South America non-stop over the ocean in autumn.

When Mid-June to July, on the summit ridges.

Where Mount Mansfield and the Green Mountains

American Bittern in the Champlain marsh

Its pumping call carries a long way across flat marsh at dusk, and the Missisquoi delta at the top of the lake is the largest piece of that habitat in the state.

When May to July, at dusk in the marsh.

Where The Champlain Valley

Mourning Warbler in the regrowth

It skulks in dense young growth after logging or blowdown, and the working forest in the Kingdom keeps producing exactly that at a scale the rest of the state does not.

When June and July, in dense regrowth.

Where The Northeast Kingdom and the boreal bogs

When to Go

Vermont works from late May to July and again for a few weeks in autumn. The summit thrush and the boreal bog birds are a June and July proposition, and the goose staging in the Champlain valley is a November one.

Spring passage

Late April to May

Warblers move up the valleys and the marsh birds arrive on Lake Champlain.

Go for The Champlain valley wetlands and the lower Connecticut River.

Trade off The summit ridges are still under snow and the bogs are cold.

Typical daily average

The breeding season

Mid-June to July

Bicknell's Thrush sings on the summit ridges and the boreal birds hold the bogs.

Go for Mount Mansfield at dusk, and Moose Bog and the Nulhegan Basin at dawn.

Trade off Blackflies are severe in June and the summit road charges a toll.

Typical daily average

The goose staging

Late October to November

Snow Geese stage at Dead Creek in tens of thousands on their way south.

Go for The Dead Creek viewing area at dawn and dusk.

Trade off The breeding birds have gone and the mountains are shutting for winter.

Typical daily average

The winter finches

December to March

Irruption years bring crossbills, redpolls and Bohemian Waxwings down into the valleys.

Go for The Northeast Kingdom roads and the Champlain valley hedgerows.

Trade off It is severely cold and most back roads are snow.

7 days

Summit to bog

Burlington (BTV), Stowe 2 nights, Island Pond 2 nights, Brattleboro 1 night, Vergennes 1 night, back to Burlington

Six days in the second half of June, which is the only window that gives every end of the state. The Mansfield toll road at dusk for Bicknell's Thrush, then east into the Kingdom for the bog birds, south to the Connecticut River for the valley warblers, and a last day on the Champlain marshes. About 350 miles.

4 days

Bicknell's Thrush on Mansfield

Burlington (BTV), Stowe 3 nights, back to Burlington

Three days in late June for a thrush that breeds only in stunted fir on a handful of north-eastern summits. The toll road takes a car most of the way up Mansfield, the birds sing at dusk and again before dawn, and the ridge is cold and exposed at any hour.

The Northeast Kingdom bogs

Burlington (BTV), Island Pond 3 nights, back to Burlington

Three June days in black spruce and tamarack, which is as close to Canada as the state gets. Moose Bog has a short boardwalk to the sphagnum, the Nulhegan roads are gravel and quiet, and Spruce Grouse, Canada Jay and Black-backed Woodpecker are all here.

The Champlain valley in autumn

Burlington (BTV), Vergennes 3 nights, back to Burlington

Three days in the first half of November for the Snow Goose staging at Dead Creek, which builds into tens of thousands on the fields beside the road. Missisquoi at the northern end of the lake adds late waterfowl and a large heron colony.

Mount Mansfield and the Green Mountains

Bicknell's Thrush on the summit ridge, one of the smallest breeding ranges in North America

The stunted spruce and fir on the highest Vermont summits is the world stronghold of Bicknell's Thrush, a bird that breeds on a handful of northeastern mountaintops and winters on Hispaniola. Mount Mansfield carries a toll road to the ridge, which puts the habitat within reach of anyone.

  • Base yourself in: Stowe
  • Look for: Bicknell's Thrush, Blackpoll Warbler, Swainson's Thrush, Yellow-bellied Flycatcher, White-throated Sparrow, Winter Wren, Blue-headed Vireo, Magnolia Warbler, Canada Warbler, Mourning Warbler, Peregrine Falcon, Ruffed Grouse, Hermit Thrush, Purple Finch
  • Parks and reserves: Green Mountain National Forest, Mount Mansfield State Forest, Mount Mansfield Natural Area (Protected area), Camel's Hump State Park (State or provincial park), Lye Brook Wilderness (Protected area)
  • Stops:
    • : Stunted spruce and fir along the summit ridge above the toll road. Bicknell's Thrush sings here at first light from late May, and the birds are heard far more often than seen.
  • Also covers: Camel’s Hump, Camel's Hump State Park, Green Mountain National Forest, Lye Brook Wilderness, Mount Mansfield Natural Area, Mount Mansfield ridge, Mount Mansfield State Forest, Stowe
  • Also watch for: Moose browse the wetlands below the ridge, and black bear range the whole range.

The Northeast Kingdom and the boreal bogs

Spruce Grouse and Boreal Chickadee at the southern edge of the boreal forest

The far north-east corner of Vermont holds black spruce bog and lowland conifer that belongs several hundred miles north, and it is the only part of the state with Spruce Grouse, Canada Jay and Boreal Chickadee together. Most of it is working forest reached on gravel.

  • Base yourself in: Island Pond
  • Look for: Spruce Grouse, Black-backed Woodpecker, American Three-toed Woodpecker, Boreal Chickadee, Canada Jay, Rusty Blackbird, Palm Warbler, Lincoln's Sparrow, Cape May Warbler, Bay-breasted Warbler, Northern Saw-whet Owl, Common Loon, Ring-necked Duck, Philadelphia Vireo
  • Parks and reserves: Silvio O. The Champlain Valley

Twenty thousand Snow Geese over Dead Creek in October

The lowland between the Green Mountains and Lake Champlain is the warmest and most open ground in Vermont, and it takes a migration the mountains never see. Dead Creek fills with Snow Geese each autumn, and the delta marshes at Missisquoi hold the state’s marsh birds.

  • Base yourself in: Vergennes
  • Look for: Snow Goose, Black Tern, American Bittern, Least Bittern, Virginia Rail, Sora, Marsh Wren, Northern Harrier, Short-eared Owl, Osprey, Bald Eagle, Wood Duck, Hooded Merganser, Bobolink, Eastern Meadowlark, Upland Sandpiper
  • Parks and reserves: Missisquoi National Wildlife Refuge, Dead Creek Wildlife Management Area, Adirondack Park (Protected area), Maquam Bay Wildlife Management Area (National wildlife refuge), Mud Creek Wildlife Management Area (National wildlife refuge)
  • Stops:
    • : A roadside pull-in on Route 17 looking over the flooded fields. The Snow Goose flock builds through October and lifts in one body when an eagle passes.
  • Also covers: Adirondack Park, Burlington, Dead Creek goose viewing area, Dead Creek Wildlife Management Area, Maquam Bay Wildlife Management Area, Missisquoi National Wildlife Refuge, Mud Creek Wildlife Management Area, Swanton, Vergennes
  • Also watch for: The clay plain holds the state’s only significant population of spiny softshell turtle, in the lake shallows.

The Connecticut River valley

A river that funnels passage down the eastern edge of the state

The Connecticut River makes the whole eastern border, and the coves and meadows along it concentrate migrants moving down the valley. Herrick’s Cove at Rockingham is the best known of them, and it works in both passages.

  • Base yourself in: Brattleboro
  • Look for: Bald Eagle, Osprey, Wood Duck, Green Heron, Great Blue Heron, American Woodcock, Wilson's Snipe, Veery, Northern Waterthrush, Nashville Warbler, Chestnut-sided Warbler, Blackburnian Warbler, Barred Owl, Common Nighthawk
  • Parks and reserves: Windmill Ridge Nature Reserve, Wantastiquet Mountain Natural Area (Protected area), Black Mountain Natural Area (Protected area), Retreat Farm (Protected area), Stephen Martin Memorial Wildlife Sanctuary (Reserve)
  • Stops:
    • : A backwater where the Williams River meets the Connecticut, walked from a car park off Route 5. Migrant warblers work the floodplain maples in May.
  • Also covers: Bellows Falls, Black Mountain Natural Area, Brattleboro, Herrick's Cove, Retreat Farm, Retreat Meadows, Stephen Martin Memorial Wildlife Sanctuary, Wantastiquet Mountain Natural Area, Windmill Ridge Nature Reserve
  • Also watch for: River otter work the coves, and the silver maple floodplain is among the least altered in New England.

Frequently Asked Questions

When is the best time to go birding in Vermont?

The second half of June, which is the only window that gives both Bicknell's Thrush on the summit ridges and the boreal birds in the Northeast Kingdom bogs. Early November is the other date, for the Snow Goose staging at Dead Creek.

How do I find Bicknell's Thrush?

Mount Mansfield, on the toll road, in the second half of June. The birds sing at dusk and again before first light from stunted fir near the ridge, so it is an evening or a very early morning rather than a daytime walk. Dress for cold and wind.

What is the Northeast Kingdom?

The three counties in the far north-east, mostly working forest with black spruce and tamarack bogs in the low ground. Moose Bog and the Nulhegan Basin hold Spruce Grouse, Canada Jay, Boreal Chickadee and Black-backed Woodpecker within a short drive of each other.

Are the blackflies really that bad?

In June, yes, particularly in the bogs and on the logging roads. A head net is worth carrying and the birds are worth the discomfort, but nobody should be surprised by it.

Is there anything in winter?

In an irruption year, crossbills, redpolls, Pine Grosbeaks and Bohemian Waxwings come down into the valleys and the Kingdom roads are the place to look. In a quiet winter there is very little, and the back roads are heavy going.

Can I bird here with limited mobility?

Sites on this page differ from each other more than any rating could carry, so this is the framework the standard British guides use, and the reserve's own page and warden hold the answers.

  • Permission · some reserves open ground to disabled visitors that is closed to everyone else, and it is granted by asking.
  • Priority · parking bays, hides and the nearest paths are often held for disabled visitors. Ask what is reserved and how to claim it.
  • Parking · how close a car reaches the birds shapes the whole day. Ring ahead for the nearest hard standing to the water or the hide.
  • Paths · surfaces, gradients and gate widths vary inside a single reserve. Ask which trail is boarded, surfaced or level.
  • Perches · a hide with a low window, or a bench where the view opens, turns a viewpoint into a morning.
