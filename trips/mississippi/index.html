Atlas / Mississippi

Birding in Mississippi

One county in Mississippi holds a Sandhill Crane that lives nowhere else on earth, about a hundred birds in wet pine savanna, and the state has the last large undammed river in the lower states running past it.

Birds that decide the trip

Mississippi Sandhill Crane in the savanna

A non-migratory form down to about a hundred birds. It lives in wet pine savanna in one county and nowhere else, the refuge exists for it, and the guided winter walks are the way to see one.

When January to March, on refuge guided walks.

Where The Gulf coast and the crane savanna

Red-cockaded Woodpecker at Noxubee

The refuge has managed and monitored its clusters for decades and marks the cavity trees, which makes it one of the more dependable places anywhere to watch the birds come out at dawn.

When April to June, at first light around the marked clusters.

Where Noxubee and the prairie belt, The Pascagoula and the pine forest

Swallow-tailed Kite over the Pascagoula

It hunts low over undammed river swamp without seeming to beat a wing, and the Pascagoula is the kind of intact bottomland it needs. Gone south by early September.

When April to August, over the river bottoms.

Where The Pascagoula and the pine forest

Least Tern on the Biloxi sand

The main colony nests on open beach between a busy road and the sea, which is a difficult place to raise young and is why the roped enclosures matter.

When May to July, on the roped beach sections.

Where The Gulf coast and the crane savanna

Prothonotary Warbler in the Delta brakes

It nests in holes over standing water in the cypress brakes, and the refuges in the Yazoo basin are where that habitat survives at all.

When April to July, over standing water.

Where The Yazoo basin refuges

Swainson's Warbler in the cane

It keeps to dense cane and thicket on wet ground and is heard long before it is seen. The undammed Pascagoula and the Yazoo basin bottoms both hold it.

When April to June, from thick cover at dawn.

Where The Pascagoula and the pine forest, The Yazoo basin refuges

Louisiana Waterthrush on Bear Creek in Mississippi

It needs a clear fast stream under closed canopy, which the sandstone country in the north-east has and the rest of the state mostly does not.

When April to June, along shaded rocky streams.

Where The Tishomingo hills

Snow Goose over the Delta

The Yazoo basin refuges take large wintering flocks that feed on the surrounding farmland and lift off the refuge water together in the morning.

When December to February, on the refuge impoundments.

Where The Yazoo basin refuges

When to Go

Two seasons matter here and they barely overlap. Winter is for the cranes on the burned savanna and the ducks in the flooded Delta reservoirs; late spring is for the breeding warblers and the woodpeckers. High summer is hot, wet and quiet.

The crane winter

January to March

The savanna is burned open and the resident cranes are easiest to see against it.

Go for The crane refuge on a guided walk, and the Yazoo basin refuges for waterfowl.

Trade off The breeding warblers are still in Central America.

Typical daily average

The breeding season

April to June

The bottomland and pine breeders are singing across the whole state.

Go for Noxubee and De Soto for woodpeckers, the Yazoo refuges and Tishomingo for warblers.

Trade off The Delta interior tracks flood and the heat builds fast from May.

Typical daily average

The tern colonies

May to July

Least Terns nest on open sand along the Biloxi beaches and the barrier islands.

Go for The Biloxi beach colonies and a ferry to Ship Island.

Trade off It is very hot on open sand and the colonies are roped off.

Typical daily average

The flooded reservoirs

November to February

Greentree reservoirs go under water and the Yazoo basin refuges fill with ducks and geese.

Go for Yazoo, Panther Swamp and Morgan Brake, and Noxubee for the goose flock.

Trade off The forest is quiet and the roads through the bottoms are often shut.

8 days

Coast to hills

Gulfport (GPT), Ocean Springs 2 nights, Wiggins 2 nights, Yazoo City 2 nights, Starkville 1 night, out of Memphis (MEM)

An open-jaw run north in late April, at the point where every breeding bird in the state is back. The crane savanna first, then the undammed Pascagoula, then across to the Yazoo basin refuges, finishing on the woodpecker clusters at Noxubee. About 500 miles.

  2. 2Grand Bay National Wildlife Refuge
  3. 3Gulf Islands National Seashore

4 days

The crane savanna in winter

Gulfport (GPT), Ocean Springs 3 nights, back to Gulfport

Three days in February for a bird found in one county and nowhere else. Book onto a refuge guided walk, which is the only way into the savanna where the cranes actually are, and spend the other days on the marsh at Grand Bay and the sound for wintering waders.

5 days

The Delta refuges

Jackson (JAN), Yazoo City 4 nights, back to Jackson

Four days working a chain of refuges that hold the last bottomland forest in the Yazoo basin. Best in late April for the warblers or in January for the flooded reservoirs and the goose flocks. Gravel roads, boat ramps and very little signage.

  1. 1Noxubee National Wildlife Refuge
  2. 2Tombigbee National Forest
  3. 3Tishomingo State Park
  4. 4Pickwick Lake

4 days

Noxubee and the north-east hills

Memphis (MEM), Starkville 2 nights, Iuka 1 night, back to Memphis

A May trip pairing the most reliable woodpecker site in the state with the only corner of it that has real hills. Noxubee has marked cavity clusters and a paved through road, and Tishomingo two hours north has sandstone bluffs, rhododendron and a different warbler list.

The Gulf coast and the crane savanna

A crane that lives in one county and nowhere else on earth

The Mississippi Sandhill Crane is a non-migratory form down to about a hundred birds, and it survives only in the wet pine savanna of Jackson County. The refuge exists for it. Offshore, the barrier islands and the Biloxi beaches carry a Least Tern colony that nests on open sand between the roads and the sea.

  • Habitat: Wet pine savanna kept by fire, salt and brackish marsh, sand barrier island and beach, and tidal flat in the sound.
  • When: January to March for the cranes, when the savanna is burned and open, and May to July for the tern colonies.
  • Getting there: Gulfport or Mobile in Alabama are the airports and Ocean Springs is the base. The crane refuge runs guided walks in winter and has a visitor centre off Interstate 10, and the barrier islands are reached by ferry from Gulfport.
  • Base yourself in: Ocean Springs
  • Look for: Sandhill Crane, Least Tern, Black Skimmer, Wilson's Plover, Snowy Plover, American Oystercatcher, Reddish Egret, Clapper Rail, Seaside Sparrow, Brown Pelican, Magnificent Frigatebird, Royal Tern
  • Parks and reserves: Gulf Islands National Seashore (National site), Mississippi Sandhill Crane National Wildlife Refuge, Grand Bay National Wildlife Refuge, De Soto National Forest (Forest or grassland), Cedar Point Site (Protected area)
  • Also covers: Cedar Point Site, De Soto National Forest, Grand Bay National Wildlife Refuge, Gulf Coast Least Tern Colony, Gulf Islands National Seashore, Mississippi Sandhill Crane National Wildlife Refuge
  • Also watch for: Bottlenose dolphins work the Mississippi Sound and the savanna holds gopher tortoise and pitcher plant bogs.

The Pascagoula and the pine forest

The last large undammed river in the country, with fire-kept pine behind it

The Pascagoula runs eighty miles to the sea without a single dam, which makes it the largest undammed river in the lower states, and the swamp forest along it has never been cut off from its floods. The pine forest inland carries the woodpeckers.

  • Habitat: Bottomland hardwood and cypress along an undammed river, longleaf and slash pine kept open by fire, pitcher plant bog in the wet flats, and oxbow lakes.
  • When: April to June for the breeding birds. The river is high in spring, which is what keeps the swamp forest alive.
  • Getting there: Gulfport or Jackson are the airports and Wiggins the base. The Pascagoula management areas are reached down gravel roads that flood, and the woodpecker clusters in De Soto are marked with painted rings.
  • Base yourself in: Wiggins
  • Look for: Red-cockaded Woodpecker, Bachman's Sparrow, Brown-headed Nuthatch, Swallow-tailed Kite, Mississippi Kite, Prothonotary Warbler, Swainson's Warbler, Hooded Warbler, Northern Parula, Barred Owl, Chuck-will's-widow, Anhinga
  • Parks and reserves: Bienville National Forest (Forest or grassland), De Soto National Forest (Forest or grassland), Mississippi Sandhill Crane National Wildlife Refuge, Ward Bayou Wildlife Management Area (National wildlife refuge)
  • Also covers: Bienville National Forest, De Soto National Forest, Lower Pascagoula River, Mississippi Sandhill Crane National Wildlife Refuge, Ward Bayou Wildlife Management Area
  • Also watch for: The river holds the Gulf of Mexico sturgeon, and the Mississippi pine uplands have black bear moving back in from the west.

The Yazoo basin refuges

Six refuges holding what is left of the bottomland forest in the Yazoo basin

The Yazoo basin was almost entirely cleared for cotton and soybeans, and this chain of refuges protects the remnants: cypress brakes, oxbow lakes and the last bottomland hardwood. Delta National Forest is the only bottomland hardwood national forest in the country.

  • Habitat: Cypress brake and oxbow lake, bottomland hardwood of overcup oak and sweetgum, greentree reservoir flooded in winter, and farmed refuge ground.
  • When: April to June for the breeding warblers, and December to February when the reservoirs are flooded for ducks.
  • Getting there: Jackson is the airport, an hour south, and Yazoo City the base. The refuges have gravel roads and boat ramps, and interior tracks are impassable in spring flood.
  • Base yourself in: Yazoo City
  • Look for: Prothonotary Warbler, Swainson's Warbler, Cerulean Warbler, Kentucky Warbler, Mississippi Kite, Anhinga, Wood Duck, Snow Goose, Greater White-fronted Goose, Bald Eagle, Barred Owl, Purple Gallinule
  • Parks and reserves: Delta National Forest (Forest or grassland), Panther Swamp National Wildlife Area (Protected area)
  • Also covers: Delta National Forest, Hillside National Wildlife Refuge, Mathews Brake National Wildlife Refuge, Morgan Brake National Wildlife Refuge, Panther Swamp National Wildlife Area, Panther Swamp National Wildlife Refuge, Yazoo National Wildlife Refuge
  • Also watch for: The refuges hold black bear, alligator and one of the densest wintering populations of waterfowl on the flyway.

Noxubee and the prairie belt

A refuge built around two shallow lakes, holding woodpeckers and a winter goose flock

Noxubee sits on the edge of the black prairie belt, with two shallow lakes ringed by cypress and pine forest behind them. It carries an active Red-cockaded Woodpecker population that has been managed and monitored for decades, which makes it one of the more reliable places to see one.

  • Habitat: Shallow cypress-ringed lakes, mature loblolly and longleaf pine managed with fire, bottomland along the Noxubee River, and old field on the prairie soils.
  • When: April to June for the woodpeckers and warblers, and November to February for the geese and ducks on the lakes.
  • Getting there: Columbus or Jackson are the airports and Starkville the base. The refuge has a paved through road, a visitor centre and marked woodpecker clusters, and needs no permit.
  • Base yourself in: Starkville
  • Look for: Red-cockaded Woodpecker, Brown-headed Nuthatch, Bachman's Sparrow, Wood Duck, Canada Goose, Bald Eagle, Prothonotary Warbler, Anhinga, Barred Owl, Wild Turkey, Northern Bobwhite, Painted Bunting
  • Parks and reserves: Tombigbee National Forest (Forest or grassland)
  • Also covers: Noxubee National Wildlife Refuge, Tombigbee National Forest
  • Also watch for: The refuge holds beaver, alligator at the northern edge of its range, and a well-watched deer herd.

The Tishomingo hills

Sandstone bluffs and rhododendron in the only corner of the state with real hills

The far north-east corner sits on the edge of the Appalachian foothills, and the sandstone outcrops and rhododendron at Tishomingo look like nothing else in the state. The breeding list there includes birds that reach their southern or western limit at this point.

  • Habitat: Sandstone bluff and boulder field with rhododendron, upland oak and hickory, hemlock in the shaded coves, and a large reservoir on the Tennessee River.
  • When: April to June for the breeding birds, and October and November for the passage along the river.
  • Getting there: Memphis or Huntsville in Alabama are the airports, both about two hours away, and Iuka the base. Tishomingo is a state park with a small fee, marked trails and a swinging bridge over the creek.
  • Base yourself in: Iuka
  • Look for: Worm-eating Warbler, Kentucky Warbler, Hooded Warbler, Louisiana Waterthrush, Acadian Flycatcher, Wood Thrush, Yellow-throated Warbler, Summer Tanager, Scarlet Tanager, Barred Owl, Bald Eagle, Wild Turkey
  • Parks and reserves: Tishomingo State Park (State or provincial park), Natchez Trace Parkway (Protected area), J. P. Coleman State Park (State or provincial park)
  • Also covers: Bear Creek, J. P. Coleman State Park, Natchez Trace Parkway, Pickwick Lake, Tishomingo State Park
  • Also watch for: The bluffs hold rare ferns and the creek carries darters found in only a few river systems.

Frequently Asked Questions

When is the best time to go birding in Mississippi?

Late April, if one trip has to do everything: the woodpeckers, the bottomland warblers and the tern colonies are all active. February is the answer if the Mississippi Sandhill Crane is the point, because the savanna is burned open then and the refuge runs guided walks.

How do I see the Mississippi Sandhill Crane?

On a refuge guided walk in winter. The birds live in wet pine savanna that is closed to general access, there are only about a hundred of them, and they exist in this one county and nowhere else on earth. Book ahead, because the walks are limited.

Where is the most reliable Red-cockaded Woodpecker?

Noxubee National Wildlife Refuge. Its clusters have been managed and monitored for decades, the cavity trees are marked, there is a paved road through the refuge and no permit is needed. Be there at first light.

What is special about the Pascagoula?

It runs about eighty miles to the sea with no dam on it, which makes it the largest undammed river in the lower states. The swamp forest along it still floods the way it always has, and that is why the bird list there is what it is.

Is any of the state hilly?

The far north-east corner. Tishomingo State Park sits on the edge of the Appalachian foothills with sandstone bluffs, boulder fields and rhododendron, and it holds a breeding list that reaches its southern or western limit at that point.

Can I bird here with limited mobility?

Sites on this page differ from each other more than any rating could carry, so this is the framework the standard British guides use, and the reserve's own page and warden hold the answers.

  • Permission · some reserves open ground to disabled visitors that is closed to everyone else, and it is granted by asking.
  • Priority · parking bays, hides and the nearest paths are often held for disabled visitors. Ask what is reserved and how to claim it.
  • Parking · how close a car reaches the birds shapes the whole day. Ring ahead for the nearest hard standing to the water or the hide.
  • Paths · surfaces, gradients and gate widths vary inside a single reserve. Ask which trail is boarded, surfaced or level.
  • Perches · a hide with a low window, or a bench where the view opens, turns a viewpoint into a morning.

Every reserve above with its own link carries its access detail there, and a warden answers what a page cannot.
