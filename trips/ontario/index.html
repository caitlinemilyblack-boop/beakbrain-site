Birding in Ontario

Ontario reaches further south than northern California and further north than most of Alaska, so one province holds Carolinian forest at one end and the second largest wetland on earth at the other.

Birds that decide the trip

Prothonotary Warbler at Rondeau

The national population is tiny and almost all of it is in the Carolinian swamp forest of the far south, which makes Rondeau and Point Pelee the only realistic places to look.

When May to July, over standing water in the sloughs.

Where Point Pelee and the Lake Erie shore

Little Gull on the Niagara

The smallest gull in the world turns up among the Bonaparte's flocks below the falls most winters, and the corridor is the most reliable place on the continent to look for one.

When Late November to early January, in the Bonaparte's flocks.

Where Long Point and the Lake Ontario shore

Canada Jay along Highway 60

The Algonquin birds have been studied continuously since 1964 and they come to a stopped car rather than the other way round, which makes this the easiest place anywhere to meet one.

When All year, at pull-ins along the park corridor.

Where Algonquin, Carden and the east

Loggerhead Shrike on the Carden Alvar

The eastern population is down to a few dozen pairs and the alvar holds a large share of them, on limestone pavement too thin to plough and grazed rather than cropped.

When May to July, on the alvar roadsides.

Where Algonquin, Carden and the east

Hudsonian Godwit on the James Bay coast

It stages on the tidal flats in autumn before flying to Tierra del Fuego, and the James Bay coast is one of the most important places on earth for that.

When Late July to September, on the coastal flats.

Where The Hudson Bay lowland

Yellow Rail in the Rainy River sedge

It calls like two stones tapped together, only after dark, from wet sedge meadow. The Rainy River valley is the accessible place for it in the province.

When June, after dark in wet sedge.

Where Thunder Bay and the northern shield, The Hudson Bay lowland

Snowy Owl on the eastern islands

Amherst Island and Wolfe Island hold wintering owls on open farmland most years, and in an irruption winter the numbers there are among the highest anywhere south of the tundra.

When December to March, on open farmland.

Where Algonquin, Carden and the east

Connecticut Warbler in the shield bog

It walks rather than hops, sings from high in a tamarack and then vanishes, and the muskeg of the northern shield is where it breeds.

When June in the north, and mid-May on the Pelee passage.

Where Thunder Bay and the northern shield, Point Pelee and the Lake Erie shore

Point Pelee and the Lake Erie shore

The southernmost land in the country, tapering to a point that migrants pile onto in May

Point Pelee runs south into Lake Erie further than any other land in the country, and northbound migrants that have crossed the lake come down on the tip. The park is small, the trails are short and the birds are at eye level, which is why it is one of the best-known migration sites in North America.

  • Habitat: Carolinian forest of hackberry, sassafras and tulip tree on a sand spit, marsh and cattail behind, savanna and dune, and open lake either side.
  • When: The first three weeks of May, with the peak usually in the second week. September and October for the raptor count at Holiday Beach.
  • Getting there: Detroit in the United States or Toronto are the airports and Leamington the base. The park charges an entry fee and runs a shuttle to the tip in May, and Pelee Island is a ferry from Kingsville.
  • Base yourself in: Leamington
  • Look for: Prothonotary Warbler, Hooded Warbler, Cerulean Warbler, Acadian Flycatcher, Louisiana Waterthrush, Connecticut Warbler, Golden-winged Warbler, King Rail, Least Bittern, Black Tern, Eastern Whip-poor-will, Red-headed Woodpecker
  • Also covers: Hillman Marsh, Holiday Beach, Pelee Island, Point Pelee National Park, Rondeau Provincial Park
  • Also watch for: The Carolinian forest here holds tree species that reach nowhere else in the country, and the point takes an enormous monarch butterfly passage in autumn.

Long Point and the Lake Ontario shore

A forty-kilometre sand spit, and a river that gathers a hundred thousand gulls in a day

Long Point reaches forty kilometres into Lake Erie and carries one of the oldest bird observatories in North America. The Niagara River east of it draws as many as a hundred thousand gulls in a winter day, with a dozen species among them, which is the densest gull watching on the continent.

  • Habitat: Sand spit with dune, marsh and cottonwood, limestone gorge and rapids, sheltered harbour and lakefront, and open lake water.
  • When: Late November to early January for the Niagara gulls, and May and September for the Long Point passage.
  • Getting there: Toronto or Buffalo in the United States are the airports and Toronto the base. Long Point has a provincial park and a research station with limited access, and the Niagara viewpoints are free along both banks.
  • Base yourself in: Toronto
  • Look for: Bonaparte's Gull, Little Gull, Iceland Gull, Glaucous Gull, Black-headed Gull, Long-tailed Duck, Harlequin Duck, Purple Sandpiper, Red-necked Grebe, Piping Plover, Caspian Tern, Tundra Swan
  • Also covers: Hamilton Harbour, Long Point, Niagara River, Presqu'ile Provincial Park, Toronto
  • Also watch for: The Long Point marsh holds one of the larger snapping turtle populations in the province, and the Niagara gorge carries wintering peregrines.

Algonquin, Carden and the east

A road across a boreal park, and a limestone plain where the soil is too thin to farm

The Highway 60 corridor through Algonquin gives road access to spruce bog, beaver pond and northern hardwood, and its Canada Jays are famously approachable. The Carden Alvar south of it is limestone pavement with almost no soil, which is why it still holds grassland birds that ploughed country has lost.

  • Habitat: Spruce and tamarack bog, beaver pond and northern hardwood in the park, limestone pavement alvar with juniper and grassland, and open farmland on the islands.
  • When: June for the park and alvar breeders, and December to March for the owls on the eastern islands.
  • Getting there: Ottawa or Toronto are the airports. Algonquin charges a day-use fee and the Highway 60 corridor is where the access is, the alvar has marked roadside stops, and the islands are reached by short ferries.
  • Base yourself in: Ottawa
  • Look for: Canada Jay, Spruce Grouse, Black-backed Woodpecker, American Three-toed Woodpecker, Boreal Chickadee, Great Grey Owl, Loggerhead Shrike, Upland Sandpiper, Eastern Whip-poor-will, Snowy Owl, Rough-legged Buzzard, Northern Hawk-Owl
  • Also covers: Algonquin Provincial Park, Amherst Island, Carden Alvar, Ottawa, Wolfe Island
  • Also watch for: Moose are common along the Algonquin corridor, and the alvar holds plants and insects found on very few other rock types.

Thunder Bay and the northern shield

Boreal forest on Precambrian rock, with a river valley on the border holding prairie birds

The shield country north of the lakes is spruce, jack pine and muskeg, and the Sibley Peninsula runs out into Lake Superior with cliffs on it. The Rainy River valley on the western border is an outlier of open farmland and marsh that holds birds the forest around it does not.

  • Habitat: Spruce and jack pine on Precambrian rock, muskeg and sedge fen, basalt cliff on the peninsula, and open farmland and marsh in the Rainy River valley.
  • When: June for the breeding birds, and October and November for the passage down the lake shore.
  • Getting there: Thunder Bay has an airport and is the base, with Rainy River four hours west. The provincial parks charge day fees and the shield roads are long, gravel in places and empty of services.
  • Base yourself in: Thunder Bay
  • Look for: Yellow Rail, LeConte's Sparrow, Nelson's Sparrow, Sandhill Crane, Black Tern, Sharp-tailed Grouse, Connecticut Warbler, Spruce Grouse, Canada Jay, Boreal Chickadee, Great Grey Owl, Boreal Owl
  • Also covers: Rainy River, Sault Ste. Marie, Sibley Peninsula, Thunder Bay
  • Also watch for: Wolves and moose are established through the shield, and woodland caribou hold the Lake Superior islands.

The Hudson Bay lowland

The second largest wetland on earth, reached by train and then by boat

The Hudson Bay lowland is peat over permafrost across an area the size of a country, and the second largest wetland complex anywhere. Shorebirds stage on the James Bay coast in enormous numbers in autumn, and there are no roads: the train to Moosonee and a boat from there is the only way in.

  • Habitat: Peat bog and fen over permafrost, coastal salt marsh and tidal flat on James Bay, stunted spruce and tamarack, and beach ridges running inland.
  • When: Late July to September for the shorebird staging, which is the reason to make the journey at all.
  • Getting there: Timmins or Cochrane are the road ends and the Polar Bear Express train runs to Moosonee. Everything beyond is by boat or charter flight, there are no roads, and a trip needs booking and local arrangement well ahead.
  • Base yourself in: Moosonee
  • Look for: Hudsonian Godwit, Marbled Godwit, Red Knot, American Golden Plover, Stilt Sandpiper, Semipalmated Sandpiper, Willow Ptarmigan, Yellow Rail, Smith's Longspur, Snow Goose, Ross's Goose, Gyrfalcon
  • Also covers: James Bay coast, Moosonee, Polar Bear Provincial Park
  • Also watch for: Polar bears come ashore along the coast in summer, and the lowland holds one of the larger woodland caribou populations left in the province.

Frequently Asked Questions

When is the best time to go birding in Ontario?

The second week of May at Point Pelee, which is the province at its most famous. June for the boreal shield and the Carden alvar, late summer for the James Bay shorebirds, and early December for the Niagara gulls. They share nothing.

Is Point Pelee too crowded in May?

It is genuinely busy, and the shuttle to the tip runs precisely because of it. Rondeau an hour east and Pelee Island by ferry get the same birds with far fewer people, and the crowds are also how a rarity gets found within minutes.

How do I reach the Hudson Bay lowland?

The Polar Bear Express train from Cochrane to Moosonee, and everything beyond that by boat or charter flight. There are no roads. It is the second largest wetland complex on earth and the shorebird staging is the reason to go, but the logistics need arranging months ahead.

What is an alvar?

Limestone pavement with almost no soil over it, so the ground stays open grassland without ever being farmed. Carden holds Loggerhead Shrike, Upland Sandpiper and Eastern Whip-poor-will on ground that was never worth ploughing.

Why does the Niagara River hold so many gulls?

The rapids and the water below the falls and the generating stations keep stunned fish at the surface all winter, so up to a hundred thousand gulls of a dozen species work a short stretch of river. Both banks are worth using, which means a passport.

Can I bird here with limited mobility?

Sites on this page differ from each other more than any rating could carry, so this is the framework the standard British guides use, and the reserve's own page and warden hold the answers.

  • Permission · some reserves open ground to disabled visitors that is closed to everyone else, and it is granted by asking.
  • Priority · parking bays, hides and the nearest paths are often held for disabled visitors. Ask what is reserved and how to claim it.
  • Parking · how close a car reaches the birds shapes the whole day. Ring ahead for the nearest hard standing to the water or the hide.
  • Paths · surfaces, gradients and gate widths vary inside a single reserve. Ask which trail is boarded, surfaced or level.
  • Perches · a hide with a low window, or a bench where the view opens, turns a viewpoint into a morning.
