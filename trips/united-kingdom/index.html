When to Go

The British birding year has three peaks rather than one. Seabirds fill the cliffs from March to July, migration runs along the coasts through August to October, and the estuaries hold their biggest flocks from November to February.

Spring

March to June

Seabirds come back to the ledges in March, summer migrants arrive through April, and by May every woodland is singing from before five in the morning.

Go for Breeding seabirds, full song, and the longest days of the year in the north.

Trade off Rooms cost the most from Easter onward, and island boats need booking weeks ahead in June.

Typical daily average March 3 to 9 °C / 37 to 48 °F, April 4 to 12 °C / 39 to 54 °F, May 7 to 15 °C / 45 to 59 °F, June 10 to 18 °C / 50 to 64 °F

Autumn

July to October

Wader passage builds from July, the coasts fill with migrants through September, and October is when a rarity can turn up almost anywhere.

Go for Big wader counts, seawatching in an onshore blow, and the best chance of a bird from a long way off course.

Trade off Song has stopped, so birds are identified on plumage and call, and the seabird colonies are empty by August.

Typical daily average July 12 to 19 °C / 54 to 66 °F, August 12 to 19 °C / 54 to 66 °F, September 10 to 17 °C / 50 to 63 °F, October 8 to 13 °C / 46 to 55 °F

Winter

November to February

Geese, swans and waders pack the estuaries, starlings gather over the reedbeds at dusk, and raptors are easy over open ground.

Go for Six figure goose and wader flocks, murmurations, and reserves you have largely to yourself.

Trade off Daylight is short, especially in Scotland, and island ferries and boat trips run to a thin timetable.

Typical daily average November 5 to 10 °C / 41 to 50 °F, December 3 to 8 °C / 37 to 46 °F, January 2 to 7 °C / 36 to 45 °F, February 2 to 7 °C / 36 to 45 °F

Frequently Asked Questions

When is the best time to go birding in the United Kingdom?

May and June for seabird colonies and full song, September and October for migration and rarities, and November to February for geese, waders and starling murmurations. May is the single strongest month if you only make one trip.

Do I need a car?

For most of this page, yes. The exceptions are real though: Norfolk works from Norwich or King's Lynn with the coasthopper bus, the Firth of Forth reserves are reachable from Edinburgh by train, Conwy RSPB has its own station, and Aviemore puts Speyside within a bike ride. Everywhere else, and especially the Solway, mid Wales and the Hebridean machair, a car is the difference between one site a day and four.

How much does it cost to visit the reserves?

Many are free. RSPB and Wildlife Trust reserves with a visitor centre usually charge non-members for entry or parking, typically between four and eight pounds, and membership of either pays for itself in a week of touring. Island boat trips and landings are the real expense: a Farne Islands landing, a Skomer crossing or a Bass Rock trip each cost considerably more than a reserve entry.

How many places to stay are on this page?

46, every one of them carrying a Green Tourism award confirmed in the scheme's own register on the date shown on its chip. That is a much smaller number than the accommodation available near these reserves, and it is deliberate: a certification a reader cannot check is a claim rather than evidence.

Do I need to book the island boats in advance?

Yes, and earlier than feels necessary. Skomer day tickets, Farne Islands landings and Isle of May crossings all sell out in June and July, and every one of them is weather dependent, so build a spare day into any trip that depends on landing somewhere.

What should I know about the tides?

On the estuaries the tide decides everything. Waders feed spread across the mud at low water and are pushed close to the hides on a rising tide, so the two hours before high water are worth planning a day around. At Snettisham and Morecambe Bay a big spring tide is the whole event. The Holy Island causeway floods twice a day and the crossing times are published.

Is British birding good for a beginner?

It is one of the easiest places to start. The reserves have hides, paths and staff, the list is small enough to learn, and a seabird colony gives close views of a dozen species in an afternoon without any skill in finding them.

What about weather and daylight?

Rain is likely in every month and rarely lasts all day, so waterproofs matter more than a forecast. Daylight is the bigger planning factor: midsummer in Shetland gives light past eleven at night, and midwinter in the same place gives around six hours, which changes how many sites fit into a day.

Where do the rarities turn up?

On the edges. Shetland, Fair Isle, North Ronaldsay, Spurn, Portland and the Isles of Scilly all sit where a bird blown off course makes its first landfall, and each has an observatory or a warden logging what arrives. October is the month, and an easterly airflow is the condition.
