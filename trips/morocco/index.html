When to Go

The Moroccan year turns on heat and on two migrations. Spring runs from February to May and is the best of it everywhere at once. Summer shuts the desert and drives the birding uphill, autumn brings the return passage, and winter fills the Atlantic lagoons and the Saharan bays.

spring

February to May

The best of the country, and the season nearly every itinerary is built for. The desert works through March and April, the mountains open as the snow clears, and passage runs the whole length of the Atlantic coast.

Go for Sandgrouse at water, desert larks displaying, the ibis on the nest and the cedar forest in full song.

Trade off Easter fills the hotels in Ouarzazate and the Draa valley, and the high passes can still be shut by snow in March.

Typical daily average
  • Agadir: 11 to 22 °C / 52 to 72 °F
  • Marrakech: 11 to 23 °C / 52 to 73 °F
  • Ouarzazate: 9 to 24 °C / 48 to 75 °F

summer

June to August

The desert closes down and the birding moves uphill. Tata has recorded 52 degrees, and a day on the Tagdilt steppe in July produces very little.

Go for Oukaimeden and the High Atlas, where Crimson-winged Finch and Alpine Accentor are on the plateau and the air is breathable.

Trade off Dawn or nothing anywhere below 1,500 metres, and the seasonal desert lakes are dry.

Typical daily average
  • Agadir: 18 to 26 °C / 64 to 79 °F
  • Marrakech: 20 to 32 °C / 68 to 90 °F
  • Ouarzazate: 21 to 36 °C / 70 to 97 °F

migration

September to November

The return passage runs down the Atlantic. Cory's Shearwater and Northern Gannet stream past the capes, and wader counts on the central lagoons climb from 3,000 birds in summer to 16,000.

Go for Seabird passage at Cap Rhir and Cap Beddouza, wader flocks on every lagoon, and raptors crossing at the top of the country.

Trade off The desert is still hot in September, and the falcons leave Essaouira in October.

Typical daily average
  • Agadir: 15 to 26 °C / 59 to 79 °F
  • Marrakech: 15 to 26 °C / 59 to 79 °F
  • Ouarzazate: 12 to 26 °C / 54 to 79 °F

winter

December to January

The Atlantic wetlands hold their biggest numbers and the Saharan coast turns mild and sunny. Common Cranes spend the winter days in the reeds at Oued Massa.

Go for Thirty thousand ducks and fifty thousand waders on the Merja Zerga, and more than a fifth of Morocco's wintering waders in Dakhla Bay.

Trade off Rain and mud in the north, snow across the Middle Atlas, and freezing nights in the palm groves of the south.

Typical daily average
  • Agadir: 9 to 20 °C / 48 to 68 °F
  • Marrakech: 7 to 18 °C / 45 to 64 °F
  • Ouarzazate: 3 to 16 °C / 37 to 61 °F

Two estuaries, an argan forest and the coastal cliffs that hold the last wild Northern Bald Ibis

Souss-Massa National Park runs for 65 kilometres along the coast south of Agadir and covers 33,800 hectares. More than 270 species have been recorded inside it, and the cliffs between here and Tamri hold the only wild breeding population of Northern Bald Ibis left in the world.

  • Habitat: Tidal mudflat, sand bar and halophytic saltmarsh at the two river mouths, backed by argan (a thorny tree that grows wild only in southwest Morocco) forest on the Souss plain and by low coastal cliffs and steppe to the north.
  • When: September to April is the productive stretch, and February to April is the peak of the northward passage. Up to 200 Common Cranes spend the winter days in the reeds at Oued Massa between November and early March. Summer is the thinnest season.
  • Getting there: Agadir has an international airport. The Oued Souss mouth sits on the southern edge of the city, Oued Massa is 40 km further south off the Tiznit road, and Tamri is 50 to 60 km north on the coast road.
  • Base yourself in: Agadir or Inezgane for the estuaries, Taroudant for the argan forest.
  • Look for: Northern Bald Ibis, Black-winged Kite, Tawny Eagle, Marbled Duck, Black-crowned Tchagra, Moussier's Redstart, Cream-colored Courser, Audouin's Gull, Common Crane
  • National park: Souss-Massa National Park
  • Stops:
    • : Wide mud and sand banks on the southern edge of Agadir, with paths and platforms along the north shore. The site is better at high tide and Northern Bald Ibis feeds here.
    • : A sand bar closes the river mouth and holds back a shallow lake, with dunes on one side and saltmarsh on the other. Black-bellied Sandgrouse comes in to drink about two hours after sunrise.
    • : The estuary and steppe here are the best place in Morocco to see Northern Bald Ibis, and up to 65 birds have been counted in a day. The birds rest near the river mouth around the middle of the day.
    • : A lighthouse cape 50 km north of Agadir and the best sea vantage point in the country. Cory's Shearwater passes in thousands and Northern Gannet peaks in October.
    • : A 50 km road up a gorge behind Agadir, through argan and thuya woodland to palm oases and Aleppo pine. Tristram's Warbler lives in the high scrub and Black Wheatear on the cliffs.
    • : The walled town at the centre of the Souss plain, and the base for the argan forest. Black-winged Kite hunts the orchards and hedges around it.
    • : Gorges on the upper Oued Souss, 100 km east of Taroudant. Booted Eagle, Black Wheatear and Blue Rock Thrush all breed on the rock walls.
  • Also covers: Agadir, Aoulouz, Cap Rhir, Inezgane, Mouth of the Oued Souss, Oued Massa, Paradise Valley, Souss-Massa National Park, Tamri, Taroudant
  • Also watch for: Egyptian Mongoose, African Wildcat, Barbary Ground Squirrel and Algerian Hedgehog all live in the park. Killer Whale and Fin Whale have both been recorded off Cap Rhir, and Common Dolphin is regular there.

The Anti-Atlas and the Jbel Bani

Palm oases, rock carvings and stone desert strung along the Anti-Atlas, where sandgrouse come in to drink at first light

South of the Anti-Atlas the ground turns to reg (flat stone desert) broken by two long ridges and a handful of oases. The region only opened to visitors in the late 1990s, so its birds are still poorly known, and every palm grove on the road between Taghjicht and Tazenakht is worth a stop.

  • Habitat: Flat stone desert with bare cliffs and dry river beds, scattered with acacia and jujube, and punctuated by palm groves along the few permanent waters.
  • When: March and April. Temperatures climb fast from May, and the highest reading in Morocco has been taken at Tata. Birding works at either end of the day and stops in the middle of it.
  • Getting there: A car is essential and the circuit from Taghjicht east to Tazenakht takes three to four days. The main roads are surfaced and easy, and permission should be sought from local officials before driving any track south towards the border.
  • Base yourself in: Taghjicht, Tata or Taliouine.
  • Look for: Fulvous Babbler, Crowned Sandgrouse, Spotted Sandgrouse, Lichtenstein's Sandgrouse, Pharaoh Eagle-Owl, White-crowned Wheatear, Trumpeter Finch, African Desert Warbler, Bar-tailed Lark
  • Stops:
    • : Palm groves along the Oued Sayed under the Adrar n'Saras cliff. A pair of Bonelli's Eagles breeds on the mountain and Laughing Dove reaches its western limit here.
    • : A fortified granary on a rock pinnacle above a palm valley, with natural pools upstream. Crag Martin, Blue Rock Thrush and Black Wheatear all use the cliff.
    • : The largest town in the Jbel Bani, with palm groves on the river and on the road west to Akka. Crowned Sandgrouse is widespread and Lichtenstein's Sandgrouse comes to acacia country south of the town.
    • : Waterfalls in a cliff-lined river bed 72 km east of Tata. Sandgrouse, Trumpeter Finch and Blue-cheeked Bee-eater all come to the water, and White-crowned Wheatear breeds on the rock.
    • : Almond orchards and a river valley between the Souss plain and the Anti-Atlas. April mornings hold heavy passage, and the steppe east of the town has Thick-billed Lark and Trumpeter Finch.
    • : A crossroads town on rocky semi-desert. Mourning Wheatear is scarce here and the orchards on the Foum Zguid road pull in migrants at first light.
    • : Granite domes and almond terraces in the heart of the Anti-Atlas. The village sits at the western end of the circuit and the surrounding rock holds Black Wheatear and Trumpeter Finch.
  • Also covers: Amtoudi, Foum Zguid, Tafraoute, Taghjicht, Taliouine, Tata, Tazenakht, Tissint
  • Also watch for: Striped Hyena and Golden Jackal both live in the Jbel Bani, and Barbary Ground Squirrel is common in the gorges. Bibron's Agama and Spiny-tailed Agama are the lizards most often seen on the rocks.

Boumalne Dades and the Tagdilt track

Stony steppe at 1,600 metres, plus two gorges cut into the southern side of the High Atlas

The plain southeast of Boumalne Dades carries almost the full set of Moroccan desert birds within an hour of one town. Above it the Dades and the Todra cut back into the mountains through palm groves and fortified villages, and migrants funnel up both valleys to cross the range.

  • Habitat: Open stony steppe at 1,500 to 1,600 metres, with dry river channels crossing it and the Jbel Sarhro rising to the south. The two valleys hold irrigated palm groves, orchards and vertical limestone walls.
  • When: March to May and again in autumn. Cream-colored Courser returns between December and March, and summer is too hot to be useful. Mornings can be cold even in late spring.
  • Getting there: Boumalne Dades sits on the main road along the south side of the High Atlas, 116 km east of Ouarzazate and 190 km west of Er Rachidia. The Tagdilt track leaves the road at the eastern edge of town, and the Iknioun road 10 km further east gives a pleasanter start on the same ground.
  • Base yourself in: Boumalne Dades, or one of the small inns up the Dades gorge.
  • Look for: Temminck's Lark, Thick-billed Lark, Greater Hoopoe-Lark, Red-rumped Wheatear, Mourning Wheatear, Cream-colored Courser, Black-bellied Sandgrouse, Pin-tailed Sandgrouse, Trumpeter Finch
  • Stops:
    • : Open steppe crossed by rough tracks, driven slowly with frequent stops. Temminck's Lark, Thick-billed Lark, Red-rumped Wheatear and Cream-colored Courser all live here.
    • : Tracks run west off this road towards the Jbel Sarhro and cover the same ground as Tagdilt on cleaner desert. Thekla's Lark favours the rockier foothills.
    • : A road climbs the gorge from Boumalne through orchards and kasbahs. Long-legged Buzzard, Bonelli's Eagle and Lanner Falcon hunt the walls.
    • : A narrow limestone slot above one of the finest palm groves in the country. Crimson-winged Finch sometimes drops in to drink and Crag Martin nests on the walls.
    • : The town at the foot of the Todra, and the start of the road up the gorge. The palm grove below it holds Common Bulbul and Western Olivaceous Warbler.
  • Also covers: Boumalne Dades, Dades Gorge, Iknioun road, Msemrir, Tagdilt track, Tinghir, Todra Gorge
  • Also watch for: Lesser Egyptian Jerboa leaves burrows all over the steppe, and Red Fox and Golden Jackal hunt them. Changeable Agama favours the rocky ground and North African Green Frog lives in the Oued Dades.

Ouarzazate and the Draa valley

The only large permanent water south of the High Atlas, and a palm valley that runs 265 kilometres into the sand

The Mansour Eddahbi reservoir east of Ouarzazate is the single big body of water in the pre-desert, and it is the only place in Morocco where Grey Heron breeds. From the dam the Oued Draa runs southeast through a chain of oases and fortified villages before it vanishes under the hammada (bare rock desert) beyond Mhamid.

  • Habitat: A reservoir of very variable extent ringed by bare stony ground and thin bushes, then a narrow irrigated valley of palm groves and gardens set in stone desert and acacia country.
  • When: March and April for migrants, May for breeding birds. The valley is best in winter and early spring, and the heat from June to September makes it unworkable.
  • Getting there: Ouarzazate has an airport and sits on the crossroads of the road along the south side of the High Atlas and the road south to the Draa. Zagora is 165 km down the valley and Mhamid 265 km, all of it surfaced, and the return is on the same road.
  • Base yourself in: Ouarzazate for the reservoir, Zagora for the valley.
  • Look for: Blue-cheeked Bee-eater, Ruddy Shelduck, Fulvous Babbler, White-crowned Wheatear, Brown-necked Raven, Marbled Duck, Desert Sparrow, Streaked Scrub Warbler, Pharaoh Eagle-Owl
  • Stops:
    • Mansour Eddahbi reservoir: Tracks run down to the north shore off the main road east of town. Up to 500 Ruddy Shelducks have been counted in spring and Marbled Duck passes through in numbers.
    • Tamdakht: Gardens and a salt river below a kasbah 35 km from Ouarzazate. The gardens act as a magnet for passerine migrants and White Stork nests on the walls.
    • : The point where the road meets the Draa and the first acacias appear. Blue-cheeked Bee-eater breeds as far north as here.
    • Tansikht: A small dam on the Draa about 28 km below Agdz. The first Blue-cheeked Bee-eaters of the year usually show here from late March.
    • : The main town of the valley, with palm groves on both banks. Blue-cheeked Bee-eater perches on the wires in the middle of town and Fulvous Babbler lives in the dry river bed.
    • : The end of the tarmac, where the river disappears under the sand. Bird density is low and Desert Sparrow, Brown-necked Raven and Egyptian Nightjar are the specialities.
  • Also covers: Agdz, Ait Benhaddou, Mansour Eddahbi reservoir, Mhamid, Ouarzazate, Tamdakht, Tansikht, Zagora
  • Also watch for: Mediterranean Pond Turtle is common the whole length of the Oued Draa and drifts downstream with the releases from the dam. Bibron's Agama and Spiny-tailed Agama live on the stony ground either side of the valley.

Merzouga, the Erg Chebbi and the Ziz valley

Morocco's only great dune field, a desert lake that fills after rain, and the palm groves of the Ziz

The Erg Chebbi is the one large sand sea in Morocco and only six bird species breed on it, but one of them is Desert Sparrow and the dunes are the easiest place in the country to find it. When rain fills Dayet Srji at the foot of the dunes, thousands of waterbirds appear in open desert.

  • Habitat: Live sand dunes rising from flat stone desert, with tamarisk fringing their western edge, a shallow seasonal lake at the southwest end, and continuous palm groves along the Oued Ziz to the north.
  • When: March to May is the best stretch, and October to February also works with fewer summer visitors. June to September is too hot. Start at dawn, because heat haze closes the desert down by mid morning.
  • Getting there: Erfoud is reached from Er Rachidia to the north, from the road along the south side of the High Atlas to the west, and from the Draa valley. Merzouga is about 50 km on from Erfoud, and hiring a driver with a four wheel drive for the first trip into the dunes is the sensible way to start.
  • Base yourself in: Erfoud, or one of the inns strung along the western edge of the dunes.
  • Look for: Desert Sparrow, Egyptian Nightjar, African Desert Warbler, Blue-cheeked Bee-eater, Fulvous Babbler, Spotted Sandgrouse, Bar-tailed Lark, Greater Hoopoe-Lark, Tristram's Warbler
  • Stops:
    • : A north to south line of live dunes east of Merzouga. Desert Sparrow nests around the inns at the northern end and Bar-tailed Lark and Greater Hoopoe-Lark breed on the sand.
    • : The village at the foot of the dunes, beside the seasonal lake of Dayet Srji. In wet years Greater Flamingo, Marbled Duck and hundreds of Ruddy Shelducks appear on open desert.
    • : The town at the head of the desert road, with dry wadis just outside it. African Desert Warbler is regular in the scrub 5 to 6 km along the Merzouga road.
    • : Palm groves and dry wadis at the southern end of the Tafilalt. Fulvous Babbler lives in the jujube and acacia along the channels.
    • : A spring in a palm grove 21 km south of Er Rachidia, which floods the Ziz again below the dam. Blue-cheeked Bee-eater reaches its northern limit on the river here.
    • : A reservoir in desert mountains north of Er Rachidia. Two rough tracks off the main road reach the water. Ruddy Shelduck breeds and more than 200 were present in April 2001.
  • Also covers: Er Rachidia, Erfoud, Erg Chebbi, Hassan Addakhil dam, Merzouga, Rissani, Source Bleue de Meski, Taouz
  • Also watch for: Bibron's Agama, Changeable Agama and Spiny-tailed Agama are all conspicuous on the stony ground. Fennec tracks cross the dunes and Lesser Egyptian Jerboa is common in the flat desert west of them.

The Saharan Atlantic coast and Dakhla

Saharan river mouths, a Ramsar lagoon and the largest bay in the country, on the Atlantic edge of the desert

The road south from Goulimine crosses a series of Saharan river mouths before it reaches Khnifiss lagoon, 6,000 hectares of tidal water set in bare desert. Nine hundred kilometres further on, Dakhla Bay holds more than a fifth of all the waders that winter in Morocco, and 36,000 were counted there in a single January.

  • Habitat: Tidal estuaries with sandy beaches, mudflats and saltmarsh, separated by long runs of sea cliff and by flat stone desert with sparse halophytic scrub.
  • When: Spring and autumn for migrants, and winter for waders, gulls and terns. The stretch around Christmas and the New Year is sunny and mild. Summer is hot inland and thin for birds.
  • Getting there: A car is essential and the round trip from Goulimine to Laayoune covers about 860 kilometres, which takes at least five days. Dakhla has an airport, and lies 514 km on from Laayoune by road. Stay on tarmac in the interior, because mines were laid here.
  • Base yourself in: Tan-Tan Plage for the northern estuaries, Laayoune or Dakhla for the far south.
  • Look for: West African Crested Tern, Lesser Crested Tern, Audouin's Gull, Slender-billed Gull, Bar-tailed Godwit, Red Knot, Greater Flamingo, Streaked Scrub Warbler, Cream-colored Courser
  • National park: Khnifiss National Park
  • Stops:
    • : A small seaside town and the best base for the northern estuaries. Northern Gannet, Sandwich Tern and thousands of Lesser Black-backed Gulls pass the shore in winter.
    • : A tidal estuary with a lagoon on each side of the road, and the cliffs above give the view over both. This is the pick of the three Saharan river mouths.
    • : A 6,000 hectare Ramsar lagoon behind a sand spit, with a warden's house on the cliff giving the best view. Wader counts run to 13,440 in December.
    • : A cape town fighting back the sand, and a strategic point for seabird passage down the coast. Royal and Sandwich Terns rest on the beaches nearby.
    • : The city sits just beyond the wide dry bed of the Saquia al Hamra. A 50 km loop through the oasis at Lemseyed produces desert larks, wheatears and Fulvous Babbler.
    • : A shallow bay 14 km wide behind a 45 km sand peninsula. The intertidal flats in the north hold the biggest wader numbers, and up to 2,500 Audouin's Gulls have been counted in January.
  • Also covers: Boujdour, Dakhla Bay, Goulimine, Khnifiss lagoon, Khnifiss National Park, Laayoune, Mouth of the Chebeika, Tan-Tan Plage, Tarfaya
  • Also watch for: Striped Hyena, African Wildcat and Saharan Striped Weasel live along the Lower Draa, and two shrews found nowhere else are named from Tarfaya. Loggerhead Turtle and Leathery Turtle both come ashore on this coast.

Frequently Asked Questions

When is the best time to go birding in Morocco?

March and April, if you want one answer. The desert is workable, sandgrouse are coming to water, the larks are displaying and passage is running up the Atlantic coast at the same time. Winter is the other answer, for waders at Dakhla Bay and Khnifiss and for the cranes at Oued Massa.

Where should I go first?

Marrakech, the High Atlas and the desert, which is the circuit almost every operator sells. Two weeks gets you Oukaimeden, the Tagdilt track, Merzouga and the Middle Atlas cedar forest, and that covers the mountain and desert specialities in one loop.

Do I need a hire car?

Yes for almost everything named here. Trains link Tangier, Rabat, Casablanca, Marrakech and Fez, and buses reach every town, and then the birding site is 10 to 40 km further on down a road nothing runs along. A standard car handles all the tarmac on these routes.

Where can I still see the Northern Bald Ibis?

On the Atlantic coast between Agadir and Tamri. The Moroccan population is the only wild breeding one left in the world, and surveys in 1998 and 1999 found 60 breeding pairs and 200 to 250 birds. Watch them from the estuary at Tamri or from the Oued Massa, and stay away from the nesting cliffs.

Do I need a four wheel drive?

For the main circuit, no. The passes, the Tagdilt track and the roads through the Jbel Bani are all fine in a standard car driven slowly. A four wheel drive earns its keep for the dune tracks around Merzouga, the mouth of the Oued Draa and the crossing between the Dades and the Todra, and a local driver is worth more than the vehicle.

Is the Tagdilt track still good?

Yes for larks, wheatears, coursers and sandgrouse, which is most of what people come for. The Houbara Bustard that made its reputation has gone, hunted out by falconry parties, and the last known record from the area was in April 1999. The tracks running west off the Iknioun road cover the same habitat on cleaner ground.

How hot does it get in the south?

The highest temperatures recorded in Morocco are from Tata, at up to 52 degrees. From May onwards the desert is a dawn and dusk proposition, with a long break in the middle of the day, and from June to September there is little point going at all.

Can I bird the Saharan coast?

Yes, and it takes time. Goulimine to Laayoune and back is about 860 km and wants at least five days, and Dakhla is a further 514 km beyond that with flights from Casablanca. Stay on the tarmac in the interior, because mines were laid during the conflict and have never all been cleared.

Can I bird here with limited mobility?

Often yes, and it varies by site. A good deal of the birding here happens from roadsides, hides and lodge grounds rather than on long walks, and some reserves keep flat, surfaced trails. Ask a lodge or reserve directly about step free access, surfaces and seating before you book. The Birdability Map records those details for birding sites, and their Access Considerations guide lists the questions worth asking. Coverage is thin outside the United States, so read a blank area as unrecorded rather than unsuitable.
